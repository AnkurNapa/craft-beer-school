Pricing wine: cost, positioning and the shelf

A good wine price has to cover your cost, fit the shelf it sits on and tell the right story. Here is a step-by-step way to get there.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa and Chatty Girija

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer
Written by and point of contactChatty GirijaQuestions about this guide? WhatsApp her

Three views of the same price

Every wine price is seen three ways. Your cost view asks whether the bottle makes money. The shelf view asks where it sits among rivals and what the buyer pays. The brand view asks what the price says about quality. A price that works for only one of these will fail.

Start with cost

Know your full cost per bottle, including fruit, winemaking, glass, closure, label, carton, labour, overhead, losses and the cost of money while the wine ages. See working out cost per bottle. This is your floor. Selling below it loses money on each sale, which only makes sense as a deliberate, short-term decision.

Be honest about the costs founders forget. Wine lost to lees, filtering and bottling does not appear on the invoice but it never reaches the shelf. Samples poured at tastings, bottles given to buyers and breakage in transit are all real costs. Add a modest allowance for each and review it after the first vintage.

Work back from the shelf

Next decide the price the customer will pay in the shop or on the menu. Then subtract each margin and tax along the route to see what you receive. In an illustration with made-up numbers, a shelf price of 100 units might leave 55 for the producer after the shop, the distributor and tax. If your cost is 50, the margin is thin. The check is simple and often painful.

The chain is different when you sell direct. A tasting room sale at 100 units keeps far more. See tasting rooms as a business. Many wineries keep the same bottle price across channels so as not to undercut their own shop partners.

Position against what is already there

Visit shops and restaurants and note the wines near your intended price: style, origin, label and bottle. Ask yourself why a buyer would pick yours. If you cannot say, the price needs a better reason.

Higher prices suggest scarcity, craft or a place. They need proof in the glass and on the label. Lower prices suit volume and need a cost base to match. Without a reason, avoid the middle, where you are neither special nor cheap.

A practical exercise helps. Pick five wines at the price you have in mind. Write down the bottle shape, closure, label style and what the shop says about each one. Then put your bottle next to them in your mind. Does it look like it belongs at that price? Packaging is part of the price signal. A cheap closure on an expensive wine undermines the number on the shelf, while a heavy bottle on a simple wine can look like a bluff.

Test it with real buyers

Show the wine and the price to shop buyers, restaurant managers and likely customers. Watch their faces when you say the number. Ask whether they would stock it at that price and what would make them pay more. Our guide on market research for a new wine brand shows how to gather this without bias.

Do not test with friends alone. They like you.

Ask the buyer to rank three price points, not just say yes or no. Record the answers and look for a pattern across ten or twenty people. If most pick the middle one, the number is probably close. If buyers split widely, the problem is more likely your positioning than the price.

Remember that a shop buyer thinks about margin and turnover, while a drinker thinks about the occasion. A wine for a gift may carry a higher price than the same wine for a weeknight.

Discounts and the long run

Discounting trains the customer to wait. If you must clear stock, do it through a defined channel such as a club or a festive offer. Keep the regular price intact. Review prices once a year. Rises should be small and explained. A winery that never changes price has a cost problem within a few years.

Pricing across a range matters too. Keep a clear step between your entry wine and the top wine so customers can see why one costs more.

Think about the whole year. Festive seasons and tourist peaks can lift demand without a discount. A limited release at a higher price can lift the brand. Use these before reaching for a price cut.

Pricing checklist

  1. Cost per bottle, fully loaded.
  2. Receipt per bottle on each channel.
  3. Shelf price of the nearest rivals.
  4. Buyer reaction.
  5. The reason for the price, stated in one sentence.

If you cannot fill all five, you are guessing.

Common questions

How do I price my first wine?

Work out cost, work back from the shelf, compare with rivals and test with buyers.

Should I price direct sales lower than trade?

Usually the same, so you do not undercut your own trade partners.

What is a good margin?

It depends on your channel and costs. Work it out from your own numbers.

When should I raise prices?

When costs rise or demand outstrips supply, with a small, explained change.
