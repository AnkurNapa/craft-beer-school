The jobs inside a brewery

If you are thinking about a career in beer, it helps to know that brewing is only one job of several. Here is what each role actually involves.

3 min read · Updated December 2022 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. The head brewer
  2. The assistant or junior brewer
  3. Cellarman or cellar operator
  4. Packaging and quality control
  5. Taproom and hospitality
  6. Sales and distribution
  7. How these roles connect

The head brewer

The head brewer sets recipes, owns the production schedule, and carries final responsibility for quality. In a small Indian craft brewery, the head brewer is often also the one troubleshooting a stuck fermentation at midnight, because there is no shift system deep enough to hand it off to someone else.

It is also a management job as much as a technical one. The head brewer schedules the rest of the production team, decides what gets brewed and when tanks free up, and is usually the person who explains to ownership why a batch needs another week of conditioning rather than shipping on the original date.

The assistant or junior brewer

The assistant brewer runs brew days under direction: milling grain, managing the mash, monitoring the boil, cleaning and sanitising vessels. It is physical, repetitive work in the early months, and it is also where most working brewers actually learned the trade, rather than in a classroom.

Cellarman or cellar operator

The cellar is where fermentation and conditioning happen, and the cellarman tracks gravity readings, temperature and yeast health across every tank in the building. A missed gravity reading on a Friday can mean a beer is packaged too early or too late on Monday, so this role rewards someone who is precise and does not skip steps when nobody is watching.

It also means managing yeast: harvesting it from a finished fermenter, checking it is still healthy enough to repitch, and deciding when a strain needs to be retired and replaced. A cellar that reuses tired yeast without checking it is a common, quiet cause of slow or incomplete fermentations further down the schedule.

Packaging and quality control

Packaging line staff keep cans, bottles or kegs filled correctly and consistently, and quality control checks that what goes out the door matches what left the fermenter. In many small breweries these overlap with the cellar team, but as a brewery grows, packaging and QC often become their own dedicated roles because the line simply needs full attention during a run.

Quality control is not limited to the packaging line. It also covers tasting finished beer for faults, keeping reference standards for what each recipe should taste like, and flagging when a batch has drifted from that standard before it ever reaches a customer.

Taproom and hospitality

Taproom staff are the face of the brewery, pouring beer and explaining it to customers who may be trying a style for the first time. This role does not require a brewing background, but the best taproom staff understand the beer well enough to describe it properly, which is where a course like sensory training becomes useful even outside the brewhouse.

It is also, for many people, the first real look inside a brewery's culture. Someone who enjoys the pace and the customer interaction in the taproom often discovers from there whether the rest of the operation, the earlier mornings, the physical work, actually appeals to them too.

Sales and distribution

Someone has to get the beer into bars, restaurants and retail accounts, negotiate placement, and manage the relationship after the sale. This role sits closer to a general sales job than to brewing itself, and it is one of the more overlooked entry points into the industry for people without a brewing background.

The best brewery salespeople still know the product cold. Being able to explain why a beer is priced the way it is, or why a particular style pairs with an account's menu, wins trust faster than a generic pitch about the brewery's story.

How these roles connect

A batch of beer touches almost every one of these roles before it reaches a glass: brewed by the brewing team, tracked through the cellar, checked by quality control, packaged, and sold by sales or poured in the taproom. Understanding the whole chain, even if you only work in one part of it, makes you a more useful hire anywhere in the building.

This is also why small breweries value versatility so highly in early hires. Someone who can step from the cellar onto the packaging line for a day, or cover a taproom shift when needed, is worth more to a five-person team than someone narrowly skilled in a single function.

Common questions

Which brewery job is easiest to get without experience?

Taproom and hospitality roles are usually the most accessible without prior brewing experience, followed by entry-level packaging or cellar assistant roles.

Do all breweries have every one of these roles?

No. Small breweries often combine several of these into one or two people. Dedicated roles for each function usually appear as the brewery grows.

Is sales a good entry point into the beer industry?

Yes, particularly for people with a general sales background. It does not require brewing knowledge to start, though understanding the product well helps close accounts.

What is the fastest way to move from taproom to brewing?

Ask for time on the brewhouse floor, take a short brewing course to show intent, and be reliable and precise in whatever role you currently hold. Most brewers we know moved this way rather than through a formal transfer process.
