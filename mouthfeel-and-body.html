Mouthfeel and body

Mouthfeel is everything you feel rather than taste or smell: weight, carbonation, warmth, texture. Here is what causes each one.

3 min read · Updated January 2023 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. Mouthfeel is physical, not chemical
  2. Body: what causes a full or thin beer
  3. Carbonation: the fizz factor
  4. Warmth and astringency
  5. Words to use instead of thick or thin
  6. How mouthfeel interacts with bitterness

Mouthfeel is physical, not chemical

Aroma and flavour come from compounds your nose and tongue detect chemically. Mouthfeel is what your mouth feels physically: how heavy the liquid feels, how much it fizzes, whether it warms your throat, whether it leaves a dry or slick sensation behind after you swallow. It is a separate sense from taste, and conflating the two is the single most common beginner mistake in a tasting note.

A simple way to isolate mouthfeel from flavour while practising is to hold a sip in your mouth for a few seconds without swallowing, paying attention only to weight and texture, before letting flavour and aroma back into focus on the next sip.

Body: what causes a full or thin beer

Body comes mainly from unfermented sugars and proteins left in the beer, called dextrins, which the yeast could not convert to alcohol during fermentation. A beer mashed at a higher temperature, roughly 68 to 70 degrees Celsius, produces more of these unfermented sugars and feels fuller in the mouth than the same recipe mashed lower, around 63 to 65 degrees, even at an identical final ABV.

This is why body and alcohol strength are not the same thing, despite how often people assume they must be. A 4 percent milk stout can feel fuller than a 7 percent dry IPA, because the stout is built around unfermented lactose and the IPA is built to ferment out almost completely dry.

Protein content from the grain bill also contributes to body and to the persistence of the head sitting on top of the beer, which is why wheat beers, brewed with a high proportion of wheat malt, tend to have both a fuller body and a notably thick, long lasting head compared with an all barley recipe.

Carbonation: the fizz factor

Carbonation adds a prickling sensation that can read as sharp or aggressive at high levels and flat or dull at low ones. It also affects how other sensations come through: high carbonation tends to scrub bitterness and sweetness off the tongue faster than low carbonation does, which is one reason a well carbonated pilsner tastes crisp rather than cloying even with some residual sweetness present.

Nitrogen dispense, used for some stouts, produces an unusually smooth, almost creamy carbonation quite different from the sharper prickle of carbon dioxide, which is why the same stout can feel noticeably different on nitro tap compared with the bottled version.

Warmth and astringency

Alcohol produces a warming sensation in the throat and chest on strong beers, distinct from any flavour it carries on its own. Astringency, a drying, puckering grip similar to strong tea left to stew too long, is usually a fault caused by tannin extraction during mashing or sparging, and is covered along with other faults in our guide to beer off flavours.

Temperature interacts with all of this too. A beer served too cold can mask genuine body and make even a full bodied stout feel thinner than it actually is, which is one more reason serving temperature and mouthfeel assessment are closely linked rather than separate concerns.

Words to use instead of thick or thin

Try light, medium or full bodied for weight, and creamy, silky, watery or chewy for texture, since these carry far more specific meaning than a single vague adjective. Prickly, soft or flat describe carbonation more precisely than simply calling something fizzy. Building this small vocabulary makes tasting notes far more useful to compare across beers and across different tasters.

It helps to build these word pairs into contrasts: light versus full, still versus prickly, silky versus astringent. Thinking in opposites gives you a scale to place a beer on, rather than a single adjective floating with no reference point.

How mouthfeel interacts with bitterness

Body and carbonation both change how bitterness registers on the tongue, which is exactly the interaction covered in our guide on how we perceive bitterness. None of these senses works in isolation, and a full tasting note accounts for how they interact with each other rather than scoring each one alone as if it existed separately.

Common questions

Is a fuller body always a sign of a better beer?

No, it depends on the style. A crisp pilsner is meant to be light bodied, and a stout is meant to be full. The question is whether the body suits the style, not whether it is heavy.

Why does my low alcohol beer feel heavier than a stronger one?

Mash temperature and unfermented sugars drive body far more than alcohol content does, so a lower strength beer mashed warm can easily feel fuller than a drier, stronger one.

What causes a slick or oily mouthfeel?

That sensation is often a sign of diacetyl, a fault covered in our guide on the butter fault, though some high body beers naturally have a rounder feel without any fault present.

Does carbonation affect how bitter a beer tastes?

Yes, higher carbonation tends to carry bitterness across the tongue faster and scrub it off sooner, which can make an identical beer taste sharper or cleaner depending on how it is carbonated.
