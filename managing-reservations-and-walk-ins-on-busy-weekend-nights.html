Managing reservations and walk-ins on busy weekend nights

Weekend nights make or break a brewpub's month. Too many bookings and the regulars who walk in get turned away. Too few and the room sits half empty until 9 pm. Here is how we would balance the two.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer
Point of contactChatty GirijaQuestions about this guide? WhatsApp her

On this page

  1. Know your real seat capacity
  2. Hold back tables for walk-ins
  3. Book in time slots, not open-ended evenings
  4. Take deposits for big groups
  5. Run the waitlist like a system
  6. Brief the floor before doors open
  7. Use booking apps carefully
  8. Measure the nights, then adjust

Know your real seat capacity

Start with how many guests you can serve well, not how many chairs fit. A 120 cover brewpub with a kitchen that can send out 60 mains an hour cannot really seat 120 people at 8 pm. Count tables by size, note which ones can be pushed together and work out how many covers the kitchen and bar can handle per hour.

Then look at your turn time. Weekend dinner tables at a brewpub often sit for around 90 minutes to two hours. If a table turns twice in an evening, it can carry two bookings. If it does not, booking it twice just builds a queue of angry guests at the door.

Hold back tables for walk-ins

Brewpubs live on walk-ins more than fine dining restaurants do. People decide on the night. If every table is booked by Thursday, the group that walks past at 8.30 pm goes to the bar next door and might not come back.

A sensible starting point is to keep around a quarter of your tables off the booking system on Friday and Saturday. Bar seating and high tables near the taps suit walk-ins anyway. Track how fast those tables fill for a month. If they are full by 8 pm every week, hold back more. If they sit empty, release some to bookings.

Book in time slots, not open-ended evenings

An open booking for "dinner at 8" lets a table sit until close. Time slots stop that. Offer seatings such as 7 to 9 pm and 9 pm to close for larger tables. Say so clearly when the guest books. Most guests accept a slot if they know about it up front. Very few accept being asked to leave when they did not.

Hold a booked table for about 15 minutes past the slot time, then release it to the waitlist. Say this in the booking confirmation as well.

Take deposits for big groups

No-shows hurt most with large groups. A table of 12 that does not turn up on a Saturday is a sixth of a small room lost. Ask for a deposit or a pre-booked set menu for groups above a size you choose, say eight or more. Adjust the deposit against the final bill. Our guide to pricing private parties and corporate bookings covers group packages in more detail.

For smaller tables, a reminder message on the afternoon of the booking does much of the work. Ask the guest to reply to confirm. Those who do not reply get a call.

Run the waitlist like a system

When the room is full, the host stand decides whether a guest waits happily at the bar or leaves annoyed. Take a name, a phone number and the party size. Give an honest wait time, then add five minutes. Text the guest when the table is ready so they can wait at the bar with a beer rather than crowd the door.

A queue at the bar is not lost revenue. A guest drinking a pint while waiting has already started spending. Make sure the bar staff know who is on the waitlist so they can be looked after.

Brief the floor before doors open

Every busy night should start with a ten minute briefing. The host shares the booking sheet: how many covers in each slot, which tables are held for walk-ins, which large groups are due and any birthday or special request. The kitchen hears the same numbers, so it can prep for the 8 pm peak rather than discover it.

Assign a section to each server and give one person the host stand for the whole rush. A host who also runs drinks will lose track of the waitlist within half an hour. Agree a signal for when the kitchen is falling behind, so the host can slow seating for fifteen minutes instead of flooding a struggling line.

Use booking apps carefully

Table booking platforms bring new guests but usually charge for it. Some also push discounts that cut into weekend margins, on the nights you least need help filling. Many brewpubs use apps to fill slow weekdays and keep weekend bookings on their own phone line or WhatsApp. Our guide to booking and delivery apps sets out what they cost.

Measure the nights, then adjust

Every Monday, look at last weekend: bookings made, no-shows, walk-ins seated, walk-ins turned away and average spend per cover. Even a rough count of guests turned away at the door is valuable. Four weekends of these numbers tell you whether to hold back more tables, change slot times or tighten the deposit rule.

Covers, turn times and spend per head are the operating numbers a brewpub owner should know without opening a spreadsheet. Learning to read them together is the difference between being busy and being profitable.

Common questions

How many tables should a brewpub keep for walk-ins?

Around a quarter of tables on busy nights is a sensible start. Track how fast they fill for a month and adjust from there.

How long should we hold a reserved table?

About 15 minutes past the booking time is common. State the rule in the confirmation message so guests know before they arrive.

Should brewpubs take booking deposits?

For large groups, yes. A deposit or pre-booked set menu protects you from costly no-shows on your busiest nights.
