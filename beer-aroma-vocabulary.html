Building your beer aroma vocabulary

Most people can smell far more in a beer than they can name. Building vocabulary is mostly a matter of practice against known references, not a talent some people are born with.

3 min read · Updated August 2022 · By Ankur Napa and Rahul Baliyan

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer
Written byRahul BaliyanBrewing Science Instructor · Delhi & North India

  2. Start with families, not single words

Why vocabulary is the bottleneck, not your nose

The human nose can distinguish an enormous range of aromas, well beyond what most people ever put into words in everyday conversation. The gap between smelling something and naming it is trained, the same way a wine taster learns to say blackcurrant instead of just fruity. Nobody is born knowing beer vocabulary, and everyone who is good at it built the skill the same slow way.

This is also why professional tasting panels train together rather than alone. Comparing notes immediately after tasting the same sample catches the moment when two people are smelling the same thing but reaching for different words, which is where real vocabulary calibration actually happens.

Start with families, not single words

Group aromas into families first: fruity, covering citrus, stone fruit, tropical and dark fruit, floral, spicy such as clove and pepper, malty covering bread, biscuit, caramel, toffee, roast and coffee, and off flavour notes covered separately elsewhere on this site. When you smell a beer, ask which family it sits in before trying to get more specific. This alone moves a taster from simply saying nice smell to saying that is citrus, probably grapefruit, quite quickly with practice.

Off flavour families deserve the same treatment once you are ready for them: our guide to beer off flavours groups faults the same way, by cause and character, rather than as an unsorted list of unpleasant words to memorise.

Train against real references

Smell an actual grapefruit, then smell an IPA known for grapefruit character, side by side in the same sitting. This single habit does more for vocabulary than any list of words on a page, because it anchors the word to an actual memory of the smell rather than a description of one you have never encountered directly.

Ten minutes spent with three or four common kitchen references, citrus peel, fresh bread, roasted coffee, caramel, will sharpen your palate faster than weeks of tasting beer alone without any point of comparison.

Do not worry if two people smell the same beer and land on different but related words, one calling a note peach and another calling it apricot, for example. What matters is that both are in the right family and roughly the right specificity, not that everyone converges on one identical word.

Match aroma to source where you can

Citrus and tropical fruit notes in a modern IPA usually come from the hops used late in the brewing process. Banana and clove in a wheat beer come from the yeast strain rather than any added ingredient. Caramel and toffee come from the malt, particularly specialty malts kilned at higher temperatures. Knowing roughly where an aroma comes from helps you predict what else you might find in the same beer, and it turns tasting into something closer to detective work than guesswork.

Roasted and smoky notes generally trace back to darker specialty malts kilned at high temperature, while a sour or tart edge usually points to a wild yeast or bacteria present by design, as in a lambic, or by accident, as an infection worth investigating further.

Use the flavour wheel as a map, not a script

The beer flavour wheel organises these families formally and is worth learning once you have a few tastings behind you. Do not start there cold, it works far better once you already have a handful of real smells anchored in memory from direct comparison.

A blind sniff test with a friend, where one person hides three or four household items and the other tries to name them by smell alone, is a genuinely useful warm up exercise before a serious tasting, since it sharpens general olfactory attention before you even pick up a glass.

Write it down every time

A one line note per beer, written at the time rather than from memory afterwards, builds a personal reference library faster than memory alone ever could. Come back to old notes after a few months and you will see your vocabulary getting more specific without having consciously tried to improve it.

Keep the same notebook or app for months rather than starting fresh each season. A long running record lets you notice patterns, that you consistently miss floral notes, say, and target your practice at that specific gap rather than practising evenly across everything.

Common questions

Do I need a trained nose to do this?

No. Vocabulary is built through practice against known references, not natural talent. Anyone who tastes deliberately and keeps notes improves.

What is the fastest way to improve?

Smell single reference items, citrus peel, fresh bread, coffee, alongside the beer you are tasting, so you anchor the word to an actual smell rather than a guess.

Why do I smell something I cannot name?

That is completely normal and is the exact gap this skill closes. Note the family it belongs to, fruity or malty for example, and refine the description over repeated tastings.

Should I trust my first impression or think about it longer?

Note the first impression immediately, since it fades fast, then come back after a minute for a second, more considered pass.
