Crystal and caramel malts

Crystal and caramel malts get their name from a stewing step that caramelises sugar inside the husk. Understanding that process explains what each colour range actually does to a finished beer.

3 min read · Updated March 2022 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  2. What this means for a finished beer
  3. Reading the colour scale
  4. How much is too much

The stewing step that makes crystal malt different

Most malts are kilned dry. Crystal and caramel malt (the two terms are largely interchangeable, with caramel more common in continental usage) are made from green, undried malt that is heated in a rotating drum at a temperature that lets the starches inside the husk convert to sugar and then caramelise, before the whole kernel is finally dried. The result is a glassy, sugar-crystal centre, which is where the name comes from, and sugars that are largely unfermentable because they have already been caramelised rather than left as plain starch for yeast to work on.

What this means for a finished beer

Because much of its sugar cannot be fermented, crystal malt adds body, mouthfeel and residual sweetness rather than alcohol, and it adds these in roughly the same proportion regardless of how well your yeast performs. A beer with 8 percent crystal malt in the grist will taste noticeably fuller and sweeter than the same recipe without it, even if both hit an identical final gravity, because the crystal malt's sugars survive fermentation while the base malt's sugars do not.

Reading the colour scale

Crystal malts are sold by colour, usually in degrees Lovibond, and the number tells you roughly what flavour to expect. A light crystal, 20L to 40L, adds a gentle caramel sweetness without much colour change, suited to pale ales that want a little more body without turning amber. A medium crystal, 60L to 80L, brings a stronger toffee character and visible amber colour, common in English bitters and amber ales. A dark crystal, 120L and above, moves toward raisin, dried fruit and a slightly burnt-sugar edge, more at home in stronger, darker ales.

How much is too much

Crystal malt is almost always used in small amounts, typically 3 to 15 percent of the grist, because its flavour is intense relative to the base malt around it. Push past 15 to 20 percent and most beers start tasting cloying or one dimensional, with the caramel sweetness overwhelming the hop character and any other specialty malts in the bill, including the roasted malts that a darker recipe might also be using.

Crystal malt versus other specialty malts

Crystal malt's job, body and residual sweetness without added colour intensity from roasting, is different from what wheat malt does for head retention or what roasted malts do for colour and coffee-like bitterness. Picking the right specialty malt starts with deciding which of these effects the recipe actually needs, rather than reaching for crystal malt as a default whenever a beer needs more character.

Wet stewing versus dry roasted caramel malt

Traditional crystal malt is made from green, still-wet malt stewed in a rotating drum, letting enzymes already active in the moist grain convert and then caramelise its own starch before drying. A newer alternative, sometimes marketed as caramel malt made from already-dried base malt reheated and toasted rather than stewed wet, produces a broadly similar colour and some similar flavour notes but with less of the true caramelised sugar centre, and brewers who have compared the two side by side generally report a less rounded, less genuinely sweet character from the dry-roasted version. Checking which process a specific product uses, when a supplier states it, is worth doing if a recipe depends on the classic caramel character rather than just the colour.

A useful exercise is brewing the same simple pale base twice, once with 5 percent of a 60L crystal malt and once without, and tasting them side by side once both have carbonated. The difference is usually more obvious than brewers expect from such a small percentage change, which is exactly why crystal malt additions are worth measuring carefully rather than rounding generously.

Storage matters too: crystal malt, like any specialty malt, should be kept sealed and away from moisture, since its already-sweet character can pick up staleness faster than a drier base malt if left open to humid air for long stretches, a real consideration in much of India for large part of the year.

Some suppliers also sell a crystal wheat or crystal rye made from those base grains instead of barley, giving a similar stewed, caramelised character but layered with the base grain's own flavour, wheat's soft bread note or rye's spicy edge, worth exploring once the standard barley-based crystal range feels familiar.

Common questions

Is crystal malt the same thing as caramel malt?

Functionally yes. Both are made the same way, stewed then kilned, and the naming difference is mostly regional convention between suppliers rather than a meaningful process difference.

Does crystal malt add alcohol to a beer?

Very little. Most of its sugar has already caramelised into an unfermentable form, so it adds body and sweetness rather than fermentable extract.

What happens if I use too much crystal malt?

The beer usually tastes cloying and one dimensional, with caramel sweetness dominating the hop character and any other malt flavours in the grist.
