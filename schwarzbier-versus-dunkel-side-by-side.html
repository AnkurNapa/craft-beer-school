Schwarzbier versus dunkel side by side

Schwarzbier and Munich dunkel are both dark German lagers, so they get muddled on menus. One is a bready, malty Bavarian beer. The other is a darker, drier beer from eastern Germany. Here is how to tell them apart in the glass.

Two regions, two dark lagers

Munich dunkel is the traditional dark lager of Bavaria. For a long time, before pale lager took over, it was the everyday beer of Munich. Schwarzbier, meaning black beer, comes from Thuringia and Saxony in eastern Germany. Köstritzer, from Thuringia, is the best known example.

Both are lagers, fermented cool and conditioned cold. Both are dark. That is where most of the similarity ends. Our guide to dunkel and schwarzbier covers each in detail. This piece puts them side by side.

Water helped shape both. Munich's water is relatively high in carbonate, which suits darker malts because their acidity balances the alkaline water. That is one reason Munich brewers made dark beer long before they made pale beer. The regions where schwarzbier developed brewed with their own local water and malt, giving a drier, sharper result. Each beer is, in part, a portrait of its local well.

The numbers compared

The BJCP guidelines give a clear picture. Munich dunkel sits at roughly 4.5 to 5.6% ABV, 18 to 28 IBU and 14 to 28 SRM, deep copper to dark brown. Schwarzbier sits at roughly 4.4 to 5.4% ABV, 20 to 35 IBU and 19 to 30 SRM, very dark brown to black.

So schwarzbier is usually darker and slightly more bitter. Dunkel is usually a touch maltier and sweeter. Strength overlaps almost completely. Colour alone is not enough to tell them apart, because a dark dunkel and a lighter schwarzbier can look similar in the glass.

Dunkel is about malt

Munich dunkel is built on Munich malt, often a large share of the grist and sometimes nearly all of it. Munich malt is kilned darker than pilsner malt, giving rich bread crust, toast and light caramel flavours. A little dark malt adds colour.

Traditionally, many dunkels were decoction mashed, a method where part of the mash is boiled and returned. Our guide to decoction mashing explains it. The flavour target is malt richness without roast: think the crust of fresh bread, not coffee. Bitterness stays in the background.

Schwarzbier is about restrained roast

Schwarzbier gets its black colour from roasted malt, but in small amounts and often using dehusked roasted malts that give colour with very little harsh roast. The result is a beer that looks like a stout but drinks like a lager.

The flavour shows light chocolate or coffee notes over a clean, fairly dry malt base. Bitterness is more noticeable than in dunkel. The finish is crisp. A good schwarzbier surprises people who expect heaviness from its colour. If it tastes acrid, burnt or thick, the roast has been overdone.

Hops play a slightly larger part than in dunkel. German varieties such as Hallertau or Tettnang add a light floral, spicy note and the firmer bitterness that keeps the finish crisp. The roast and the hops work together: both push the beer towards dryness, which is why schwarzbier feels more refreshing than its colour suggests.

How to taste them side by side

Pour both at around 7 to 9 °C into identical glasses. Look first: hold each against a light. Dunkel usually shows ruby or chestnut highlights. Schwarzbier often looks opaque, with only a hint of ruby at the edge.

Smell each. Dunkel gives bread, toast and a little caramel. Schwarzbier gives light roast, cocoa and sometimes a touch of coffee. Taste the dunkel first, then the schwarzbier. Note sweetness, roast and bitterness. Most people find the dunkel rounder and sweeter, the schwarzbier drier and more bitter.

Let both warm for ten minutes and taste again. As the beers warm, the dunkel usually shows more caramel and bread, while the schwarzbier's roast becomes more obvious. Write down what changed. That small exercise teaches more about dark malts than any label.

Why dark lager suits Indian drinkers

Dark beer in India is often associated with heavy, sweet stouts. These two lagers show another side: dark, flavourful and still clean and refreshing at around 5% ABV. They work well with food, from tandoori chicken and seekh kebabs to mushroom curries and dal makhani.

Dunkel's bread crust malt matches roasted and grilled food. Schwarzbier's light roast and dryness cut through richer gravies. Both are good options for drinkers who want more flavour than a pale lager without the weight of a stout. Our guide to Czech dark and amber lagers covers another family worth trying.

What side by side tasting teaches

Tasting two similar beers together sharpens your palate faster than tasting either alone. Differences that are invisible in isolation become obvious in comparison. Professional sensory training uses this method all the time, with carefully chosen pairs and reference samples.

For a first step, set up the dunkel and schwarzbier pairing above with a friend. Each of you write three words for each beer without talking. Then compare notes. You will be surprised how often you agree and where you do not.

Common questions

What is the difference between schwarzbier and dunkel?

Dunkel is a malty Bavarian dark lager built on Munich malt. Schwarzbier is a darker, drier and slightly more bitter lager from eastern Germany with light roast.

Is schwarzbier a stout?

No. It looks dark like a stout but is a lager, fermented cool and conditioned cold, with a light body and restrained roast.

Which is stronger, dunkel or schwarzbier?

They overlap almost completely, both roughly 4.5 to 5.5% ABV.

What temperature should they be served at?

Around 7 to 9 °C, which lets the malt and roast flavours show.
