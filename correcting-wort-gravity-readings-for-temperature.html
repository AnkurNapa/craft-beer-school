Correcting wort gravity readings for temperature

Hydrometers are calibrated at one temperature. Read them on warm wort and you get a gravity that is too low, sometimes by a whole degree Plato. Here is the size of the error and how to correct it properly.

Warm wort is less dense

Liquids expand as they warm. The same wort weighs less per litre at 40 °C than at 20 °C, so a hydrometer floats lower and reads a lower gravity. Nothing about the sugar has changed. Only the reading has.

Most brewing hydrometers and Plato scales are calibrated at 20 °C. Older specific gravity hydrometers, especially from the US or UK, may be calibrated at 15.6 °C (60 °F). Check the stem or the box. A correction table built for the wrong calibration temperature makes the problem worse.

How big the error gets

Water density gives a good feel for the size of the correction. Compared with a reading at 20 °C, a sample at:

  • 25 °C reads low by roughly 0.001 SG, about 0.3 °P.
  • 30 °C reads low by roughly 0.0025 SG, about 0.6 °P.
  • 40 °C reads low by roughly 0.006 SG, about 1.5 °P.
  • 60 °C reads low by roughly 0.015 SG, nearly 4 °P.

Wort expands a little more than water, so the true correction is slightly larger, especially at higher gravities. These are guide figures, not a substitute for a proper table.

An Indian brewery floor in May can easily be at 32 °C. A sample cooled only to room temperature can still be 0.7 °P under. On a 12 °P lager that is a 6% error in the extract figure that feeds your brewhouse yield.

Correction tables and calculators work, within limits

Correction tables and online calculators add a temperature correction to your reading. They work well within about 10 °C of the calibration temperature. Beyond that, errors grow, because the correction assumes a particular wort composition and a hydrometer glass that behaves exactly as expected.

A worked example: you read 1.046 on a sample at 32 °C with a 20 °C hydrometer. The correction is about plus 0.003, so the true gravity is close to 1.049, roughly 12.2 °P. If you had logged 1.046, your yield calculation would understate extract by more than 5%.

Cooling the sample is the reliable fix

The best correction is not to need one. Cool the sample to 20 °C before reading.

  1. Draw the sample into a clean stainless or glass container with a lid.
  2. Stand it in iced water and stir with a thermometer.
  3. Read the hydrometer once the sample is within 1 °C of the calibration temperature.

A cooling coil or a small sample cooler on the brewhouse makes this a two minute job. The lid stops evaporation, which would otherwise concentrate the sample and push the reading up.

Refractometers and density meters

A refractometer reads a drop of wort. Most have automatic temperature compensation, but that only works once the drop and the prism are at the same temperature. Put a drop of hot wort on a cold prism and wait 30 seconds before reading. Refractometers read in Brix, calibrated for sucrose, so wort usually needs a correction factor of about 1.02 to 1.04 to convert to Plato. Measure your own by reading the same cooled sample on both instruments.

Once fermentation starts, alcohol distorts refractometer readings. Our guide to correcting refractometer readings once fermentation has started covers that problem.

Digital density meters with an oscillating U-tube measure temperature and correct automatically. They are the most reliable option if the budget allows, though they still need the sample free of bubbles and solids.

Calibrate the instrument too

A hydrometer can be wrong even at the right temperature. Check it once a month in distilled water at 20 °C. It should read 1.000 or 0 °P. If it reads 1.001, subtract that from every reading. Glass hydrometers break. Replacements from different makers do not always agree.

Write the calibration check in the lab log. It takes two minutes and saves weeks of chasing phantom yield problems.

Gravity feeds everything downstream

Original gravity sets your ABV, your extract figures, your yield and the starting point of every fermentation curve. An error at the start runs through the whole batch record. When numbers do not add up, a warm gravity reading is one of the first things we check.

Building a set of habits where every reading is taken the same way is less exciting than buying instruments. It also gets you further, faster.

Your first step this week

Take your next knockout sample and read it twice: once at whatever temperature it reaches in five minutes on the bench and once properly cooled to 20 °C. Note both. The difference is the error your brew sheets have been carrying.

Common questions

Why does warm wort give a low hydrometer reading?

Warm wort is less dense than the same wort at 20 °C, so the hydrometer floats lower and reads a lower gravity.

What temperature are hydrometers calibrated at?

Most brewing hydrometers are calibrated at 20 °C. Some older specific gravity hydrometers use 15.6 °C (60 °F). Check yours.

Can I use a correction table instead of cooling?

Within about 10 °C of the calibration temperature, yes. For hot samples, cooling to 20 °C is more reliable.
