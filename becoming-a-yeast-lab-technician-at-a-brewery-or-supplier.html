Becoming a yeast lab technician at a brewery or supplier

A yeast lab technician keeps a brewery's yeast healthy, counted and free of contamination. The work suits people with a microbiology or food science background who like routine done properly. Here is what the job involves.

Yeast is the one ingredient that is alive

Malt and hops arrive in sacks and bales. Yeast arrives alive, or is harvested alive from the last batch. Its condition decides how the next fermentation goes. Too few healthy cells and fermentation starts late and finishes high. Contaminated yeast spreads the problem into every batch it touches.

The yeast lab exists to prevent both. In a large brewery that is a dedicated team. In a craft brewery it might be one person with a microscope in a corner of the QC room. At a yeast supplier, it is the core of the business: propagating, testing and shipping cultures to breweries.

What a typical day looks like

Most days are built around a few routine tasks:

  • Cell counts. Using a microscope and a haemocytometer to count cells in a slurry, so the brewers can pitch the right amount. A common target for ales is around 0.75 million cells per millilitre per degree Plato, with roughly double that for lagers.
  • Viability checks. Staining with methylene blue or methylene violet to see what share of cells are alive. Healthy slurry for repitching usually shows 90% or more.
  • Plating. Spreading samples on selective media to check for bacteria and wild yeast, then reading the plates days later.
  • Propagation. Growing a fresh culture from a slant or a small pure sample up to a pitchable volume, step by step.
  • Record keeping. Logging generation numbers, counts and results so trends show up before they become problems.

The microbiology you actually use

You do not need a PhD. You do need clean technique. Aseptic handling, flame sterilising loops, working near a flame or in a laminar flow cabinet and labelling every plate are the habits that matter. A single sloppy transfer can contaminate a culture that took a week to build.

Good counting is a skill in itself. A slurry must be mixed well and diluted accurately, often 1 in 100 or more, before it goes onto the haemocytometer. Counting too few squares or skipping the dilution maths gives a number that looks precise and is wrong by half. Two technicians counting the same sample should land within about 10% of each other. If they do not, the method needs work before the numbers mean anything.

You should know the common beer spoilers. Lactobacillus and Pediococcus cause sourness and, in Pediococcus's case, diacetyl. Acetobacter turns beer vinegary where oxygen gets in. Wild yeasts, including diastatic strains, can over-attenuate beer and cause gushing in bottles or cans. Brewery lab or supplier lab

The two settings feel different. In a brewery lab, yeast is one job among several. You might also measure gravity, pH, colour and dissolved oxygen, run sensory panels and check packaging. The pace follows the brewing schedule. On a busy week you might count slurry for three fermenters before lunch.

At a yeast supplier, the focus is narrower and deeper. You maintain a bank of strains, check their purity and genetic stability, grow them in larger volumes and package them for shipping. Quality systems are stricter because a contaminated batch goes to many customers at once. Cold chain matters too. Liquid yeast shipped across India in summer must stay cold. Supplier labs spend real effort on that.

Who gets hired and why

Most yeast lab technicians come from microbiology, biotechnology, food technology or a related science degree. Lab experience from pharma, dairy or food testing transfers well, because the aseptic technique is the same. What new hires often lack is brewing context: why a count matters to the brewer, what a slow ferment looks like, how yeast changes over generations.

That context is what turns a careful technician into a useful one. Knowing that a viability of 85% means the brewers should pitch more slurry, or start fresh, is worth more than a perfect count delivered without comment. The physiology behind it, from glycogen reserves to stress from high alcohol, is where a brewing science course earns its keep.

Getting your first role

Larger breweries and yeast suppliers hire through formal applications. Craft breweries often hire QC staff who then take on yeast work. Either way, show three things: clean technique, an understanding of beer and a habit of keeping records.

  • Practise cell counts and staining until you can do them quickly and repeatably.
  • Learn the media commonly used for beer spoilers and what grows on each.
  • Read about yeast generations, harvest timing and storage.
  • Put any lab work with numbers and methods on your CV, not just a list of techniques.

Your first step: if you have access to a microscope, borrow a sachet of dried brewing yeast, rehydrate it, then count and stain it. Write up the method and result in one page. That page is a better interview conversation than a line on a CV.

Common questions

What qualifications does a yeast lab technician need?

Most have a degree in microbiology, biotechnology, food technology or a related science. Brewery-specific knowledge can be learned on the job or through a brewing science course.

What is a normal yeast viability for repitching?

Brewers commonly look for 90% or higher viability in slurry they plan to repitch. Below that, they pitch more or propagate fresh yeast.

Is lab experience from pharma useful in a brewery?

Yes. Aseptic technique, documentation and working to procedures transfer directly. The new part is learning how yeast and beer behave.
