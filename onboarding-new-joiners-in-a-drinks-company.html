Onboarding new joiners in a drinks company

Most drinks companies onboard new joiners like any other office: systems first, product never. A month spent on the product early saves a year of half-understood reports. Here is the onboarding shape we recommend to team leads and L&D managers.

3 min read · Updated September 2026 · By Ankur Napa

The first month decides a lot

New joiners in a drinks company often come from banks, IT services, consumer goods or consulting. They are good at their jobs and new to the product. In the first month they are also at their most curious and least embarrassed to ask basic questions. After that, people stop asking what a hectolitre is and start guessing.

That is why product knowledge belongs in onboarding, not in an optional course someone might take next year. The cost is a few days. The return is a person who reads their own data correctly from the start.

Week one: how the product is made

Start with the basics of the drinks your company sells. Beer is grain, fermented in weeks. Wine is fruit, made once a year from one harvest. Whisky is a fermented grain wash that is distilled and then aged in oak for years. Those three timelines explain most of what a new joiner will see in plans and reports. Our guide to beer, wine and whisky compared is a good pre-read.

Keep it concrete. Show the malt, the hops, a grape cluster if the season allows, a piece of charred oak. People remember what they have held. Explain why the same company may run a brewery in weeks, a winery on an annual calendar and a distillery on a horizon of many years.

A vocabulary they will use daily

Every company has its own dialect: cases, hectolitres, ABV, bright tank, wash, vintage, age statement, depletions, sell-in. Give new joiners a short glossary on day one and walk through it in person. Better still, show where each term appears in the reports they will own. Our beer glossary for new joiners is a starting point for beer businesses.

Week two: see a site, safely

Nothing replaces standing in a brewery, winery or distillery. If your company has a plant within reach, a guided visit should be part of onboarding. Brief people properly before they go. Production sites have hot surfaces, wet floors, moving forklifts and noisy packaging lines, so closed shoes and the site's safety gear are non-negotiable.

One hazard is worth naming because office staff never expect it. Fermentation gives off carbon dioxide, which is heavier than air and collects in pits, cellars and inside tanks. You cannot see it or smell it. Visitors stay with their guide and never lean into an open tank or enter a confined space. A good guide will say so before anyone walks in.

If a site visit is not possible, a live online walkthrough with someone from production is the next best thing.

Week three: the numbers behind their job

Once people know the product, connect it to their desk. Planners learn why tank space or cask stock limits supply. Finance learns why yield, losses and excise move the margin. Commercial teams learn why each Indian state behaves like its own market. Tech teams learn where production data is collected and how reliable it is.

This is where onboarding usually stalls, because the people who can explain it are busy running plants. A structured module saves their time.

Week four: a responsible tasting

Close the month with a guided tasting of the portfolio the team supports, if your company policy allows it. Keep it opt-in, with small pours, spit cups, food and water. Make sure non-drinkers can take full part by smelling the samples and following the discussion. Nobody is asked why they are not drinking. Arrange transport home and keep it to a training session, not a party.

Make it repeatable

The best onboarding is the same every time. Write the plan down, keep the materials in one place and run it for every intake, whether that is one person or thirty. Ask each cohort at the end of the month what still confused them and fix that part first.

Give every new joiner a buddy outside their own function, ideally someone from production or quality. A ten-minute call when a strange number appears teaches more than any slide. It also builds the link between head office and the plants that most drinks companies say they want and few actually build.

Common questions

How long should product onboarding take?

Spread across the first month, a few days in total is usually enough to cover how the product is made, the vocabulary, a site visit and the numbers behind each role.

Is a plant visit safe for office staff?

Yes, with a proper briefing, the site's safety gear and a guide. Carbon dioxide from fermentation collects in low spaces and tanks, so visitors stay with the guide and never enter a confined space.

Should onboarding include tasting?

Only if company policy allows it. Keep it opt-in, with small pours, spit cups and non-drinkers fully welcome.
