Positioning a beer brand

Positioning is the decision about who your beer is for and who it is not for. Skip that decision and every other branding choice becomes a guess.

3 min read · Updated January 2024 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. Positioning is a decision, not a description
  2. Pick a lane before the market picks one for you
  3. What you give up matters as much as what you claim
  4. Test the position against your actual beer, not your ambitions
  5. Let positioning drive packaging and pricing, not the other way round
  6. Revisit it, but do not reinvent it constantly

Positioning is a decision, not a description

Many breweries write a positioning statement that is really just a description: premium craft beer, brewed with passion, for people who appreciate quality. That sentence could sit on any label in the country and describe nothing specific. Real positioning names a particular drinker, a particular occasion, and usually something the brand deliberately is not, and it should be uncomfortable to write because it means ruling things out on purpose.

If your positioning statement could be copied onto a competitor's label without anyone noticing the swap, it has not actually positioned anything yet. Try the swap test on your own statement before you commit budget to building a brand around it, and be suspicious of any line that survives the swap unchanged.

Write down who the beer is explicitly not for. That sentence is usually more useful for making design and marketing decisions than the one describing who it is for, because it removes options rather than adding vague ones, and it forces a level of honesty that a purely aspirational statement never delivers, especially once the founders themselves have to defend it out loud to a room of investors or staff.

Pick a lane before the market picks one for you

A session lager built for a hot Chennai evening after work is a different brand, aimed at a different drinker, from a barrel-aged strong ale built for a slow winter evening in the hills, even if both come out of the same brewhouse. Trying to be both at once, in the same range with the same packaging language, confuses the shelf and the drinker equally, and it makes every design decision afterwards a compromise between two audiences.

Choose the lane deliberately, based on who you actually want walking into the taproom, rather than discovering it by accident after a few years of inconsistent messaging and a range that has drifted in six directions at once.

What you give up matters as much as what you claim

A brand positioned around approachability and easy drinking gives up the connoisseur customer who wants technical detail and rare releases. A brand positioned around technical seriousness gives up the casual drinker who just wants something cold after work. Both are legitimate positions, and both can build a genuinely loyal following over time. Trying to hold both at once usually means holding neither convincingly, and it shows up first in muddled packaging and inconsistent tone across your own channels.

Test the position against your actual beer, not your ambitions

Positioning has to match what the brewery can consistently deliver, not what the founders wish it could deliver. A brand positioned as premium and exacting needs consistency batch to batch to back that claim up, because a single inconsistent pour undoes months of careful messaging. If quality control is still inconsistent, a positioning built on approachability and character survives small variation far better than one built on precision does, so be honest about where your process actually stands before you commit to a claim it cannot yet support.

Let positioning drive packaging and pricing, not the other way round

Once the position is set, packaging, price point and even where you choose to sell should follow from it rather than being decided separately by whoever is in the room that week. Our packaging costs guide covers how packaging choices should reflect the position you have chosen, and seasonal and limited releases covers how far a brand can stretch beyond its core position before it starts to blur into something the customer no longer recognises. Price especially should be set with intent, because a mismatched price undermines a position faster than almost any other single decision.

Revisit it, but do not reinvent it constantly

A position can sharpen as a brewery matures, usually by becoming more specific rather than by changing direction entirely. What weakens a brand is flipping position every year chasing whatever category is trending, because customers who committed to the earlier version stop trusting the brand to be anything consistent, and consistency is most of what a small brand has to sell against larger, better funded competitors who can outspend it on almost everything else.

Common questions

Can a brewery have more than one position?

A brewery can run sub-brands or ranges with different positions, but each individual beer or range needs its own clear position rather than trying to be everything at once.

How do we know if our positioning is too narrow?

Narrow positioning is rarely the real problem for a new brewery. Vague positioning that fails to attract anyone strongly is far more common and far more damaging.

Should positioning change as we grow?

It can sharpen and deepen, but changing it entirely confuses the customers who already trust the earlier version, so any shift should be gradual and well communicated.
