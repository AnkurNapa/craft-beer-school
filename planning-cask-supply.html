Planning cask supply

A distillery can run out of casks long before it runs out of grain. Most whisky casks are second hand, imported and in limited supply. This guide explains how cask supply is planned, from the filling forecast to the cooperage and what changes when the wood lands in India.

The cask is an ingredient, not a container

Much of a mature whisky's flavour and nearly all its colour come from the cask. So a cask plan is part of the recipe plan. A blend that calls for spirit matured in ex-sherry casks cannot be made from ex-bourbon barrels just because those were cheaper this year.

Scotch must mature in oak casks of no more than 700 litres. Many other whisky rules use oak or wood with similar limits. Within that, the type, size and history of each cask decide how the whisky turns out. Our guide to whisky maturation and casks explains what the wood does.

Know the common sizes and where they come from

  • Barrel. The American standard barrel holds about 200 litres. Bourbon must be aged in new charred oak, so each barrel is used once for bourbon, then sold on. This is the workhorse cask for Scotch, Irish and Indian whisky.
  • Hogshead. Around 250 litres, often rebuilt from barrel staves with new ends.
  • Butt and puncheon. Around 500 litres, traditionally associated with sherry seasoning in Spain.
  • Virgin oak. New casks that have held nothing before, giving strong oak flavour quickly.

Because bourbon must use new oak, the American whiskey industry releases a steady flow of used barrels. That supply is linked to how much bourbon is being made. When bourbon production rises or falls, the price and availability of ex-bourbon barrels for everyone else follow.

Turn the filling plan into a cask plan

The cask plan starts from the filling forecast: how many litres of new make spirit, of which type, will be filled in each future month. Convert that into litres at filling strength, then divide by the cask size to get the number of casks needed, by type.

An example with round numbers: a distillery planning to fill 100,000 litres of spirit at 63.5% ABV into barrels of about 200 litres needs around 500 barrels, before allowing for rejects. Add a margin for casks that fail inspection on arrival. Then split the total by type according to the blend recipes: so many first fill ex-bourbon, so many refill, so many sherry seasoned.

Lead times and contracts decide what you can get

Good casks are not bought off the shelf. Sherry seasoned casks are often made to order in Spain and seasoned for a period before shipping. Ex-bourbon barrels are bought through cooperages and brokers, often on annual or multi-year contracts. Lead times of many months are normal, longer for special or new oak casks.

For an Indian distillery, casks are usually imported, which adds shipping time, customs clearance and the risk of damage in transit. Planning teams lock in the bulk of their needs well ahead and keep spot purchases for top ups.

Reuse stretches the supply, up to a point

A cask can be filled more than once. The first time it holds whisky after its original use, it is called first fill and gives the most flavour. Second and third fills give less each time. After two or three fills many casks are rejuvenated: the inside is shaved and re-charred to expose fresh wood. Our guide to first fill versus refill casks covers the trade-offs.

So a cask plan tracks every cask's history, not just its count. A warehouse full of tired third fill casks is not a substitute for the first fill wood a recipe calls for.

Indian heat changes how casks behave

Casks dry out in transit and in storage. Dry staves shrink and leak when filled. The floor detail most teams learn early: casks are checked and often swelled with water before filling, then pressure or leak tested. A small weep at a stave joint on filling day becomes a large loss over years in a hot warehouse.

Heat also speeds maturation. Spirit picks up flavour from the wood much faster in India than in Scotland, so a cask may be ready sooner and may be exhausted sooner too. Refill cycles and finishing times built on Scottish experience often need adjusting.

Cask cost belongs in the whisky budget

Casks are a real line in the cost of maturing whisky, not packaging overhead. A first fill sherry seasoned butt costs many times what a refill ex-bourbon barrel does. Imported casks are priced in foreign currency, so a weaker rupee raises the cost of the same plan. Freight, customs duty and the share that fail inspection all add to the landed cost per usable cask.

That is why finance and blending teams need to agree the cask mix together. A recipe change from refill to first fill casks may improve the whisky. It also changes the cost of every litre filled for years. Some distillers also sell casks back to the market after their last useful fill, which recovers a little of the cost. A cask plan with prices attached makes those trade-offs visible early.

A first step for planners

Take next year's filling plan and turn it into a cask requirement by type and month. Then compare it with casks on order, casks in the yard waiting to be filled and empty casks due back from emptying. The gap, by month, is your cask risk. Most cask shortages show up on that sheet six months before they reach the filling store.

Common questions

Why are most whisky casks second hand?

Bourbon must be aged in new charred oak, so used bourbon barrels are released in large numbers. Other whisky makers buy them. Sherry seasoned casks are reused in the same way.

How many times can a whisky cask be used?

Often two or three fills before it gives little flavour. Many casks are then rejuvenated by shaving and re-charring the inside.

What size are whisky casks?

Common sizes are the barrel at about 200 litres, the hogshead at about 250 litres and the butt or puncheon at about 500 litres. Scotch casks may not exceed 700 litres.
