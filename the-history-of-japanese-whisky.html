The history of Japanese whisky

Japanese whisky is barely a hundred years old. It began with a partnership between a businessman and a chemist who had trained in Scotland. It went through a long boom, a long slump and a revival so strong that stocks ran short. Here is how it happened.

A young chemist went to Scotland to learn

In 1918 Masataka Taketsuru, the son of a sake brewing family, travelled to Scotland to study chemistry at the University of Glasgow. He also worked at Scottish distilleries, including in Speyside and Campbeltown, taking detailed notes on malting, distilling and maturation. In 1920 he returned to Japan with his notebooks and his Scottish wife, Rita.

Japan already had a market for western style spirits, but much of what was sold as whisky was imitation: neutral spirit with flavourings. Taketsuru brought back the method for the real thing.

The first distillery opened near Kyoto in 1923

Shinjiro Torii, a merchant who had made his name selling wine, wanted to make genuine whisky in Japan. He hired Taketsuru and in 1923 built a distillery at Yamazaki, between Kyoto and Osaka. The site was chosen for its soft water and humid climate, where several rivers meet and mist is common.

The first whiskies sold slowly. Their heavy, smoky style, close to the Scotch Taketsuru had studied, was not what Japanese drinkers expected. Torii pushed for a lighter style suited to local food and taste. That tension between Scottish method and Japanese preference shaped the industry from the start.

A second founder chose Hokkaido

In 1934 Taketsuru left to start his own company. He built a distillery at Yoichi on the northern island of Hokkaido, whose cool climate, sea air and peat reminded him of Scotland. Yoichi became known for heavier, peatier spirit, distilled in pot stills heated by direct coal fire.

So within about a decade Japan had two whisky houses with two founders and two philosophies. Their rivalry explains something unusual, helped by the fact that Japanese distillers rarely trade spirit with each other. Scottish blenders swap whisky between distilleries all the time. Japanese producers tend to make many different styles under one roof instead, using different still shapes, yeasts and casks.

Boom, slump and the highball revival

After the Second World War whisky became the drink of Japan's growing salaried class. Cheap blends were sold in whisky bars across the country. Consumption peaked in the early 1980s, then fell for more than two decades as drinkers moved to shochu, beer and other drinks.

Two things turned it around. From the early 2000s Japanese single malts and blends started winning top awards in international competitions, which made whisky lovers abroad pay attention. At home, producers promoted the highball, whisky and chilled soda in a tall glass with plenty of ice, as a light drink to have with food. It worked. Demand at home and abroad grew so fast that well-known age statement whiskies were withdrawn in the 2010s because there was not enough aged stock to sell.

Mizunara oak gives a local signature

During the war, imported casks were hard to get, so Japanese coopers made casks from native mizunara oak. It is difficult to work with: it is porous, grows crooked and casks made from it tend to leak. Distillers found that after long ageing it gives aromas often described as sandalwood, incense and coconut.

Mizunara is now prized and expensive, usually used for finishing or for a portion of a blend rather than for whole ranges. Our guide to mizunara oak covers how it behaves in the warehouse.

A 2021 standard finally defined Japanese whisky

For most of its history Japanese law said little about what could be called Japanese whisky. Some bottles labelled that way contained imported whisky blended in Japan. In 2021 the Japan Spirits and Liqueurs Makers Association introduced a labelling standard, with a transition period that ended in 2024.

Under it, Japanese whisky must be made from malted grain with other cereals allowed, using water from Japan. It must be fermented, distilled and matured in Japan, for at least three years in wooden casks of no more than 700 litres, then bottled in Japan at a minimum of 40% ABV. Whiskies that do not meet the standard can still be sold, but not labelled as Japanese whisky. It is a voluntary industry standard rather than a law, though members follow it.

How to taste the history

The highball is the easiest place to start. Take one part whisky to three or four parts very cold soda, with plenty of ice, stirred once. A good Japanese blend stays clear and fragrant even when diluted. Then try a single malt neat next to it, with a few drops of water. Our guide to tasting Japanese whisky suggests what to look for. Keep the measures small and the water glass full.

The other classic Japanese serve is mizuwari, whisky stretched with chilled still water over ice, often around one part whisky to two parts water. It was built for long evenings with food. Try it with something lightly salted or grilled and notice how the dilution brings out fruit and cereal that the neat whisky hides behind its alcohol. It is a reminder that Japanese whisky grew up at the dinner table, not only in the tasting room. That makes it a good fit for Indian food too, where a neat 46% spirit fights with chilli rather than helping it.

Common questions

Who founded Japanese whisky?

Shinjiro Torii built the first dedicated malt whisky distillery at Yamazaki in 1923, with Masataka Taketsuru, who had trained in Scotland, as his distiller. Taketsuru later founded his own distillery at Yoichi in 1934.

What does the label Japanese whisky guarantee?

Under the 2021 industry standard, the whisky must be fermented, distilled, matured for at least three years and bottled in Japan, using Japanese water and malted grain.

Why were some Japanese whiskies discontinued?

Demand grew faster than aged stocks could support in the 2010s. Producers withdrew several age statement whiskies because the old whisky simply was not there.
