Becoming a BJCP beer judge

Becoming a BJCP judge takes real, deliberate study and tasting practice. This guide covers the actual structure of the path without inventing pass marks or fees that change over time.

What a BJCP judge is actually trained to do

BJCP's certification system trains people to evaluate a beer against a defined style guideline, scoring it on specific criteria and, just as importantly, writing clear, useful feedback the brewer can actually act on. It is a skill built on structured tasting and a genuinely deep familiarity with style parameters, not a general opinion about whether a beer tastes good.

A strong judge can tell a brewer precisely why a beer missed a style, whether that is an off flavour, a balance issue, or a simple mismatch against the guideline the beer was entered under.

Every BJCP scoresheet works through the same structure: aroma, appearance, flavour, mouthfeel and overall impression, each scored against the style's specific descriptors. Learning to move through that structure quickly and consistently, under a strict time limit per beer, is itself a skill separate from simply knowing what a style should taste like.

The path usually starts with the entry exam

Most candidates begin with BJCP's entry-level exam, which tests knowledge of styles, ingredients, the brewing process and off-flavour recognition. It is a serious exam that rewards real study, not a formality, and BJCP publishes its own current study guide and exam format directly on its website, which is the accurate source for structure and requirements.

Effective study usually blends two things: working systematically through the BJCP style guidelines until the numbers for each style, gravity range, bitterness, colour, are second nature, and tasting as many examples of each style as possible against those numbers. Reading about a style and tasting it are different kinds of learning, and the exam rewards both.

Tasting exams test something books cannot teach alone

Higher BJCP ranks require a tasting exam, where a candidate evaluates real beers under exam conditions and has to produce a judge's scoresheet that another experienced judge would recognise as accurate and fair. This is where deliberate, structured tasting practice, ideally alongside experienced judges, matters more than any amount of additional reading.

Exam boards score each candidate's scoresheet against a panel of experienced graders, checking not just whether the score itself lands in a reasonable range but whether the written justification actually supports it. A scoresheet that gives a beer a middling score but describes only positive qualities, or the reverse, tends to lose marks regardless of the number written at the top.

How the rank system is generally structured

BJCP organises judges into a series of ranks, typically from an entry Recognised level through Certified, National, Master and Grand Master, each asking for more tasting experience, a longer judging record and often a stronger exam result than the one before. Progress through the ranks generally needs both a passing exam score and an accumulating log of real competition judging, not the exam on its own.

Because the exact scoring thresholds and experience requirements change from time to time, treat BJCP's own website as the current source rather than anything printed a few years back.

Build experience by actually judging

Local and regional homebrew competitions are where most candidates get real judging reps, sitting alongside more experienced judges, comparing notes, and learning to write feedback that is specific rather than vague. India's homebrewing and craft beer competition scene has grown enough that opportunities to judge, even informally, exist for anyone willing to seek them out.

New judges tend to make the same few mistakes early on: scoring too generously because criticising a beer feels uncomfortable, writing vague feedback such as good balance instead of naming the actual malt or hop character responsible, and losing track of time so the last beer in a flight gets a rushed, thin scoresheet. All three improve quickly with practice and honest feedback from a judging partner.

Larger competitions also run a best of show round, where a small panel re-tastes the top scoring beers from every category to choose overall winners. Watching or assisting at that stage is a fast way to see how experienced judges argue for a beer, because best of show decisions are made out loud, by consensus, rather than on a private scoresheet.

Fees, exam dates and requirements change, so check BJCP directly

As with other certifications, BJCP's own website is the only reliable source for current fees, exam scheduling and rank requirements, since these are set and updated by BJCP itself and can shift meaningfully between exam cycles.

Judging sharpens skills useful far beyond competitions

The tasting discipline and style knowledge built through BJCP training transfers directly into quality control work at a brewery, described in our jobs inside a brewery guide, and into a homebrewer's own recipe development, covered in our homebrewer to professional guide.

Common questions

Do I need to be a brewer to become a BJCP judge?

No. Many BJCP judges are enthusiasts, hospitality professionals or homebrewers rather than commercial brewers. The requirement is trained tasting skill and style knowledge, not a brewing job.

How long does it take to become a certified judge?

It varies considerably by how much deliberate tasting practice and study a candidate puts in, and by how often they can sit exams, so there is no fixed timeline that applies to everyone.

Where do BJCP exams happen in India?

Availability depends on local homebrew clubs and organisers running exam sessions, so check BJCP's own site and Indian homebrewing communities for current opportunities near you.
