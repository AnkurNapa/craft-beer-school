Imperial stout

Big, dark and built to age, imperial stout takes everything a regular stout does and pushes it further: more malt, more roast, and considerably more alcohol. Here is what that extra strength actually changes in the brewhouse.

3 min read · Updated February 2025 · Craft Beer School

A beer built for a long journey

Imperial stout traces back to strong export beers brewed in England for the Russian imperial court in the 18th century, strong enough in alcohol and hop bitterness to survive the sea crossing to the Baltic without spoiling. The name stuck even once the export trade that created it had ended, and the modern craft revival of the style borrowed the name and the strength while dropping the actual voyage.

What sets it apart from a regular stout

ABV typically runs 8 to 12%, sometimes higher, built from a large grain bill with an original gravity that can reach 1.090 to 1.120. Roasted malts, chocolate, black patent and roasted barley layer up into flavours of dark chocolate, coffee, dried fruit and sometimes a warming, almost sherry-like character from the alcohol itself.

Some versions add extras on top of this base, vanilla, cacao nibs, barrel ageing in used spirit casks, which is a modern addition to the style rather than anything historical, but it sits naturally alongside a beer already built around depth and intensity.

Getting a healthy fermentation at that gravity

A wort this dense stresses yeast hard. Brewers pitch a much larger cell count than for an ordinary ale, aerate the wort thoroughly before pitching, and sometimes step feed sugar or extract partway through fermentation to avoid overwhelming the yeast at the start. Skimp on any of these and the beer finishes with unwanted hot, solventy fusel alcohol notes instead of the rounder warmth the style is after.

A healthy fermentation also needs longer than a standard ale, sometimes two to three weeks in primary before the yeast has genuinely finished, and rushing that timeline is one of the more common reasons a homebrewed imperial stout tastes harsh rather than rich.

Hops balance weight, not aroma

Bitterness needs to be assertive, 50 to 90 IBU, simply to stop the beer tasting like liquid dessert against that much malt. Hop aroma is usually restrained, since the point is not a hop forward beer, and English or American bittering varieties both work depending on which side of the style you are aiming for.

Why this style rewards patience

Imperial stout is one of the few beer styles that genuinely improves with months, sometimes years, of bottle conditioning, as harsh alcohol notes soften and oxidative sherry and dried fruit character develops in a controlled way. A cool, dark cellar around 12 to 15 degrees Celsius suits long term storage far better than a fridge, which slows the maturation almost to a stop.

Brewing and storing it in Indian conditions

The main practical challenge locally is not the brewing, it is storage. A beer meant to mature slowly over a year needs a stable cool spot, and an un-cooled Indian storeroom running through a full summer will age it far faster and less gracefully than a proper cellar would. If cellar space is not available, treat it as a beer to drink within a few months rather than one to lay down, and compare it against our lighter barleywine guide if you want a pale equivalent built the same way.

Serving it properly

A snifter or tulip glass concentrates the aroma better than a straight pint glass, and a serving temperature of 12 to 15 degrees Celsius, warmer than most beer, lets the complex malt and alcohol character open up rather than staying locked down by the chill.

Pour a smaller measure than you would for a session beer. At this strength and this intensity of flavour, a full pint is rarely the point.

Why brewers keep coming back to this style

Few styles reward a brewer's process control as directly as imperial stout does. Get the yeast health, oxygen management and packaging right, and the beer keeps improving for years in the bottle. Get any one of them wrong, and the same high gravity that gives the style its depth will just as readily expose the mistake.

It is also one of the more satisfying styles to compare across breweries and vintages, since the base recipe leaves enough room for real variation in roast intensity, barrel character and age that two glasses poured a year apart rarely taste identical.

Common questions

Why is it called imperial stout?

It was originally brewed strong for export to the court of the Russian empire in the 18th century, and the name outlasted the trade that created it.

Does imperial stout get better with age?

Often yes, over months to a couple of years in a cool, dark, stable spot. Harsh alcohol notes soften and richer dried fruit and sherry character can develop.

Why does imperial stout need so much more yeast?

The very high original gravity stresses yeast heavily. A larger pitch and good aeration help fermentation finish cleanly instead of producing harsh fusel alcohols.

Is imperial stout always very bitter?

It carries firm bitterness, usually 50 to 90 IBU, mainly to balance the large amount of malt sweetness rather than for hop aroma.
