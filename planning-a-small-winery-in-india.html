Planning a small winery in India

A winery has one shot a year at its raw material. Planning a small one in India means getting grapes, cold handling, acid and cash flow right before the fruit arrives, because nothing can be redone until next season.

4 min read · Updated September 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Grapes and the Indian harvest

Most Indian wine grapes come from Maharashtra and Karnataka, with smaller plantings elsewhere. Many vineyards use double pruning: a back pruning after harvest and a fruit pruning around October, so the grapes ripen in the dry, cooler months and are picked roughly between January and March.

Decide whether you will own vines, contract growers or buy fruit at harvest. Owned or contracted vines give you control over ripeness and picking date. Bought fruit is quicker to start but you get what is left. Either way, agree in writing on the sugar and acid you will accept.

Sugar, acid and the heat

Grapes ripened in warm conditions build sugar fast while acid falls. The result can be high-alcohol wine with a high pH, which tastes flat and is harder to protect from spoilage.

  • Sugar. Measure in degrees Brix. Around 21 to 24 °Brix covers most still table wines.
  • pH. Many winemakers aim for roughly 3.2 to 3.5 for whites and 3.4 to 3.7 for reds.
  • Titratable acidity. Typically around 5 to 8 g/L, expressed as tartaric acid.

Adding tartaric acid to the must is a common correction in warm regions. Pick early in the day and get the fruit cool fast. Grapes that sit in the afternoon sun in a crate start fermenting with whatever yeast is on them before they reach the winery.

The kit you actually need

  • A destemmer-crusher.
  • A press. A pneumatic press is gentler and better for whites, but a basket press works at small scale.
  • Jacketed stainless tanks and a chiller big enough for harvest week, when everything ferments at once.
  • Pumps, hoses and a cleaning set-up that handles sticky skins and seeds.
  • Barrels if you plan oak-aged wines, plus space to keep them cool.

If you run a brewery, your tanks and glycol may help, but harvest compresses a year of crushing into a few weeks. Size cooling for that peak.

Fermentation: whites and reds differ

Whites are pressed first and the juice is fermented cool, usually around 12 to 18 °C, to keep fresh fruit aroma. Reds ferment on their skins, warmer, around 25 to 30 °C, because colour and tannin come from the skins. The cap of skins rises and must be punched down or pumped over a few times a day.

Watch red ferments closely. Above roughly 32 to 35 °C yeast can stall, leaving sugar behind and a wine exposed to spoilage. On a warm February afternoon a large red tank with no cooling can get there faster than people expect.

Sulphite and stability

Sulphur dioxide protects wine against oxidation and microbes. What matters is the molecular form. How much of it you get from a given free SO2 level depends on pH. At high pH you need much more free SO2 for the same protection, which is one more reason to manage acid.

Before bottling, check that the wine is dry or stabilised, that it is protein and cold stable if it is a white and that free SO2 is where you want it. Our budget QC lab guide covers equipment that carries over.

After fermentation: oxygen and hygiene

Once fermentation finishes, wine has lost the carbon dioxide blanket that protected it. Keep tanks full or under inert gas, top up barrels regularly and avoid splashing on transfer. A half-empty tank in a warm room is how wine turns to vinegar, because acetic acid bacteria need air.

Cleaning matters as much as in a brewery. Grape skins and seeds stick in pumps, valves and press bags. Clean everything the same day it is used during harvest, however tired the team is. Check for mould in hoses and gaskets before the next pick arrives.

Licensing, cash and the first vintage

Several Indian states have their own wine policies. They differ on licensing, taxation and where wine can be sold. The broad sequence is similar to other alcohol production: site and in-principle approval from excise, pollution-control consents, inspection, the production licence, then label registration before sale. Rules vary by state and change, so work with a licensing adviser and check the current notification.

Cash flow is the quiet risk. You buy all your fruit in a few weeks, then wait months before red wine is ready to sell. Model that gap before signing anything. If you want a product that sells sooner or uses the same grapes differently, look at how fortified wines are made.

Common questions

When is grape harvest in India?

In the main wine regions of Maharashtra and Karnataka, harvest usually falls roughly between January and March, after a fruit pruning around October.

Why do Indian wines sometimes taste flat?

Grapes ripened in the heat lose acid while sugar rises, giving a high pH. Picking earlier and adjusting acid with tartaric acid are common fixes.

Can I use my brewery tanks for wine?

Often for fermenting and storage, if cleaning and cooling are adequate. You still need a crusher, a press and a chiller sized for harvest week.
