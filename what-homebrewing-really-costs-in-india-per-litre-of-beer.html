What homebrewing really costs in India per litre of beer

Ask a homebrewer what a litre costs and you will usually get the ingredient bill divided by 20. The real figure is higher, because energy, losses and equipment all count. We will not hand you a price list, since prices move every month, but we will give you a cost sheet you can fill in from your own receipts.

Start with the litres you actually drink

The first mistake is dividing by the wrong number. A 20 litre batch does not give you 20 litres of beer. Some wort stays behind with the hops in the kettle. Yeast and sediment, called trub, sit at the bottom of the fermenter. Dry hopping soaks up more. A typical homebrew batch that starts at 20 litres in the fermenter ends up as about 17 or 18 litres in bottles.

So the bottom line of your cost sheet is always packaged litres. Measure them: count your bottles and multiply by 0.33 or 0.5.

The ingredient bill

For a 20 litre pale ale at an original gravity of about 1.050, the quantities look roughly like this:

  • Malt: 4.5 to 5 kg, most of it base malt with a little specialty malt.
  • Hops: 60 to 100 g for a pale ale. An IPA with heavy dry hopping can use 200 g or more.
  • Yeast: one 11 g packet of dry yeast, or a liquid pack.
  • Water treatment: a few grams of brewing salts and a Campden tablet to remove chlorine.

Multiply each by what you paid. In India, nearly all specialty malt and hops are imported, so they cost more than in Europe or the US and their prices follow the rupee. Hops are the line that grows fastest as you brew hoppier beers. Yeast is the line people underestimate: one packet looks cheap until you notice it costs as much as a kilo of malt.

Energy is a real cost

You can estimate energy from physics. Heating 27 litres of water from 25 °C to boiling takes about 2.4 kWh. Boiling off 4 litres over an hour takes about 2.5 kWh more, because turning water into steam needs a lot of energy. That is roughly 5 kWh in theory. Real kettles lose heat through their walls and lids, so expect more.

On an electric element, multiply your kWh by your tariff. On LPG, note how many brew days a cylinder lasts and divide. If you run a fridge for fermentation, add its running cost over two or three weeks. In a hot city a fridge holding 18 °C works hard.

Water, cleaning and the small stuff

An immersion chiller can use 100 to 150 litres of tap water per brew day. Some homebrewers collect it to clean with afterwards. If you buy drinking water cans for brewing because your borewell water is very hard, count those too.

Then the small items: sanitiser, cleaning powder, bottle caps (about 55 to 60 per batch in 330 ml bottles), priming sugar and the occasional replacement airlock or tube. None of these is much alone. Together they add a noticeable amount per litre.

The equipment nobody divides

Your kettle, fermenter, chiller and bottles all wear out or get replaced. The honest way to include them is to guess how many batches each item will last and divide its price by that number. A kettle that lasts 100 batches adds very little per litre. A starter kit you abandon after four batches adds a lot.

Bottles look free once you own them, but you will break and lose some every year. Kegs cost more at the start and nothing per batch afterwards.

Putting it together

Add up the ingredient bill, energy, water and consumables for one batch. Add the per batch share of equipment. Divide by packaged litres. That is your real cost per litre.

Most homebrewers find two things. First, a litre of their homebrew still costs well below the price of a litre in a brewpub, because the bar price carries excise, rent, staff and margin. Our guide on why craft beer costs more explains where that money goes. Second, the cost per litre falls sharply as they get better. A brewer whose mash only extracts 60% of the sugar from the malt needs noticeably more grain for the same gravity than one who reaches 75%.

That second point is where technique turns into money. Better efficiency, fewer dumped batches and less loss to trub all reduce the cost of every litre. If you are still on a kit, our guide to the homebrewing starter kit in India covers what to buy first.

Common questions

Is homebrewing cheaper than buying beer in India?

Per litre, usually yes, because bar and shop prices include excise, rent, staff and margin. The gap narrows if you count equipment and brew rarely.

How many litres of beer do I get from a 20 litre batch?

Usually about 17 to 18 litres in bottles. Some wort is lost to hops, trub and sediment.

What is the most expensive ingredient in a homebrew batch?

In hoppy beers it is usually the hops, since they are imported. In malt-led beers the malt bill leads, with yeast close behind for a single packet.

How can I lower my cost per litre?

Improve your mash efficiency, cut losses in the kettle and fermenter and avoid dumped batches. Buying base malt in bulk with a club also helps.
