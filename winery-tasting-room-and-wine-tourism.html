Starting a tasting room and wine tourism at a small Indian winery

For a small winery, a tasting room turns visitors into customers who know your name. Done badly, it ties up the winemaker every weekend and loses money on tours. This is how to plan one that pays.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Why a tasting room matters

Every bottle you sell through a distributor and a shop passes on margins at each step. A bottle sold at your own counter, where state rules allow direct sales, keeps much more of that value at the winery. Visitors who taste your wine where it is made also tend to remember it, look for it in shops and bring friends.

India already has a wine tourism culture to build on. Nashik in Maharashtra has drawn weekend visitors for years. Wineries around Bengaluru and in other regions have followed. Our guide to Indian wine regions shows where the clusters are. The opportunity is real, though it depends on location, licences and how you run the day.

Licences come first

Selling and serving wine at a winery needs the right permissions from the state excise department. Some states have wine tourism or winery policies that allow tasting, on site sales and food. Others treat a tasting room like a bar or do not allow it at all. Rules, fees and timings vary by state and change, so start with the current notification and a licensing consultant before you design anything.

Ask specific questions. Can you sell by the glass as well as by the bottle? Can you serve food? Who needs the food licence? What are the permitted hours? Can you sell wine to take away? Can you run events? The answers shape the whole plan.

What visitors pay for

Most tasting rooms earn from a mix of these:

  • Tasting flights. Four to six small pours of 25 to 30 ml with notes, for a fixed fee. Many wineries credit part of the fee against a bottle purchase.
  • Guided tours. Vineyard, crush area, tanks and barrel room, finishing with a tasting.
  • Bottle sales. Usually the main profit, if your licence allows it.
  • Food. Simple plates that match the wines keep people longer and spending more.
  • Events. Harvest days, grape stomping, music evenings and private bookings.

Keep pours honest and small. A tasting is about learning the wines. A responsible room watches how much each guest is served.

Plan around the Indian seasons

In the main grape regions, harvest and crush mostly fall in the cooler months early in the year, roughly January to April depending on the region and the vineyard. That is your busiest season for visitors and for the winery. Plan staffing so the winemaker is not leading tours in the middle of a crush.

The monsoon brings mud, fewer visitors and wet vineyard walks. Summer afternoons are hot, so schedule tours for mornings and late afternoons, give visitors shade and water. Keep tasting wine cool. A white served warm in a hot tasting room does your wine no favours.

Staffing and the visitor experience

The person behind the counter is your brand. Train them on every wine, the vineyard and how the wine is made, so they can answer questions without reading notes. They also need to know when to stop serving a guest and how to handle someone who has had too much.

Think about how people arrive. Most wineries sit outside cities, which means someone is driving. Offer non-alcoholic drinks that feel like a choice, not a punishment: grape juice from your own vineyard, sodas or alcohol-free options. Spit buckets on every counter. Some wineries partner with drivers or tour operators so groups do not need to drive.

Running the numbers

A tasting room has its own costs: staff on weekends, wine poured for free or at a low price, glassware, cleaning, food waste and the building itself. Count the wine you pour as a cost at its real value, not as a free sample.

Work out a typical weekend: visitors per day, the share who buy a flight, the share who buy bottles and the average spend. Compare that with the cost of staffing and running the room. Many tasting rooms run close to break even on tastings and make their money on bottle sales and repeat customers. Our guide to taproom economics applies much the same thinking to breweries.

Then add the long view. A visitor who joins a mailing list or a wine club and buys a case every few months is worth far more than one weekend's tasting fee.

Common questions

Do I need a separate licence for a winery tasting room?

Usually yes. Tasting, on site sales and food each need permission from the state. The rules vary widely. Check the current state policy with a licensing consultant.

When is the busiest season for wine tourism in India?

The harvest and crush months early in the year, roughly January to April in the main regions, plus cool weekends through the winter.

How big should a tasting pour be?

Most tasting rooms pour about 25 to 30 ml per wine in a flight of four to six wines. That is enough to learn the wine without overserving.

How do tasting rooms make money?

Mostly through bottle sales and repeat customers. Tasting fees and tours often only cover their own costs.
