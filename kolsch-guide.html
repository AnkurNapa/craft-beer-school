Kölsch

Kolsch sits in an odd technical middle ground, and it is one of the only beer names in the world protected by regional law. Here is how both of those facts shape the beer.

3 min read · Updated January 2024 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. An ale brewed to taste like a lager
  2. What to expect in the glass
  3. A legally protected name
  4. Fermentation: the technical middle ground
  5. The small glass is not a gimmick

An ale brewed to taste like a lager

Kolsch, from Cologne, is fermented with a top fermenting ale yeast, technically making it an ale, but at unusually cool ale fermentation temperatures, then cold conditioned afterward much like a lager. The result is a beer with an ale's yeast heritage but a lager's clean, crisp character, deliberately blurring a line that most styles keep firm.

This hybrid approach was largely a response to pilsner's growing popularity in the 1800s, giving Cologne's brewers a way to make something similarly crisp and pale while keeping their traditional top fermenting yeast.

What to expect in the glass

Kolsch pours a very pale gold, brilliantly clear, with a delicate aroma of light fruit, sometimes a faint apple or pear note from its ale yeast, alongside a mild noble hop character. Bitterness sits at a moderate 18 to 30 IBU, and the finish is crisp, dry and clean, closer to a lager than most ales manage.

A well made Kolsch should show only the faintest ale character. If the fruity ester profile is prominent, it has probably fermented too warm for the style.

A legally protected name

Since 1986, the Kolsch Konvention, an agreement among breweries in the Cologne region, has legally restricted the name Kolsch to beer brewed within a defined area around the city, following an agreed set of production standards. This makes it one of only a small number of beer names anywhere with this kind of formal protection, similar in spirit to protected regional food names, and it is worth knowing about even if you have no plans to brew commercially in Germany yourself.

Breweries elsewhere making a similar style typically label it Kolsch style ale rather than using the protected name directly.

Fermentation: the technical middle ground

A specific Kolsch ale yeast strain ferments at the cool end of ale territory, generally 15 to 18°C, cooler than a typical ale fermentation, before the beer is cold conditioned near 0°C for several weeks, similar to lagering. This combination is what suppresses most of the fruity ester character an ale yeast would normally produce at warmer temperatures.

Getting this balance right needs real temperature control discipline, since fermenting even a little too warm pushes the beer toward a fruitier character that moves it away from the clean, lager like profile the style is meant to have.

A brewery without dedicated lager tank space can still manage this style reasonably well, since the cold conditioning period, while important, does not need the months a genuine lager sometimes gets. A few focused weeks near freezing does most of the work.

The small glass is not a gimmick

Kolsch is traditionally served in a tall, narrow 200ml glass called a stange, and in Cologne's traditional beer halls a server will keep bringing fresh, small glasses on a tray until you place a coaster over your empty one to signal you are done. The small serving size exists partly because Kolsch loses its delicate crispness relatively quickly once poured, so a smaller, fresher pour keeps the beer at its best throughout drinking.

This is worth taking seriously if you are pouring Kolsch commercially: a large glass that sits half finished for twenty minutes does the beer no favours.

Where it sits next to altbier

Altbier, from nearby Dusseldorf, follows a broadly similar hybrid brewing logic, top fermenting yeast with cold conditioning, but lands as a darker, maltier, more bitter beer. The rivalry between the two cities and their signature beers is genuinely part of local culture, not just a brewing footnote.

Serve Kolsch cold, 7 to 9°C, and drink it relatively fresh rather than letting a poured glass sit, since its whole appeal is that crisp, immediate character.

For a brewery introducing the style locally, pouring small measures rather than full pints helps make that point about freshness practical rather than just theoretical, keeping every glass close to how it tasted the moment it left the tank rather than twenty minutes later.

A common point of confusion is treating kolsch and altbier as interchangeable because both are top fermented and cold conditioned. They come from the same brewing philosophy but taste distinct: kolsch is pale, delicate and lager like, altbier is darker, maltier and firmer on the hop side. Serving one in the other's small cylindrical glass will not make them the same beer.

Common questions

Is Kolsch an ale or a lager?

Technically an ale, since it uses a top fermenting yeast, but it is fermented cool and then cold conditioned in a way that gives it a much cleaner, more lager like character than a typical ale.

Why is Kolsch only allowed to be called that from certain breweries?

The Kolsch Konvention, a 1986 agreement among Cologne region breweries, legally restricts the name to beer brewed in that area following agreed standards, similar to protected regional food names elsewhere.

Why is Kolsch served in such a small glass?

The traditional small stange glass, around 200ml, keeps the beer fresh throughout drinking, since Kolsch's delicate crispness fades noticeably once it has been poured for a while.

What is the difference between Kolsch and altbier?

Both use a similar hybrid ale fermentation with cold conditioning, but Kolsch is pale, light and mildly bitter, while altbier is darker, maltier and more firmly bitter.
