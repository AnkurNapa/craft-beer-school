Adding a non-alcoholic beer to an existing brewery

A brewery adding a non-alcoholic beer has three routes: limited fermentation, dealcoholisation or a contract partner. Each changes equipment, hygiene and cost.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Why breweries add it

Reasons include meeting demand from drinkers who want a choice, filling a daytime slot and reaching markets where alcohol is restricted or taxed heavily. Check local law first: rules on alcohol-free claims, licences and labels differ by country and change over time. Talk to your national regulator or a licensing adviser before you plan.

Think about positioning too. A non-alcoholic beer must be good enough that people choose it for taste. If it is treated as an afterthought, drinkers notice. Decide who will buy it and where it will be sold before you decide how to make it.

Three routes to a non-alcoholic beer

Limited fermentation. Use a yeast that cannot ferment maltose, or stop fermentation early. Equipment needs are modest. The risk is a worty flavour, because wort sugars and aldehydes remain.

Dealcoholisation. Make normal beer, then remove alcohol by vacuum distillation or membrane filtration. It gives the best beer character but needs specialist kit, which small breweries rarely own. Some breweries send beer to a contract dealcoholiser.

Contract brewing. A partner makes it. This avoids capital cost but gives you less control. Our guide to non-alcoholic beer styles shows what each route tends to taste like.

Whichever route you pick, run a pilot. Brew a small batch, take it through the full process including packaging and test it for flavour, alcohol level and stability over several weeks at the storage temperature your customers will use.

Hygiene is the main risk

Alcohol protects beer from many microbes. Without it and with residual sugar in the package, non-alcoholic beer is far easier to spoil. Brewers usually respond with stricter cleaning, sterile or near-sterile filtration and often pasteurisation. Pasteurisation is measured in pasteurisation units (PU). Your target depends on the beer and equipment.

Keep non-alcoholic production on clean lines where you can. Residual yeast from regular beer in a shared line can restart fermentation in a sealed can.

Package choice matters. Cans protect from light and oxygen well. Bottles need good closures and care with oxygen. Keg is possible, but cleaning of lines for a product without alcohol protection needs strict discipline.

Stability testing is simple in principle. Hold packaged samples warm for several weeks and look for haze, pressure rise, off flavours or yeast growth. A failure in the test is cheap. A failure in the market is not.

Staff training matters as well. Operators need to understand why a shortcut in cleaning that is harmless for regular beer is risky here.

Measuring alcohol accurately

A hydrometer is not enough at low levels. Use a proper alcohol analyser that is validated for low ABV and check against a reference. Measure every batch before release, because a label claim depends on it. Keep the records and verify with an external lab from time to time.

Different countries use different test methods and tolerances. Confirm which method your regulator accepts and keep calibration certificates for the analyser.

Flavour and recipe

Aim for body and balance. Use malts that add body, such as Munich, oat or dextrin malts. Balance them with hop aroma rather than bitterness alone. Carbonation near the upper end helps mouthfeel. Test small pilot batches and taste them next to commercial examples.

Common starting points are a lager or a pale ale, because clean styles are easier to judge. Heavily hopped styles need care: hop aroma shines but harsh bitterness can stand out without alcohol to soften it.

Costs and workflow

Count the full cost: yield loss in dealcoholisation, extra cleaning, lab testing, packaging and contract fees if used. Plan production runs so non-alcoholic beer is made on a clean schedule. Track results on your one-page dashboard so you can see whether the product pays.

Check the supply chain too. Special yeast or enzymes may take weeks to arrive. Hop and malt choices for a non-alcoholic recipe may differ from your regular range.

Common questions

Can I make non-alcoholic beer in my existing brewery?

Yes, using limited fermentation, or by sending beer to a contract dealcoholiser. Hygiene and testing need extra attention.

Is non-alcoholic beer easier to spoil?

Yes. Without alcohol and often with residual sugar, microbes grow more easily, so cleaning and stability treatment matter more.

How do I measure low alcohol accurately?

Use an analyser validated for low ABV and check against a reference lab. A hydrometer is not accurate enough.

What can I call my beer?

Rules vary by country. Check with your regulator or a licensing adviser before printing a label.
