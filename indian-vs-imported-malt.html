Indian malt or imported malt for a craft brewpub

Malt is the biggest ingredient line in your beer after water. Where you buy it changes your recipes, your working capital and how often you run out. Here is how we would make the call before signing the first order.

What you are actually choosing between

Indian maltsters mostly malt barley grown in Rajasthan, Haryana, Punjab, Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh. Much of it was historically six-row barley bred for the big lager brewers, though two-row malting barley is now grown and malted here too. The product range is narrower: pale base malt is easy to find, while a full set of caramel, roasted and specialty malts is not.

Imported malt usually comes from Germany, Belgium, the UK or Australia, through a distributor who brings it in by sea and holds stock in India. You get two-row varieties, tight specifications and every specialty malt a recipe could ask for. You also get import duty, freight, distributor margin and a supply chain measured in months.

For a brewpub making four to eight beers, the question is rarely all of one or all of the other. It is which malts justify the extra cost and lead time.

Quality: read the certificate, not the flag

Good Indian base malt can make clean, bright lager. Poor imported malt that sat in a hot warehouse can make dull beer. Judge each supplier on its certificate of analysis, lot by lot.

The numbers that matter most for a brewpub:

  • Moisture, ideally under 5% when it leaves the maltings. Higher means less extract per kilo paid for and a shorter safe storage life.
  • Extract, which sets how much malt each batch needs.
  • Protein. Six-row and some Indian two-row lots run higher, which can mean more haze and foam-positive protein. Fine for a wheat beer, a nuisance in a pilsner.
  • Friability and homogeneity, which tell you how well the grain was modified and how it will mill and lauter.
  • Colour in EBC, so your pale lager stays pale from lot to lot.

If a supplier cannot send a certificate for every lot, that is your answer, whatever the price.

Landed cost and working capital

The price per kilo on a quote is not your cost. For imported malt, add freight inside India, the distributor's minimum order and the cost of holding more stock because resupply is slow. For Indian malt, check delivery charges, bag size, whether the maltster will sell small lots to a brewpub at all. Ask how quickly they can resupply.

Then work out cost per litre of finished beer, not cost per kilo. A higher-extract malt can close part of the price gap because you use less of it. Run the numbers on your own brewhouse efficiency, which on a small brewpub system is often lower than the recipe books assume.

Lead time is the hidden cost. An importer's container arrives on its schedule, not yours. If your flagship runs on imported pilsner malt and the stock runs dry, you either change the recipe or stop brewing it. Neither is good for a taproom that sells mostly one beer.

Storage changes the answer in India

More stock on hand means more malt sitting through heat and humidity. A sack that picks up moisture loses extract and crispness. In a damp monsoon store it can go musty. If you choose imported malt, budget for proper storage: pallets off the floor, a dry room away from the brewhouse steam, sealed bins for opened sacks and strict first-in-first-out.

Ask your distributor how they store stock. A warehouse with no climate control in May is where good malt goes to age.

A split that works for most brewpubs

Here is the pattern we would start from and then test against your own numbers.

  • Base malt for high-volume beers from a reliable Indian maltster, once you have brewed trial batches and the certificates are consistent. This is where most of your tonnage and cost sit.
  • Imported base malt only where the style depends on it, such as a German pilsner or helles where the malt character is the beer.
  • Imported specialty malts in small quantities: caramel, roasted, Munich, Vienna and wheat. They are a small share of the grist, so the price premium barely moves cost per litre.

Our overview of base malts explains what each type brings, which helps decide where a swap would show.

Run a side-by-side before you commit

Brew the same recipe twice, once on each base malt, with the same water, yeast and schedule. Record mash pH, first wort gravity, brewhouse yield and lauter time. Then taste both beers blind with your team. Many owners find the gap smaller than the price difference suggests on some beers and larger on others.

This is where most opening plans fall short. They pick a supplier on a single quote, without a costed recipe, a stock policy or a reorder point. Getting those three right is the difference between a malt bill you control and one that surprises you every quarter.

Common questions

Is Indian malt good enough for craft beer?

Good Indian base malt makes clean, bright beer. Judge each lot on its certificate of analysis and a trial brew rather than on where it was malted.

Why do brewpubs import specialty malt?

The domestic range of caramel, roasted and specialty malts is limited. These malts are a small share of the grist, so importing them adds little to cost per litre.

How much malt stock should a brewpub hold?

Enough to cover the resupply lead time plus a safety margin. Imported malt needs more stock on hand, which means more storage space and more cash tied up.

What is the biggest risk with imported malt?

Running out. Container schedules are slow and a flagship built on one imported malt can stop when the stock does.
