A homebrewing starter kit for India

India's heat, hard borewell water and patchy cold storage change a few equipment choices from the standard Western starter list. This is what we tell our own first-batch students to buy, and what to skip.

4 min read · Updated December 2021 · Craft Beer School

  2. A hydrometer, and why it is not optional

The core fermentation kit

A food-grade fermenting bucket or a plastic carboy with an airlock is the one piece of equipment you cannot brew without. Buy two if your budget allows, since having a second vessel free means you are not stuck when a batch needs more conditioning time.

Add an auto-siphon or a length of food-grade tubing for racking beer off the sediment, a long spoon for stirring, and a thermometer you trust. Most of the mistakes we see in a first batch trace back to skipping the thermometer, which we go through in ten mistakes almost every first homebrew makes.

A large stainless steel or aluminium pot, sized well beyond your batch volume to leave room for a vigorous boil without boiling over, rounds out the essentials. Most extract kits need only a 10 to 15 litre pot for a partial boil, though a full-volume boil pot in the 25 to 30 litre range gives you more flexibility as you progress.

A hydrometer, and why it is not optional

A hydrometer measures the dissolved sugar in your wort and, later, your beer, and it is the only honest way to know whether fermentation has actually finished. Taste and smell are useful but they will not tell you the gravity has stopped dropping.

We cover proper technique, including temperature correction, in how to use a hydrometer properly. Buy one on day one, not after your first stuck fermentation.

A test jar tall enough to fully submerge the hydrometer, and a turkey baster or wine thief to draw a clean sample without disturbing the whole batch, are small, inexpensive additions that make hydrometer readings far less fiddly than trying to manage with kitchen glassware not built for the job.

Some starter kits bundle a hydrometer with a cheap plastic test jar that cracks or clouds within a few batches. If yours does, replacing just the jar is a small cost worth paying, since a cloudy jar makes an already fiddly reading harder to take accurately.

Sanitiser, not just a bottle brush

Cleaning removes visible grime. Sanitising kills the microbes you cannot see, and skipping it is the single fastest way to ruin a batch in Indian humidity, which favours mould and wild yeast in anything left even slightly damp. A no-rinse acid sanitiser such as Star San is worth sourcing before your first brew day. The difference between the two jobs is explained fully in cleaning versus sanitising in brewing.

A dedicated brewing bucket and utensils kept entirely separate from kitchen use is worth the small extra cost too, since scratches from general kitchen scrubbing, or lingering traces of dish soap and its fragrance, both work against the sanitation habits described in cleaning versus sanitising in brewing.

Heating and cooling, the India-specific problem

Ale fermentation wants a fairly steady 18 to 22 °C. Across most of India for most of the year, ambient room temperature runs well above that, especially after March. An old refrigerator with a plug-in temperature controller, sometimes called an STC, is the most reliable fix we know, and it costs far less than replacing a batch that fermented too hot and came out with harsh, solvent-like flavours. We go into this properly in controlling fermentation temperature at home.

Bottling equipment, a capper and a bag of crown caps, or a set of reusable swing-top bottles, is the other piece worth buying before brew day rather than scrambling for afterwards. A grain bag borrowed from a friend or a length of muslin cloth can substitute in a pinch for straining hops, but a proper capper is difficult to improvise well.

What to buy later, not now

A grain mill, a dedicated mash tun, a wort chiller and a kegging setup all make later batches easier, but none of them will improve a first extract batch enough to justify the outlay before you know you enjoy the hobby. Start with the fermentation kit, the hydrometer and reliable temperature control, brew two or three batches, and let what frustrated you decide what you buy next.

A grain mill matters more than it might seem, since most homebrew shops in India sell malt pre-crushed to a generic setting rather than milled to your own recipe's needs, and a personal mill lets you dial in crush consistency once you are troubleshooting stuck mashes or low efficiency on a regular basis.

Common questions

Can I brew with tap or borewell water without treating it?

Often yes for a first batch, especially with extract kits where the base malt sugars are already balanced. Very hard borewell water can affect flavour and mash chemistry once you move to all-grain, which is worth revisiting then rather than before your first brew.

Do I need a mash tun for my first batch?

No. An extract kit needs no mashing at all, and brew in a bag needs only a large pot. A dedicated mash tun only becomes useful once you are doing regular all-grain batches.

What is the minimum spend to get a real batch of beer?

The fermentation bucket, airlock, hydrometer, sanitiser and basic tools cover the essentials. We deliberately avoid quoting figures here since equipment prices shift, but this core list is small compared with most other hobbies.
