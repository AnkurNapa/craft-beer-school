English bitter

Bitter is the definitive English pub beer, and the name confuses more people today than it did when it was coined, back when almost everything else on offer was sweeter.

3 min read · Updated May 2024 · Craft Beer School

A name that made more sense in its own time

English bitter earned its name in the 1800s largely by contrast with the sweeter, less hopped porters and milds that dominated English pubs at the time, not because it was aggressively bitter by any absolute measure. Compared to a modern IPA, even a strong bitter is a genuinely restrained, balanced beer.

The style split over time into three broad strength tiers, ordinary, best and extra special bitter, or ESB, that still guide how the style is categorised today.

Three tiers, one family

Ordinary bitter sits lowest in strength, roughly 3.2 to 3.8% ABV, a genuinely sessionable everyday pub beer. Best bitter runs a step up, around 3.8 to 4.6% ABV, with a bit more malt depth. Extra special bitter, or ESB, tops the family at 4.6 to 6.2% ABV, fuller bodied and more assertive while keeping the same fundamental balance.

All three share the same essential character, English malt, English hops, a clean ale fermentation, just scaled to different strengths and depths of flavour.

A brewery building a bitter range for the first time often starts with best bitter as the anchor recipe, since it sits comfortably in the middle of the family and is easy to scale up or down once the base recipe is dialled in.

What to expect in the glass

Bitter pours pale gold to copper depending on the tier, with a biscuity, lightly caramel malt aroma and a distinctly earthy, floral hop character from traditional English varieties, generally Fuggles or East Kent Goldings. Bitterness sits at a moderate 25 to 40 IBU, firm but balanced rather than sharp.

Carbonation is notably lower than most other beer styles, especially in its traditional cask conditioned form, giving a smoother, softer mouthfeel that suits long, unhurried drinking.

Cask conditioning: why it matters to the style

Traditional English bitter is often served as cask ale, or real ale, meaning the beer completes a secondary fermentation in the cask it is served from, producing natural, gentle carbonation without added carbon dioxide. It is served at cellar temperature, roughly 11 to 13°C, using a hand pump rather than a pressurised tap, which pulls the beer up rather than pushing it.

Cask ale needs to be looked after properly once tapped, since it has a shorter usable window than a filtered, pasteurised keg beer, and a poorly kept cask is where bitter gets its occasional unfair reputation for tasting flat or stale.

A brewery without the infrastructure or the through-put to run genuine cask ale can still brew a very good bitter in keg form, keeping carbonation deliberately lower than most other keg styles to preserve something closer to the traditional mouthfeel.

The malt and hop balance behind it

English pale ale malt forms the base, often with a modest proportion of crystal malt for colour and a light caramel note, kept restrained enough that malt and hops stay in genuine balance rather than either dominating. English hop varieties contribute an earthy, floral, sometimes slightly minty character quite different from the citrus and resin of American hop varieties.

An English ale yeast strain, fermented around 18 to 20°C, adds a light fruity ester that rounds out the malt and hop balance without adding noticeable sweetness.

Where it sits next to mild

Against English mild, bitter is hoppier, generally paler and usually a touch stronger, while mild leans further into malt with much lower hop character. The two styles developed alongside each other in English pubs and remain a useful pair for understanding how much hop rate alone changes a beer built on a similar malt foundation.

Serve bitter at cellar temperature if cask conditioned, or slightly cooler for a bottled or kegged version, in a straight pint glass or a dimpled mug if you want the full traditional pub experience.

Once you understand the ordinary, best and ESB tiers well enough to identify them blind, moving on to compare English hop varieties directly against American ones is a genuinely useful next step in training your palate for style work generally.

A common mistake outside Britain is serving bitter too cold, straight from a fridge set for lager. At that temperature its malt and hop balance simply switches off. Serve it closer to cellar temperature, around 11 to 13 degrees Celsius, and the beer opens up properly.

Common questions

Why is bitter called bitter if it is not that bitter?

The name developed in contrast to the sweeter porters and milds common in English pubs at the time it emerged, not because it is aggressively bitter by modern standards.

What is the difference between ordinary, best and ESB?

They represent increasing strength and malt depth within the same family, roughly 3.2 to 3.8% ABV for ordinary, 3.8 to 4.6% for best, and 4.6 to 6.2% for extra special bitter.

Why is cask conditioned bitter served warmer than most beer?

Cellar temperature, around 11 to 13°C, lets the beer's malt and hop character come through fully. Serving it colder, as with many other styles, would mute those flavours.

Does bitter need to be cask conditioned to be authentic?

Cask conditioning is the traditional serving method and affects carbonation and mouthfeel, but bottled and kegged versions of bitter are also common and can be well made.
