Bock and doppelbock

Bock and its stronger sibling doppelbock are proof that a lager can be built for intensity rather than crispness. Here is what separates the two and how each is brewed.

3 min read · Updated August 2022 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

A style built around strength, not subtlety

Bock is a strong, malty German lager, generally 6.3 to 7.2% ABV, with a rich, warming character that sets it apart from the crisp, session strength lagers most people associate with German brewing. Doppelbock, meaning double bock, pushes further still, commonly 7 to 10% ABV, and traditionally carries a name ending in the suffix -ator, a nod to the original Salvator brewed by Paulaner monks in Munich.

The Lenten fasting story behind doppelbock, that monks brewed it as liquid bread to sustain themselves through a period of restricted eating, is part of local brewing lore. Treat it as tradition rather than a documented fact, but the beer's substantial malt character certainly supports the idea.

What to expect in the glass

Bock pours deep amber to brown, with a rich, bready, sometimes toffee like malt aroma and minimal hop presence, generally 20 to 27 IBU, present only to keep the sweetness from becoming cloying. Doppelbock goes further still, deeper in colour, more intensely malty, with dried fruit, caramel and sometimes a light dark chocolate note in stronger versions.

Neither style should taste hot from alcohol despite the strength. A well fermented bock or doppelbock hides its ABV behind a smooth, warming malt character rather than a boozy edge.

A noticeable alcohol burn on the finish is usually a sign of yeast stress during fermentation rather than an inherent feature of the style, however strong the beer is meant to be.

Building enough malt to carry that strength

A large proportion of Munich malt provides the deep, bready backbone both styles need, often supplemented with a touch of caramel or dark malt for colour and complexity, though restraint still matters since this should read as malty rather than roasted. Reaching the required starting gravity, often well above 1.070 for doppelbock, usually needs a longer boil or a partial second mash to extract enough sugar.

This is a genuinely demanding brew day. A standard single infusion mash and boil schedule sized for an everyday lager will not comfortably reach doppelbock strength without adjustment.

Fermentation has to work harder too

A healthy, well pitched lager yeast at 9 to 13°C is essential here, since a beer this strong stresses yeast more than a standard gravity fermentation, and underpitching risks stalled fermentation, excess diacetyl, or unwanted fusel alcohols. A larger yeast pitch than usual, and patient, unhurried fermentation, both matter more here than in a session strength lager.

Extended lagering afterward, often two to three months for a genuine doppelbock, gives the beer time to mellow and integrate its alcohol into the malt character rather than sitting apart from it.

Bock's other variations

Maibock, or Helles bock, is a paler, hoppier variation traditionally brewed for spring celebrations, trading some of bock's dark malt depth for a crisper, more golden character while keeping the strength. Eisbock takes the opposite direction, partially freezing a finished doppelbock and removing the ice to concentrate flavour and alcohol into something stronger still.

These variations show how flexible the underlying strong lager idea actually is, even though standard bock and doppelbock remain the most widely brewed versions.

Eisbock in particular is a genuinely demanding style to attempt, since freeze concentration needs careful temperature control and specialist equipment most small breweries do not have on hand, which is part of why it remains rarer than the other variations even among dedicated lager brewers.

Serving and pairing

Serve at 10 to 13°C, warmer than a pale lager, in a stein or tulip glass that lets the aroma develop. Doppelbock in particular suits rich, dark food, roasted meats, aged cheese, even dessert, treated almost like a dessert wine rather than an everyday beer.

Against Munich Dunkel, bock and doppelbock are noticeably stronger and more intensely malty, sharing a family resemblance in colour without the same session drinking intent.

Pour it in a smaller measure than you would a session lager. A full pint of doppelbock is a genuinely different commitment than a full pint of Helles, and most drinkers appreciate being served accordingly rather than finishing a large glass out of habit.

Common questions

Why do doppelbock names end in -ator?

It traces back to Salvator, the original doppelbock brewed by Paulaner monks in Munich. Many breweries later adopted a similar -ator suffix for their own versions as a nod to that tradition.

Is bock always dark?

No. Maibock, or Helles bock, is a paler, more golden variation, though standard bock and doppelbock are usually amber to dark brown.

Why does doppelbock need such a long fermentation and lagering time?

Its high starting gravity stresses yeast more than a standard lager, and extended cold lagering afterward lets the alcohol integrate smoothly into the malt character rather than tasting sharp or hot.

What food goes well with doppelbock?

Rich, dark food suits it well, roasted or braised meats, aged cheese, even chocolate desserts, since its malt sweetness and warmth can stand up to strong flavours.
