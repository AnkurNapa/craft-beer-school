Noble hops and the European classics

Hallertau, Tettnang, Saaz and Spalt are the four hops that shaped what a European lager is supposed to smell like. Low in alpha acid and high in tradition, they are still the benchmark for the style.

3 min read · Updated October 2023 · Craft Beer School

What makes a hop noble

"Noble" is not a botanical classification, it is a traditional grouping of four European hop varieties, Hallertau, Tettnang, Saaz and Spalt, all low in alpha acid (typically 3 to 6 percent) and low in the cohumulone fraction of that alpha acid, which is associated with a harsher, coarser bitterness. The result is a soft, refined bitterness paired with a spicy, herbal, sometimes floral aroma rather than the citrus or pine of American hops covered in our American hops guide.

Hallertau and Saaz: the two most widely used

Hallertau, grown in Germany's Hallertau region, is mild, herbal and slightly spicy, and remains the default hop in a huge share of German lagers. Saaz, grown around Žatec in the Czech Republic, is more distinctly spicy and slightly earthy, and is inseparable from Czech pilsner, where it defines the style's characteristic dry, peppery hop character. Both are used across the boil, for bitterness early and aroma late, unlike the split scheduling common with higher alpha American hops.

Tettnang and Spalt: the quieter two

Tettnang, from the Lake Constance region of Germany, is similar to Hallertau but slightly more floral and delicate. Spalt, one of the oldest documented hop growing regions in the world, is mild and subtly spicy, used more sparingly today than the other three but still present in traditional German styles that want a gentler hop presence than Hallertau provides.

Why noble hops need generous quantities to register

Because their alpha acid content is low, noble hops need to be used in larger weights than a high alpha American hop to hit the same IBU, following the isomerisation chemistry covered in our guide to alpha acids. This is not a drawback so much as part of the style: a generous amount of a low alpha, refined hop delivers the soft, rounded bitterness and full aromatic character that a small amount of a high alpha hop cannot replicate, however carefully the timing is managed.

Where noble hops still belong

Any classic German or Czech lager, pilsner, helles, marzen, dunkel, expects a noble hop, and substituting a citrus-forward American variety changes the beer's character completely rather than merely adjusting it. Outside their traditional home, noble hops also work well in some Belgian styles and in any beer where a spicy, herbal hop character is wanted without the fruit-forward profile of the New Zealand and Australian hops now common in modern craft brewing.

Why noble hops are grown where they are

Each of the four noble hop regions, Hallertau and Spalt in Bavaria, Tettnang near Lake Constance, and Žatec in Bohemia, has centuries of documented cultivation behind it, and the varieties grown there today are closely tied to those specific growing conditions, soil and climate as much as to the seed stock itself. Attempts to grow noble hop varieties elsewhere in the world generally produce a recognisably similar but not identical aroma profile, which is why serious lager brewers pay attention to a noble hop's actual growing region printed on the package, not just its variety name.

Noble hop blends in commercial lager brewing

Many commercial German and Czech lagers use a blend of two or more noble hops rather than a single variety, balancing Hallertau's mild herbal character against Saaz's sharper spiciness, for example, in the same way modern American IPAs blend Citra and Simcoe from our American hops guide to cover different parts of an aroma profile that no single hop reaches alone.

If genuine noble hops are difficult to source reliably in India, some newer varieties bred specifically to approximate noble character, sometimes marketed as noble-type hops, can serve as a reasonable substitute, though a serious pilsner brewer chasing an authentic result will usually still seek out the genuine regional varieties when supply allows.

Price is worth factoring into a decision here too: genuine estate-grown noble hops from their traditional regions typically cost more than an equivalent weight of a high alpha American hop, reflecting both lower per-acre alpha yield and the smaller specialised growing regions involved, a cost worth planning for rather than discovering partway through sourcing ingredients for a lager recipe.

A useful way to learn the family is tasting a beer built almost entirely around one noble hop, a simple German pilsner using only Hallertau, for example, before trying a version built around Saaz instead. The side by side comparison teaches the difference between them far faster than reading a description of each one in isolation.

Common questions

Are noble hops always low in alpha acid?

Yes, by definition. Typically 3 to 6 percent alpha acid, which is why they are used in larger quantities than high alpha American or New World hops to reach the same bitterness.

Can I substitute Cascade for Saaz in a pilsner recipe?

You can, but it will taste like a different beer. Cascade's citrus and floral character is nothing like Saaz's spicy, earthy profile, and the substitution changes the style rather than approximating it.

Why is cohumulone content mentioned with noble hops?

Lower cohumulone is associated with a softer, more refined bitterness rather than a harsh one, and noble hops are naturally low in it, which contributes to their reputation for smooth bitterness.
