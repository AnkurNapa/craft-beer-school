Choosing bottles and crown caps or swing tops for homebrew in India

Bottling is where most homebrewers in India start. The choice of glass and closure decides how your beer carbonates and keeps. Here is what to collect, what to buy and what to throw away.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

What a homebrew bottle has to do

A bottle of homebrew is a small pressure vessel. When you bottle condition, the yeast eats a dose of priming sugar and makes carbon dioxide inside a sealed bottle. A normal pale ale sits around 2.3 to 2.7 volumes of CO2. The bottle has to hold that pressure, seal tightly and keep light out.

That rules out a lot of glass. Wine bottles, spirits bottles and most fancy juice bottles are not built for pressure. Some will fail.

The bottles to collect

India gives homebrewers one big advantage: beer bottles are everywhere.

  • 650 ml bottles are the standard Indian large bottle. They are thick, take a standard crown cap and are easy to find. One 20 litre batch fills about 30.
  • 330 ml bottles suit stronger beers and sharing. Imported beer bottles in this size are usually fine if they are not twist-off.
  • 500 ml bottles are a good middle ground, often from imported wheat beers.

Brown glass is best. Green and clear glass let light through. Light reacts with hop compounds to give a skunky smell within minutes. Our guide to lightstruck beer explains why. If you use green bottles, keep them in a closed box.

The bottles to avoid

  • Twist-off bottles. The threaded neck does not seal well under a standard crown cap.
  • Badly scuffed returnable bottles. Large Indian brewers reuse their bottles, so many have a white scuffed band. Light scuffing is cosmetic. Deep chips at the neck or a scratched lip are not.
  • Anything with a chipped lip. The cap cannot seal on it.
  • Thin decorative bottles with no beer history.

Run your finger round every lip before you fill. It takes seconds.

How many to gather

For a 20 litre batch, plan on about 19 litres actually reaching bottles once you leave sediment behind. That is roughly 29 bottles of 650 ml or 57 of 330 ml. Collect a dozen more than you need. A few will fail inspection and you will always lose one or two to a chipped lip found mid-fill.

Buy caps by the hundred rather than the bag of fifty. They are cheap. Running out halfway through bottling with an open bucket of primed beer is a bad afternoon. Mixing sizes in one batch is fine, though small and large bottles carbonate at slightly different speeds in the first week.

Crown caps and cappers

Standard crown caps for beer bottles are 26 mm. Oxygen-absorbing caps, with a liner that scavenges a little oxygen from the headspace, cost slightly more and help hoppy beers stay fresh longer. Store caps sealed and dry. In a humid monsoon they rust surprisingly quickly.

You need a capper:

  • Two-handed winged cappers are cheap and portable. They work, but they can crimp unevenly on bottles with slightly different necks.
  • Bench cappers cost more, adjust for height and crimp more consistently. If you plan to bottle often, buy one.

A good crimp is even all round. Twist the cap gently with your fingers. If it moves, recap it.

When swing tops make sense

Swing-top bottles, with a ceramic or plastic stopper on a wire bail, need no capper. They reseal, look good on a table and are easy for friends to open.

The weak point is the rubber gasket. Gaskets harden and crack with age and with heat. Replace them every few batches and keep a spare bag. Clean under the stopper carefully, because residue hides in the hinge. They are also heavier and more expensive than a box of reused 650s.

Swing tops are also the closure most likely to be forced open by curious guests and left half shut, so tell people to press the bail down firmly after pouring a sample.

Many homebrewers end up with both: crown caps for most of a batch and a few swing tops for gifts.

Prime it right whatever you choose

The bottle is only as safe as the priming. Too much sugar, or bottling before fermentation has finished, creates more pressure than any bottle should hold. Check that gravity is stable for two or three days before you bottle. Weigh priming sugar rather than guessing. Our guide to bottle conditioning and priming sugar gives the amounts.

Bottles, caps and a capper are a small investment. Turning them into beer that pours with a steady head every time comes from understanding fermentation, priming and conditioning. That is the part worth learning properly.

Common questions

Can I reuse Indian 650 ml beer bottles for homebrew?

Yes. They are thick, take a standard 26 mm crown cap and are ideal for homebrew, provided the lip is not chipped.

Are swing-top bottles better than crown caps?

Not better, just different. They need no capper and reseal, but the gaskets wear out and need replacing every few batches.

Why should I avoid twist-off bottles?

The threaded neck does not give a reliable seal under a crown cap, so the beer may go flat or leak.

Do oxygen-absorbing caps make a difference?

They help a little, mostly for hoppy beers that you plan to keep for more than a few weeks.
