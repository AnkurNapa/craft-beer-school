How to set up a beer tasting

A good tasting is not about having rare beer. It is about setting the room up so everyone actually notices what they are drinking.

4 min read · Updated November 2021 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

On this page

  1. Start with the guest list, not the beer list
  2. Pick an order that respects the palate
  3. Set the table so people can actually taste
  4. Give people a structure, not a lecture
  5. Read labels before you pour, not after
  6. Where this leads if you want to go further

Start with the guest list, not the beer list

Four to six people is the sweet spot for a home tasting. Fewer and the conversation runs dry, because you need enough different opinions in the room to make comparing notes interesting. More than six and half the group is talking about something else by the third beer, and the person leading the tasting spends more time managing the room than the beer.

Ask everyone what they usually drink before you plan anything. A table of lager drinkers tasting five imperial stouts back to back will learn nothing except that their palates are tired by beer three. Mix familiar styles with one or two that stretch the group, rather than five unfamiliar styles at once, which overwhelms rather than educates.

It also helps to tell people in advance roughly what is being poured, hoppy, malty, sour, dark, so nobody spends the first beer recalibrating their expectations instead of actually tasting. A short heads up by message the day before does most of that work for you.

Pick an order that respects the palate

Light to dark, low bitterness to high, low alcohol to high. That is the whole rule, and it holds for almost any lineup you can put together.

A hoppy IPA poured before a delicate pilsner will flatten the pilsner completely, because bitterness and strong aroma linger on the palate and reset what you can perceive in whatever comes next. A heavy stout poured early does the same thing to every beer that follows it, coating the tongue with roasted malt character for the rest of the evening.

If you are unsure where a beer sits in the order, taste it yourself an hour before the guests arrive and slot it in by feel rather than guessing from the label alone. See our glassware guide for which shape suits which style, and pour at the right serving temperature for each one rather than serving everything fridge cold, which flattens aroma across the board.

Set the table so people can actually taste

Water and plain crackers between beers, nothing scented in the room, and good light so people can see the colour and the head clearly. A candle burning on the table, or someone's perfume, will fight every aroma you are trying to teach people to notice, and most hosts never think to check for this.

Pour small measures, 60 to 90 ml is enough to judge a beer properly, and it means six beers do not turn into six pints by the end of the evening, which defeats the point of a tasting entirely.

Room temperature matters too. A stuffy, overheated room speeds up how fast beer warms in the glass and tires the nose faster than a cool, well ventilated one, so open a window or run a fan quietly in the background rather than relying on air conditioning alone, which can dry out the air and blunt aroma over a long evening.

Give people a structure, not a lecture

Look, smell, taste, and a one line note on each. That is enough structure for a home group without turning the evening into an exam nobody signed up for.

Ask two questions per beer: what does it remind you of, and would you order a second one. Both get quieter drinkers talking without demanding technical vocabulary they do not have yet, and both questions genuinely reveal something useful about how the beer is landing with the group.

Encourage people to write their note before hearing anyone else's opinion out loud. Group tastings drift quickly towards whatever the most confident person in the room says first, and a written note taken independently protects everyone's honest first impression before the discussion starts.

Read labels before you pour, not after

A minute with the label tells the room the style, the ABV and sometimes the brewing date, which sets expectations before the first sip and gives people a frame of reference for what they are about to taste. See our guide on reading a beer label if the printing on Indian craft cans still looks like a puzzle, since a lot of useful information is often squeezed into a small corner.

Where this leads if you want to go further

A home tasting teaches you to notice differences between beers sitting side by side. Naming what you are noticing, in the vocabulary that judges and brewers actually use, is a separate skill, and it is the one that turns a fun evening into something you can use professionally, whether that means judging competitions or working the floor of a brewery.

Common questions

How many beers should a beginner tasting include?

Four to six is plenty. More than that and palate fatigue sets in, and the later beers get less attention than they deserve.

Should I serve food during a tasting?

Keep it plain, bread or plain crackers, and save anything with strong flavour for after the tasting is done, so it does not compete with the beer.

Does glass shape actually matter for a casual tasting?

Yes, more than most people expect. A narrow glass concentrates aroma at the nose, which changes what you notice even in the same beer.

What is the ideal group size?

Four to six people. It is small enough that everyone gets heard and large enough that you get a real range of opinions on each beer.
