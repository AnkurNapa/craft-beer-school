Launching a new beer

A beer launch is not the day the tank finishes fermenting. It is a sequence of decisions made weeks earlier, most of which have nothing to do with brewing.

3 min read · Updated July 2023 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. Set the date backward from distribution, not forward from the tank
  2. Packaging decisions cannot be last minute
  3. Tell your own channels before anyone else
  4. The launch event is where the story lands
  5. Watch the first batch closely
  6. After the first weekend

Set the date backward from distribution, not forward from the tank

Brewers naturally think about launch day as the day the beer is ready. Distributors and retailers think about it as the day stock is on a shelf they can sell from. If you announce a launch date before confirming when the beer can actually be in front of a customer, you create a gap that competitors and impatient followers will notice.

Work backward from the date beer needs to be on shelf or in the taproom, and build fermentation, packaging and logistics timelines against that, with a buffer for the inevitable delay somewhere in the chain. Cold chain transport across Indian states adds its own uncertainty, so build in extra days for anything travelling beyond your home city, especially through the hotter summer months when a delayed truck matters even more.

Packaging decisions cannot be last minute

Can and label artwork, print runs and any new packaging format all have lead times measured in weeks, not days, and Indian can and label suppliers are no exception. If the new beer needs a new can design, start that conversation the same week you finalise the recipe, not after the beer is already in the fermenter. See our packaging costs guide for what drives that timeline and cost.

Confirm excise approval for new labels early too. Label approval in most states takes longer than brewers expect, and it is one of the few launch dependencies entirely outside your control, so a rushed application at the last minute is the single most common reason a launch date slips.

Tell your own channels before anyone else

Your taproom staff, your social media followers and your existing distributors should hear about a new beer before the general public does, not after. Staff who find out about a launch from a customer look unprepared, and distributors who feel like an afterthought respond accordingly on reorders.

A short internal briefing, even a single message with the beer's story, style and how it fits alongside your existing range, prevents most of this. Give staff the chance to taste it before launch day so they can answer questions confidently at the bar.

The launch event is where the story lands

A new beer without an event is just new stock. A launch event, even a modest one in the taproom on a Friday evening, gives the beer a moment and gives your regulars a reason to be first. Our taproom events guide covers how to run one that people actually return for, rather than a one-off with free samples and nothing to bring people back.

Invite the people who already show up for your other events before opening it to the wider public. Regulars talk about a launch to friends more convincingly than any post you write yourself, and they forgive a rough edge that a stranger would not.

Watch the first batch closely

The first commercial batch of a new recipe is also the first real test of it at scale, after however many smaller test batches came before. Taste it against your original target before it reaches a customer, and be honest about whether it matches. A launch date is not a reason to ship a beer that missed the brief.

If the batch is close but not quite right, it is often better to hold the date and communicate a short delay than to launch something you would not personally reorder. A weak first impression is far harder to undo than a delayed one.

After the first weekend

Track sell-through in the taproom and any early retail placements in the first two weeks, not just the launch weekend itself. A beer that sells out on launch night and then stalls tells you something different from one that builds steadily, and that difference should shape whether you brew it again, adjust it, or fold it into the seasonal rotation.

Our guide to seasonal and limited releases is useful here if the beer was never meant to be permanent, and it will save you from repeating a launch cycle for something that was always meant to be a one-off.

Common questions

How far ahead should we plan a launch?

For a new can design or label, start artwork and print conversations at least four to six weeks out. For an existing packaging format with a new recipe inside it, two to three weeks is often enough.

Should we launch quietly or with an event?

An event, even a small one, gives the beer a specific moment to be remembered by rather than blending into normal stock. It does not need to be large to work.

What if the first batch does not match the recipe target?

Do not ship it to meet the date. Adjust the launch date or hold the batch back for the taproom only while the next batch is corrected.
