Legs and tears in a whisky glass

Legs, tears, curtains: whisky drinkers have a word for the streaks that slide down the glass after a swirl. They are fascinating to watch. They are also one of the most over-read signs in whisky.

What you are actually seeing

Swirl a whisky in a glass and a thin film coats the inside. Within seconds, a ridge forms near the top of that film. Droplets gather along it, grow heavy and run back down in streaks. Those streaks are the legs or tears.

Wine drinkers know them too. The effect is the same in any mix of alcohol and water. Whisky shows it strongly because it is so much stronger than wine.

The physics behind the tears

The effect is named after the Italian physicist Carlo Marangoni, who studied it in the nineteenth century. It works like this.

Alcohol evaporates faster than water. In the thin film on the glass, alcohol escapes quickly, so the film becomes more watery than the whisky below it. Water has a higher surface tension than an alcohol and water mix. That stronger surface tension pulls more liquid up the glass from the bowl. The liquid piles up into a ridge until gravity wins and it falls back as drops.

So tears are a sign that two liquids with different evaporation rates and surface tensions are mixed together. In whisky, those liquids are ethanol and water.

What legs can tell you

The main thing legs track is alcohol strength. A cask strength whisky at 58% will usually show thicker, more persistent tears than the same whisky reduced to 40%. Add a splash of water to your glass and swirl again; the legs change.

They can hint at texture too. Whiskies with more dissolved compounds can show slower, oilier looking legs. Some drinkers read slow legs as a sign of an older or richer whisky. There is a grain of truth in that, but strength and temperature have a much bigger effect.

What legs cannot tell you

Legs say nothing reliable about quality, age or flavour. A young, cheap whisky at high strength can show beautiful tears. A superb old whisky at 40% may show modest ones.

The glass matters as much as the liquid. Detergent residue, dust or a towel that left lint can all break up the film and change how legs form. Temperature matters too: warm whisky evaporates faster. In an Indian summer at 32 °C, the same whisky will behave differently from a cool air conditioned room.

Some bars spin the glass and talk at length about the legs. Enjoy the show, then judge the whisky with your nose and mouth.

Use the swirl for aroma instead

The swirl has a better use than producing legs. It coats the glass and lifts aroma into the headspace above the whisky. Use a tulip shaped nosing glass that narrows at the top so the aroma gathers. Our whisky glassware guide explains why shape matters.

Swirl gently, then bring the glass to your nose slowly with your mouth slightly open. Strong spirit can sting the nose if you plunge in. Short sniffs at a little distance give a clearer read than one deep breath. Our guide to nosing whisky properly walks through it step by step.

Legs in beer, wine and whisky compared

Brewers rarely talk about legs because beer at 4 to 6% ABV barely shows them. A strong barley wine at 10% or more may show faint tears. Wine at 12 to 15% shows them clearly, which is why wine drinkers argue about them so much. Whisky at 40% and above shows them strongly. Cask strength whisky shows them most of all.

That sliding scale is the best proof that legs follow alcohol. The more ethanol there is to evaporate, the stronger the surface tension difference and the more liquid gets pulled up the glass. Sugar and glycerol in sweet wines can slow the drops, which is where the idea of legs as a sign of body comes from. Whisky has no residual sugar to speak of, so in whisky the story is almost entirely about strength.

Why we over-read visual cues

People trust what they can see. Colour and legs are visible. Aroma and texture take attention and practice to describe. So beginners lean on the visual and treat it as evidence.

Trained tasters do the opposite. They glance at the glass, note colour and clarity, then spend nearly all their time on nose and palate. That shift, from looking to smelling with intent, is the biggest single step in learning to taste.

Your first step

Pour 15 ml of a cask strength whisky and 15 ml of a 40% whisky into identical glasses. Swirl both and watch the tears. Then add water to the strong one until the legs look similar. Taste both. You will see in one evening that legs track strength while flavour is a separate question.

Common questions

What are legs in whisky?

Legs or tears are the streaks of liquid that run down the inside of a glass after swirling. They form because alcohol evaporates faster than water.

Do whisky legs show quality?

No. They mainly reflect alcohol strength and temperature. A cheap strong whisky can show thick legs and a fine 40% whisky thin ones.

Why do cask strength whiskies have thicker legs?

Higher alcohol creates a bigger difference in surface tension as the alcohol evaporates, which pulls more liquid up the glass.
