Why homebrew bottles gush and how to avoid bottle bombs

When a homebrew bottle foams out of its neck the moment you open it, something produced more carbon dioxide than you planned. Left alone, the same problem can burst bottles. Here is how to find the cause and what to do with a batch that is going wrong.

Gushing means too much carbon dioxide

Every bottle-conditioned beer carries a planned amount of carbon dioxide, usually 2 to 3 volumes. A gusher holds more than that, or holds it in a way that releases all at once. Glass beer bottles are built to hold the pressure of normal carbonation with a good safety margin. Push well past it and the glass can fail. A bottle bomb sends sharp fragments across a room, so treat a gushing batch as a safety issue, not just a messy one.

The causes fall into four groups. Two are about sugar: too much left in the beer at bottling or too much added as priming. One is about microbes. The last is about temperature and the bottle itself. Each leaves different clues, so the way the batch behaves tells you where to look.

Cause one: bottling before fermentation finished

This is the most common cause by far. If the beer still had a few gravity points left when you bottled, that sugar ferments in the bottle on top of the priming sugar. Just a few extra points of gravity can add more carbon dioxide than the priming sugar itself.

The fix is a habit. Take gravity readings on three separate days. Only bottle when all three match. Do not trust the airlock or the calendar. Some beers, especially high gravity ones and those with dry hops, keep dropping slowly for days after they look finished.

Cause two: too much priming sugar

Over-priming is the next culprit. Common mistakes include using a priming figure meant for a larger batch, guessing sugar by volume rather than weight or putting two carbonation drops in a 330 ml bottle. Uneven mixing in a bottling bucket can also leave some bottles with far more sugar than others. That shows up as a batch where a few bottles gush and the rest are fine.

Weigh priming sugar on a kitchen scale. Use a priming calculator with your actual volume. Stir syrup in gently but thoroughly. Our guide comparing bottles, crown caps and swing tops also explains which bottles are strong enough for higher carbonation.

Cause three: infection

If a batch was fine at first and then gets more carbonated week after week, suspect an infection. Wild yeast, especially diastatic strains, can ferment the dextrins that normal brewing yeast leaves behind. Some bacteria do the same. The beer slowly dries out, carbonation climbs and the flavour often turns thin, phenolic or sour.

The source is usually bottles or kit that were not clean enough. Old bottles with dried residue are a common hiding place. Our guide to cleaning and reusing old beer bottles covers the routine.

Cause four: heat and nucleation

Warm beer holds less dissolved carbon dioxide. Open a bottle at 30 °C and the gas comes out of solution fast. Bottles stored in an Indian summer can gush when opened warm even when the carbonation level is correct. Chill them for a day and they pour normally.

Rough particles also release gas suddenly. Dirty bottles, bits of hop matter or a scratched glass give carbon dioxide somewhere to form bubbles all at once. This kind of gushing is usually mild and inconsistent.

What to do with a batch that is overcarbonating

Act early. If one bottle gushes, assume the rest are building pressure too.

  1. Move the whole batch into a fridge straight away. Cold slows the yeast almost to a stop and keeps more carbon dioxide dissolved.
  2. Store bottles in a closed crate or a lidded box until they are cold. Wear eye protection and gloves when you handle them.
  3. Once cold, crack each cap briefly to release pressure, then recap with a fresh cap.
  4. If the cause is infection, drink the batch soon or discard it. Venting will not stop it.

Always keep a few of each batch in a PET bottle. A firm plastic bottle tells you when carbonation is done. One that turns rock hard tells you something is wrong before any glass is at risk. Good habits like labelling and dating your bottles help you trace which batch is the problem.

Your first step: buy a 500 ml PET soft drink bottle, rinse and sanitise it and fill it alongside the glass on your next bottling day. Squeeze it every few days.

Common questions

Why are only some of my bottles gushing?

Uneven priming is the likely cause, usually from sugar syrup that was not stirred evenly through the bottling bucket.

How can I tell if gushing is caused by infection?

Infected bottles get more carbonated over time and often taste thin, sour or phenolic. Gushing from over-priming appears early and stays the same.

Can a homebrew bottle really explode?

Yes. Heavily overcarbonated glass can burst. Chill suspect bottles, store them in a closed box and wear eye protection when handling them.
