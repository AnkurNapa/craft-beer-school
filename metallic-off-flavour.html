Metallic off-flavours

Metallic character is one of the easier faults to trace, because it almost always comes from a piece of equipment rather than the recipe. Once you know where to look, the source is usually obvious.

3 min read · Updated January 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

  2. Why this fault matters more than it seems
  3. Corroded or worn equipment is the usual cause

What metallic beer tastes like

Drinkers usually reach for blood, iron, or old coins to describe it. It often sits at the back of the mouth rather than the front, and it can be genuinely unpleasant even at low levels, unlike some faults that only become obvious once they build up.

It is also one of the faults most likely to be blamed on the wrong thing, because a drinker will often assume it is the beer's fault rather than the vessel it was poured into.

Some people are also simply more sensitive to trace metal than others, in the same way some tasters pick up diacetyl far more readily than the average drinker. A beer that one person calls faintly metallic can taste entirely clean to someone else at the same table.

Why this fault matters more than it seems

A metallic note is small on paper, a few parts per million of iron or copper, but it changes how everything else in the beer reads. Hop bitterness can start to taste harsher and less rounded, and malt sweetness can pick up a slightly sharp edge, even though neither ingredient actually changed.

This is why experienced brewers treat even a faint metallic note seriously rather than dismissing it as too small to matter. By the time a panel agrees they can taste it clearly, the underlying corrosion or contamination has usually been building for a while.

Corroded or worn equipment is the usual cause

Iron picks up readily in beer, and it takes very little to be noticeable. Ageing stainless steel with worn or scratched surfaces, an old aluminium keg coupler that has started to corrode, or a poorly maintained tap line can all shed trace metal into the beer as it passes through.

Cheap or old draught fittings are a common offender in bars, since they see constant contact with beer and cleaning chemicals and wear faster than tank surfaces that only touch beer occasionally.

Sometimes it is the glass, not the beer

A can that has been dented, or a glass that was recently polished with a cloth that had metal residue on it, can transfer a metallic note that has nothing to do with how the beer was brewed or packaged. If a metallic taste disappears when you pour the same beer into a fresh, clean glass, the beer was never the problem.

This is worth checking before assuming a brewery has a quality issue, since it is one of the few faults that can be entirely a serving problem rather than a brewing one.

Water chemistry can be a contributing factor too

Borewell water in parts of India can carry naturally high iron content, and if a brewery is not treating its water properly before brewing, that iron can carry through into the finished beer. This is a different root cause from equipment corrosion but produces a similar taste, which is why tracing the source properly matters rather than guessing.

A brewery that tests its water supply regularly, rather than assuming it is consistent, catches this kind of shift before it reaches a customer's glass.

How brewers track it down

The process is usually elimination: taste the beer straight from the tank, then again after it passes through each piece of equipment on its way to the glass, from the bright tank to the line to the tap. Wherever the metallic note first appears is where the problem lives.

Replacing a single worn fitting or coupler often solves what looked, at first, like a much bigger problem with the beer itself.

What you can do as a drinker

If a draught beer tastes metallic, ask for the same beer in a can or bottle before assuming the brewery is at fault. If the packaged version tastes clean, the issue is almost certainly in that bar's line or tap, not the brewery, which is one more reason draught line cleaning matters as much as anything that happens inside the brewhouse.

Mention it to the bar staff too. A bar that hears the same complaint from more than one customer has a genuine reason to check its lines sooner rather than waiting for the next scheduled clean.

Common questions

Is metallic tasting beer safe to drink?

Generally yes, at the trace levels involved in a flavour fault. It is unpleasant rather than dangerous, though a beer that tastes strongly metallic is best sent back.

Why does draught beer taste metallic more often than bottled beer?

Draught beer passes through more equipment, taps, couplers and lines, that can corrode or wear with use. Packaged beer has a shorter, more controlled path from brewery to glass.

Can water treatment fix a metallic taste?

Yes, if the cause is iron in the source water. Standard water treatment steps used in brewing can remove excess iron before it ever reaches the brew.

Does a metallic taste mean the can is damaged?

Not necessarily, but a dented or scratched can is worth ruling out first, since damage to the internal lining can occasionally cause exactly this kind of note.
