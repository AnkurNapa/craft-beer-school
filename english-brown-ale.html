English brown ale

Brown ale sits between mild and porter, and the two English traditions inside it, southern and northern, do not agree on much except the colour. Here is how to tell them apart and what each one is doing with its grain.

3 min read · Updated September 2024 · Craft Beer School

Two beers wearing one name

English brown ale is really two separate traditions that happen to share a colour. The southern style, associated with London, is darker, sweeter and lower in strength, often below 3.5% ABV, built on crystal and chocolate malt with barely any hop presence to speak of.

The northern style, associated with Newcastle, runs drier, a touch stronger at 4 to 5.4% ABV, and shows more of a nutty, biscuity malt character with restrained but noticeable hop bitterness. Neither style is trying to impress you with intensity. Both are built for an evening of steady drinking.

What to expect in the glass

Colour ranges from deep amber to chestnut brown, generally clear, with a tan head that does not last long. The aroma leads with caramel, toffee and toasted nuts, sometimes a faint chocolate note, and hops stay in the background as light earthy or floral support rather than a feature.

On the palate it is medium bodied, moderately sweet in the southern version and drier in the northern one, finishing clean without the roasted bite you would get from a porter or stout.

The grain bill that builds the flavour

A pale ale malt base carries most of the fermentable sugar, with 5 to 15 percent crystal malt for caramel sweetness and body, and a small addition of chocolate malt, sometimes as little as 2 percent, for colour and a whisper of roast without pushing the beer into porter territory.

Some northern recipes add a touch of amber malt for a biscuit note. Mash temperature usually sits at the higher end, around 66 to 68 degrees Celsius, to leave more unfermentable dextrin behind and keep the body from thinning out.

Yeast and fermentation

An English ale yeast strain does the work here, fermenting at 18 to 20 degrees Celsius and leaving a light fruity ester, apple or light stone fruit, that suits the malt character without fighting it. Attenuation is moderate rather than aggressive, which is part of why the finished beer keeps some sweetness.

This is a beer where a clean, well controlled fermentation matters more than an exotic strain. Nothing here is meant to shout.

Hops stay in a supporting role

Traditional English varieties, Fuggles or East Kent Goldings, provide bitterness in the 20 to 30 IBU range and a mild earthy, floral aroma. This is not a beer for late, heavy dry hopping. A small bittering addition and perhaps a light flavour addition late in the boil is usually enough.

Brewing it in India

The awkward part in most Indian breweries is not the recipe, it is holding fermentation at 18 to 20 degrees Celsius through an Indian summer without a proper glycol jacket. A brewer we know ferments brown ale in a converted dairy chiller room because the tank's own cooling could not hold that band through a Chennai afternoon.

Once you have temperature control sorted, brown ale is a forgiving beer to brew and an easy one to keep in regular rotation, since it uses ordinary malts and does not depend on fresh hop character to taste right months after packaging.

Serving it right

Serve brown ale a little warmer than a lager, around 10 to 13 degrees Celsius, in a nonic pint glass or a simple tulip. Too cold and the caramel and nut notes disappear, which defeats the point of the style.

It also carries lower carbonation well, closer to cask conditioned levels than a fizzy lager, so do not chase a huge foamy head when you pour it. A thin cap that clears fast is normal and correct for this beer.

Common mistakes brewers make with it

The most frequent error is treating brown ale as a dumping ground for whatever specialty grains are left in the store. Add too much chocolate malt and it slides into porter. Add too much crystal and it turns cloying rather than caramel sweet, especially once it warms up in the glass.

The second common mistake is over hopping it to make it feel more interesting. A brown ale with a strong citrus or resin hop character stops reading as a brown ale and starts reading as a confused pale ale with dark colouring. Restraint is the actual skill in this style, not addition.

Common questions

Is brown ale the same as a porter?

No. Porter is darker and more roast forward, usually with some roasted barley or black malt character. Brown ale relies on crystal and a touch of chocolate malt, and stays nuttier and less bitter with roast.

Why are there two brown ale styles?

They developed in different English regions with different water and local preferences. London's version stayed sweeter and weaker, Newcastle's stayed drier and a little stronger, and both names stuck.

Does brown ale need a lot of hops?

No. It is a malt driven style. Enough hops to balance the sweetness is the goal, not hop aroma or flavour as a feature.

What food goes with brown ale?

Roasted or grilled meats, mild curries and nut based dishes suit it well, since the malt sweetness and low bitterness will not fight spice the way a hoppier beer can.
