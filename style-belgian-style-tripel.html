GobletBelgian Tripel

Golden, strong and surprisingly dry. It drinks lighter than its 8 to 9 percent.

Written byAnkur NapaMaster Brewer and course instructor
Written byChatty GirijaBeer podcaster and creative strategist

By Ankur Napa and Chatty Girija

Colour4-7 SRM
Bitterness20-45 IBU
Strength7.1-10.1%

Serve in a goblet at 7-10 °C

TasteProfileIn IndiaFoodBrewed here

What it tastes like

Tripel is the strong golden ale of the Belgian abbey tradition, brought to its modern form at Westmalle in the 1930s. It looks like a pale lager and carries the strength of a fortified wine, which is exactly why it catches people out. The trick is sugar. Brewers add plain white or candi sugar to a pale malt base, and the yeast turns it into alcohol without leaving body behind. The result is a beer that sits around 8 to 9 percent but finishes dry and almost crisp.

The flavour is mostly yeast and malt in balance. Expect banana, pear and orange from the esters, a peppery, clove-like spice, soft honeyed malt and a firm but quiet bitterness from noble hops. Alcohol shows as a gentle warmth, never as solvent or heat. A hot, boozy tripel usually means fermentation ran away without temperature control. A good one is lively, bottle conditioned and dangerous in the most polite way.

Style profile

Look
Deep yellow to rich gold, bright to slightly hazy from bottle conditioning, with a dense, long-lasting white head.
Aroma and flavour
Spicy phenols and pear, banana and orange esters over soft, honeyed pale malt. A light herbal hop note and gentle alcohol warmth.
Mouthfeel
Medium-light body for its strength, high carbonation, and a dry, slightly bitter finish with a little warmth.
Ingredients
Pilsner malt, a good share of white or candi sugar, noble hops such as Saaz or Styrian Golding, and a Belgian abbey yeast.

In India

Tripel is a beer to treat with respect in Indian heat. It is strong, and a cold, fizzy glass on a hot afternoon goes down faster than its alcohol deserves, so pour it in small measures. It travels reasonably well because of its strength, but bottles left in a hot godown lose their freshness and turn honeyed and dull. Goa has a packaged example, and Bengaluru brewpubs pour one now and then. It suits a slow dinner or a wedding toast better than a session.

At the Indian table

Food pairings contributed by Anuradha Rao

  • Hyderabadi chicken dum biryaniThe beer's carbonation and dry finish clear the ghee, and its spice meets the whole garam masala.
  • Malai koftaRich, creamy gravy is cut by the bubbles, while the honeyed malt sits comfortably with the cashew.
  • Aged goudaNutty, crystalline cheese and a strong golden ale share a sweetness without either taking over.
  • Kaju katliThe beer's dryness stops the sweet from cloying, and the cashew picks up the soft malt.
