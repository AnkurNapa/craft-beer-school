Beer serving temperature by style

Cold hides flaws, but it also hides flavour. Here is the temperature range that actually suits each major style, and why.

3 min read · Updated April 2022 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. Why temperature changes what you taste
  2. Lagers: 4 to 7 degrees Celsius
  3. Pale ales and IPAs: 7 to 10 degrees Celsius
  4. Amber ales, bitters and Belgian styles: 10 to 13 degrees Celsius
  5. Stouts, porters and strong ales: 12 to 16 degrees Celsius
  6. Managing this in an Indian kitchen

Why temperature changes what you taste

Cold suppresses your ability to smell, because aroma compounds need warmth to evaporate off the surface of the beer and reach your nose. It also mutes sweetness and rounds off bitterness, which is why the same beer can taste almost unrecognisably different at two different temperatures. This is not a flaw to be hidden, it is a lever, and different styles are designed around different points on it.

This is also why the same bottle can taste noticeably better on a second glass poured later in the evening, once the first glass has had time to warm properly in the room. Nothing about the beer changed, only the temperature at which you are experiencing it.

Lagers: 4 to 7 degrees Celsius

A clean, well made lager is built to be refreshing, and serving it properly cold suits that job well. Go much colder than about 4 degrees and you also numb the subtle malt and hop character that separates a good pilsner from an ordinary one, which is exactly why mass market lagers are often served closer to freezing: there is less character to lose in the first place.

Serving a lager too warm has the opposite problem: any minor flaw in a simple, clean recipe becomes far more obvious once the beer loses the crispness that normally distracts from it, which is one reason lager brewing is often considered harder to get exactly right than brewing a bigger, more forgiving ale.

Pale ales and IPAs: 7 to 10 degrees Celsius

A little warmer than a lager, because these styles are sold on hop aroma, and aroma needs some warmth to show properly. Serve an IPA straight from the fridge and you will taste mostly bitterness with the fruit and floral notes arriving late, if at all, by the time the glass has warmed in your hand towards room temperature.

A useful trick if you do not own a thermometer is to judge by touch: a lager glass should feel properly cold to hold, an IPA glass should feel cool but not painfully so, and a stout glass should feel closer to room temperature than to fridge temperature.

Amber ales, bitters and Belgian styles: 10 to 13 degrees Celsius

These styles carry more malt complexity and, in Belgian beers, yeast derived esters and spice notes that need warmth to open up fully. Cellar temperature, which is roughly what this range describes, is the traditional serving point in the regions these styles come from, and it is worth trusting that tradition over instinct.

Belgian tripels and dubbels in particular are often served in their own branded glass at a specific temperature by the breweries that make them, a tradition worth respecting rather than treating as marketing, since the glass and temperature were chosen together to show the beer at its best.

Stouts, porters and strong ales: 12 to 16 degrees Celsius

The darkest, strongest and most complex beers benefit from the most warmth of all. A barleywine or an imperial stout served cold will taste thin and one dimensional, hiding almost everything interesting about it. Given time in the glass to warm towards the top of this range, the same beer often reveals dried fruit, chocolate or sherry notes that were simply not perceptible when it was cold.

Barleywines and other very strong ales are sometimes served even warmer still, closer to 16 to 18 degrees, particularly if they have spent time ageing, since warmth helps release the slow, complex aromas that develop during long storage.

Managing this in an Indian kitchen

Most home fridges run at 2 to 4 degrees, colder than almost anything on this list wants for proper serving. Pull the beer out 10 to 20 minutes before serving rather than pouring it straight from the door shelf, and pour into the right glass from our glassware guide so it warms evenly rather than from the outside in.

If you are building a lineup for a tasting session, temperature is one more variable worth controlling alongside pour order, since a warm early beer and a cold late one will not compare fairly against each other.

A simple habit worth building is checking the label or style before deciding where the beer goes in your fridge. Keeping stouts and strong ales on a counter or cooler shelf, rather than the coldest part of the fridge, saves you the wait later.

Common questions

Is it true that all beer should be served ice cold?

No. That habit comes from mass market lagers designed to be refreshing above all else. Most craft styles are built to show more character at slightly warmer temperatures.

What happens if I serve a stout too cold?

You lose most of the roasted, chocolate and dried fruit character that makes a stout interesting, and you are left tasting mostly bitterness and carbonation.

How long should I let beer warm up before drinking?

Ten to twenty minutes out of a home fridge is usually enough to bring most ales into their proper range, depending on the room temperature.

Does serving temperature affect head retention?

Slightly. Colder beer holds carbon dioxide better, so a very warm pour can go flatter faster, which is one reason lagers stay near the colder end of their range.
