Branding a beer festival stall so people remember you next week

Beer festivals give a brewery thousands of drinkers in a day, most of whom will forget it by the next weekend. Here is how we would brand a stall so the visit turns into a regular.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

What a festival visitor actually remembers

Picture a Saturday beer festival in Bengaluru or Pune. Visitors walk a row of stalls, each with a banner, a tap tower and a queue. They taste small pours of many beers, often from a branded festival glass. Between stalls they talk to friends. After a few hours, everything blurs.

What sticks is usually one of three things: a beer that surprised them, a person who made them laugh or a stall that was easy to find again. Your branding should support all three, without trying to say everything about the brewery.

Signage that works from across the ground

Most stalls hang a banner at eye level, which disappears once a queue forms in front of it. Put the brewery name high, above head height, large enough to read from twenty metres. Use your core colour so the stall looks like your cans. A tall flag or a backdrop above the tap tower does more than a long table banner.

Then put a clear, short tap list at the front: beer name, style, strength and one line on taste. People choose in the queue. If they have to ask what each beer is, the line slows and they move on to the next stall.

Make the pour part of the brand

The beer is the strongest branding you have. Serve it properly: clean lines, the right temperature, a decent head. In Indian outdoor festivals, heat is the enemy. Kegs sitting in the sun, warm lines and a jockey box without enough ice turn a crisp lager into a foamy, flat mess. Keep kegs shaded and iced. Pour a test glass before the gates open. A beer that pours badly says more about your brewery than any banner.

Choose your festival list carefully. Bring two or three beers you are proud of and one that starts conversations. Twelve taps of average beer leave no memory. Our guide to launching a new beer covers when a festival is the right place to debut something.

Staff are the stall

The people pouring are the brand in that moment. Put brewers on the stall for at least part of the day. Visitors love talking to the person who made the beer. Give everyone the same short description of each beer so the story is consistent. Branded T-shirts help people find you again.

Serve responsibly: check age where the festival requires it, refuse visibly intoxicated guests, keep water available and point people toward food and safe transport home. A stall that handles this well earns more respect than one that pours freely.

Giveaways that bring people back

Stickers and coasters are cheap and people keep them. A small card with the taproom address, a QR code for your tap list and an invitation to visit gives the visitor a reason to come back. Merchandise works if it is good enough to wear. Our guide to beer merchandise covers what sells and what gathers dust.

Avoid offers that encourage heavy drinking, such as free pints for collecting stamps. They are a responsibility risk and in many states they may breach rules on promotion.

Capture the contact

The real value of a festival is the people who liked your beer. Give them an easy way to stay in touch: follow your social account, join a newsletter with clear consent or book a taproom visit. A QR code on the tap list and the giveaway card does most of the work. Keep the ask simple and honest about what they will receive.

Note which beers people liked most and what they asked about. That is free market research.

The week after

Most breweries go quiet after a festival. That is exactly when visitors are deciding where to drink next. Post photos from the stall within two days, thank people who came and tell them where to find the beers they tasted. If you captured emails, send one short note. If you have a taproom, invite them for the same beer with a reason to come, such as a brewer's talk or food pairing.

Building that link between a stall and a regular visit is community marketing. It works when it is planned before the festival, not improvised after it.

Common questions

How many beers should we bring to a beer festival?

Usually three or four you are proud of. Too many taps spread your staff thin and make it harder for visitors to remember any one beer.

What giveaways work best at a beer festival stall?

Stickers, coasters and a small card with your taproom address and a QR code. They are cheap, people keep them and they give a reason to visit.

How do we keep beer cold at an outdoor festival in India?

Shade the kegs, keep them iced and use a jockey box or cooling unit with enough ice for the day. Pour a test glass before opening.
