How to read a beer label

Once you know what to look for, a beer label stops being decoration and starts being a genuinely useful guide to what is in the can, from the style you should expect to how fresh it actually is.

3 min read · Updated August 2026 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. ABV: more than just a strength number
  2. IBU and what it actually measures
  3. The style name is doing real work
  4. Dates: packaging date versus best before
  5. Ingredients and process notes worth reading
  6. Putting it together before you buy

ABV: more than just a strength number

Alcohol by volume tells you how strong a beer is, but it also hints at body and character. A beer under 4 percent is usually built to be light and easy drinking, while anything above 8 percent is generally meant to be sipped slowly rather than drunk the way you would a session lager.

ABV alone will not tell you how sweet, bitter or hoppy a beer is, so treat it as one data point rather than the whole picture. Two beers at the same ABV can sit at completely different points on every other axis of flavour, from dry and crisp to rich and sweet.

IBU and what it actually measures

International Bitterness Units measure a specific chemical marker of bitterness, not how bitter the beer will taste to you. A high IBU beer with a lot of residual malt sweetness can taste less bitter than a lower IBU beer that is very dry, since sweetness and bitterness balance each other on the palate.

Use IBU as a rough signal of hop intensity rather than a precise prediction of how bitter the beer will feel in your mouth. Two brewers measuring the same beer can also get slightly different readings depending on their lab method, so treat the number as approximate rather than exact. If a number looks unusually high or low for the style, that alone is worth a second look before you buy.

The style name is doing real work

A style name like pale ale, stout, saison or gose sets expectations that matter more than most drinkers realise, because it tells you what the beer is meant to taste like before you have poured a drop. A gose that tastes sour and salty is on style. A pilsner that tastes the same way, as covered in our guide on sourness as infection, is very likely faulty.

If a can does not name a recognisable style at all, that is itself useful information, and it is worth asking staff or checking the brewery's own description before you buy. A brewery confident in what it made will usually tell you exactly what to expect.

Dates: packaging date versus best before

A packaging or bottled on date tells you exactly how old the beer is, which is the single most useful piece of information on the whole can for a hop forward style like an IPA. A best before date is a food safety boundary and tends to be set conservatively, often well past the point where flavour has already faded.

Where a brewery gives both, trust the packaging date over the best before date for judging freshness, particularly for hoppy or pale styles. For a stronger, darker beer built to age, the gap between the two dates matters much less, and the beer may even improve for a while past its packaging date.

Ingredients and process notes worth reading

Adjuncts like fruit purée, lactose or spices, and process notes like barrel ageing or a specific yeast strain, all set expectations for flavour before you taste. A label that mentions Brettanomyces or a wild fermentation is telling you the funky character you might otherwise assume is a fault is actually intentional.

This context is exactly what separates a correct phenolic note in one style from a genuine fault in another, and the label is often the fastest way to know which you are holding. A brewery proud of an unusual process will almost always say so, since it is part of what makes the beer worth trying.

Putting it together before you buy

Check the style first to set your expectations, glance at ABV and IBU as rough signals rather than exact predictions, and prioritise the packaging date over the best before date whenever both are shown. None of this takes more than a few seconds once it becomes a habit, and it consistently improves the odds that what you buy is what you actually wanted.

Over time this turns buying beer from a guessing game into something closer to reading a menu you actually understand, which makes trying an unfamiliar brewery or style far less of a gamble.

Common questions

Is a higher IBU always a more bitter beer?

Not necessarily. Malt sweetness and body can balance out a high IBU, so two beers with the same IBU number can taste quite different in actual bitterness.

Why do some cans only show a best before date and not a packaging date?

Not every brewery lists both, sometimes due to labelling space or local regulation. Where only a best before date appears, treat it as a wide safety margin rather than a precise freshness guide.

Does a higher ABV mean a better beer?

No. ABV is a measure of strength, not quality. Many excellent beers sit at a modest 4 to 5 percent, and strength should be chosen based on the drinking occasion, not treated as a quality marker.

What does it mean if a label lists a specific yeast strain?

It usually signals the brewery wants you to notice that yeast's character, commonly fruity esters in a Belgian style or clove in a wheat beer, as a deliberate part of the flavour rather than a fault.
