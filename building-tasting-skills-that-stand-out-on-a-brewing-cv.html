Building tasting skills that stand out on a brewing CV

Tasting skill is one of the few things a brewery cannot teach quickly. Applicants who can detect and name common faults and show evidence of it stand out. Here is how to build tasting skills worth putting on a brewing CV.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Why tasting skill gets you hired

A brewer tastes beer at every stage: wort, young beer, finished beer and packaged beer. The ability to spot a fault early, before it reaches a customer, saves batches and reputations. Head brewers know this, so they value candidates who can taste with discipline.

The problem is that every CV says "good palate". It means nothing without evidence. What stands out is a specific, checkable skill: you can identify diacetyl, acetaldehyde, DMS and oxidation at typical levels. You also have a record or a certificate to show it.

Learn the common off-flavours first

Start with the faults brewers meet most often:

  • Diacetyl: butter or butterscotch, from fermentation or bacteria. Its flavour threshold in lager is often quoted at around 0.1 mg/L.
  • Acetaldehyde: green apple, from young or rushed beer.
  • DMS: cooked sweetcorn, from pale malt and a weak boil or slow cooling.
  • Oxidation: cardboard or paper, from oxygen and age.
  • Lightstruck: skunky, from light hitting hop compounds.
  • Isovaleric acid: cheesy or sweaty, often from old hops.

Our guide to beer off-flavours explains where each one comes from. Knowing the cause matters at interview as much as spotting the flavour.

Train with spiked samples

The standard way to train is with spiked samples. A clean, neutral lager is dosed with a known off-flavour compound from a training kit, at a level near its threshold. You taste it next to an unspiked control and learn to recognise the difference. Repeat until you can find it blind.

Kits are available from brewing suppliers. If cost is an issue, share one with a few friends or a homebrew club. Some faults can also be made at home: leaving a clear bottle of hoppy beer in sunlight for an hour gives a clear example of lightstruck character, for instance.

Train in short sessions. Your palate tires after a handful of samples, so six or eight beers in one sitting is plenty. Taste at the same time of day, away from strong food and perfume, with water and plain crackers between samples. Spit when you are training. You are measuring, not drinking.

Keep a tasting log

A tasting log is the evidence most applicants lack. For each beer, write the date, the beer, the appearance, aroma, flavour, mouthfeel and any faults, with a short note on likely cause. Over months, the log shows growth. It also gives you something concrete to show at interview.

Write in plain, specific words. "Moderate citrus and pine hop aroma, light bready malt, slight green apple suggesting young beer" tells a reader far more than "nice and hoppy". Our guide on how to taste beer sets out a method.

Recognised certificates

Certificates give an employer an outside check on your skill. The common ones for beer include:

  • Cicerone programme: Certified Beer Server, then Certified Cicerone and higher levels.
  • BJCP: the US-based beer judging scheme, which tests style knowledge and judging.
  • WSET: beer qualifications from the Wine and Spirit Education Trust.

Each has a different focus. None replaces experience, but any of them shows you took tasting seriously. Our guide to the BJCP exam explains one of the most respected tests.

Join a panel

If you work in or near a brewery, ask to join its tasting panel. Panels taste beer under controlled conditions, often using methods such as the triangle test, where a taster finds the odd sample out of three. Guessing gives one in three right, so repeated correct answers show real skill.

Volunteering as a steward at a beer competition is another way in. You see how judges work, taste a range of beers and meet people in the industry.

Putting it on your CV

Write specific, checkable lines. "Trained on 12 common off-flavours using spiked samples; can identify diacetyl, DMS and acetaldehyde at threshold levels" is far stronger than "good palate". Add certificates with dates. Offer to bring your tasting log to interview.

Expect to be tested. Many breweries run a tasting test for brewer candidates. A CV claim you cannot back up does more harm than leaving it out.

Your first step: buy or share an off-flavour training kit, start with diacetyl and acetaldehyde. Taste each spiked sample next to a clean control until you can pick it out blind three times running.

Common questions

What off-flavours should a brewer be able to identify?

Common ones include diacetyl, acetaldehyde, DMS, oxidation, lightstruck character and isovaleric acid. Knowing the cause of each matters as much as spotting it.

How can I prove tasting skill on a CV?

Use specific claims backed by evidence: off-flavour training, a tasting log, panel experience and recognised certificates such as Cicerone or BJCP.

What is a triangle test?

A sensory test where a taster is given three samples, two identical and one different. The taster must find the odd one out. Guessing gives a one in three chance.
