Lactose in beer: sweetness, body and the allergen label

Brewing yeast cannot ferment lactose, so whatever goes in stays in. That makes it a useful tool for sweetness and body. It is also a responsibility on the label. Here is how to use it and how to tell drinkers it is there.

What lactose does

Lactose is milk sugar. Ordinary brewing yeast, both ale and lager strains, lacks the enzyme to break it down, so lactose passes through fermentation untouched. It raises the final gravity, adds gentle sweetness and gives a fuller, rounder body.

It is much less sweet than table sugar, so it reads as soft and creamy rather than sugary. That is why it became the defining ingredient of the milk stout, where it rounds off roasted bitterness. More recently it has turned up in milkshake IPAs, pastry stouts and fruited sours, where brewers want sweetness without a stuck fermentation.

How much to use

A typical milk stout uses lactose at around 5% to 10% of the fermentables by weight. For a 20 litre homebrew batch that is often 250 to 500 g. On a 1,000 litre brewpub batch, the same share scales to roughly 12 to 25 kg. Start at the lower end. Lactose is easy to add more of in the next batch and impossible to remove.

Remember that it counts towards your original gravity but stays in the final gravity. Your measured apparent attenuation will look lower than the yeast's rated figure. Your ABV calculation still works normally from OG and FG. Do not chase a 'stuck' fermentation that is simply lactose doing its job.

When to add it

Most brewers dissolve lactose in the kettle for the last 10 to 15 minutes of the boil. It dissolves easily in hot wort and the boil sanitises it. Stir well so it does not settle and scorch on the element or kettle base.

Some brewers add it later, as a boiled and cooled syrup during conditioning, to adjust sweetness to taste. That gives more control, but the syrup must be handled cleanly. Lactose cannot be fermented by brewing yeast, but some bacteria can use it, so a dirty addition is an infection risk.

Buying and storing lactose

Buy food-grade lactose, sold as milk sugar or lactose powder, from a food ingredients supplier or homebrew shop, with the grade printed on the pack. Pharmaceutical lactose is also very pure but usually costs more. Lactose absorbs moisture and forms hard lumps in humid weather, so keep it sealed in an airtight container with the date of purchase on it.

For a brewpub, weigh each addition rather than counting scoops. A lumpy, damp powder weighs more per scoop than a dry one. Your sweetness will drift from batch to batch without anyone noticing why.

The allergen question

Lactose for brewing is made from milk and can carry traces of milk protein. Milk is a recognised food allergen. Drinkers with a milk allergy need to know, as do people avoiding dairy for other reasons, including vegans. A beer that looks like an ordinary stout gives no clue.

In India, food labelling rules under FSSAI require common allergens, including milk, to be declared on packaged foods. FSSAI also sets standards for alcoholic beverages. State excise departments add their own label approval process on top. How the rules apply to a specific beer label changes with notifications, so check the current FSSAI labelling regulations and your state's requirements, or ask a licensing consultant. Telling drinkers in the taproom

Draught beer has no can label, so the menu and the staff carry the message. Write 'contains milk (lactose)' on the tap list and on any menu board. Brief bar staff so they can answer when someone asks whether the stout is dairy-free. A one-word 'milk' tag next to the beer name is easy to read in a dim taproom.

Watch for cross-contact too. If you use lactose in some beers and not others, keep it labelled and stored away from other ingredients. Clean the kettle and hoses well before the next batch.

Alternatives to lactose

If you want body and sweetness without milk, you have options. Raise mash temperature to make more unfermentable dextrins. Use oats, crystal malts or a dextrin malt. Choose a less attenuative yeast. Some brewers use maltodextrin, which adds body with little sweetness. None of these exactly copy lactose, but together they can get close for drinkers who need a dairy-free beer.

Balancing sweetness, body and attenuation on purpose is one of the first real recipe design skills. A beer that is cloying in the glass is usually a grist and mash problem before it is a lactose problem.

Common questions

Does yeast ferment lactose?

No. Ordinary brewing yeast cannot break down lactose, so it remains in the beer and adds sweetness and body.

How much lactose goes in a milk stout?

Typically 5% to 10% of fermentables, often 250 to 500 g in a 20 litre batch. Start low and adjust in the next batch.

Is a lactose beer safe for someone with a milk allergy?

No. Lactose comes from milk and can carry milk protein, so the beer should be clearly labelled and staff should be able to say so.

Can vegans drink milk stout?

Not if it contains lactose, which is made from milk. Tell drinkers on the tap list and label.
