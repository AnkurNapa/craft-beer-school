Vienna lager

Vienna lager is the quiet ancestor behind several more famous styles. Here is what it actually tastes like, and why so few Viennese breweries make it any more.

3 min read · Updated June 2022 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Anton Dreher and a new kind of malt

Vienna lager was developed by brewer Anton Dreher in the 1840s, around the same time pilsner was emerging in Bohemia, using a newly developed lightly kilned amber malt that came to be called Vienna malt. It gave the beer a warm copper colour and a toasty, biscuity character without the heavy roast of darker styles.

The style found more lasting popularity abroad than at home, travelling to Mexico with Austrian and German brewers in the 1800s and influencing the amber lagers still brewed there today, while the original Viennese version became relatively rare.

What to expect in the glass

Vienna lager pours a clear amber to light copper, with a bready, toasty, slightly biscuity aroma and a clean malt sweetness that never turns heavy. Hop character is moderate, 18 to 30 IBU, present as a firm supporting bitterness rather than a feature.

It should finish clean and dry for its malt intensity, without the fuller body of a Marzen or the higher strength of a bock. This is a session strength beer built around a very specific malt character rather than a big beer wearing an amber colour.

Clarity matters too. A hazy Vienna lager, unless deliberately unfiltered, usually points to a cold conditioning period that was cut short rather than a fault in the recipe itself.

Vienna malt is the whole point

Vienna malt is kilned a little darker than standard pale malt, giving it a light golden to amber colour and a distinctive toasty, slightly nutty flavour that pale malt cannot replicate. Most Vienna lager recipes use it as the majority of the grain bill, sometimes with a small addition of Munich malt for extra depth.

Because that malt character carries the whole beer, the same discipline that applies to pilsner applies here: any process shortcut shows up clearly against a relatively simple grain bill with nowhere obvious to hide it.

Freshness of the malt itself matters more than brewers sometimes assume. Vienna malt that has sat too long in a humid store loses some of its toasty character before it ever reaches the mash tun, which is a real risk in Indian monsoon conditions without proper sealed storage.

Fermentation stays true to lager form

A clean bottom fermenting lager yeast at 9 to 13°C, followed by several weeks of cold lagering, is standard here, exactly as with pilsner and Helles. There is nothing exotic in the process. The style earns its character almost entirely from the malt choice rather than any unusual fermentation technique.

This makes Vienna lager a reasonable style for a brewery already comfortable with pilsner or Helles to add without learning a substantially new process, since it is really the same lager technique applied to a different malt bill.

How it differs from its amber lager relatives

Against Marzen, Vienna lager is generally lighter in body and a touch lower in strength, without Marzen's fuller, more intensely malty character. Against Munich Dunkel, Vienna stays paler and less rich, since Dunkel leans on darker Munich malt for a deeper, almost bready sweetness.

Tasted together, the three sit on a clear spectrum of malt intensity and colour, Vienna the lightest of the three amber and dark lagers, Marzen fuller, Dunkel darkest and richest.

Serving it well

Serve at 7 to 10°C in a standard lager glass or stein. It pairs naturally with grilled meats and roasted vegetables, where the toasty malt character complements rather than competes with char and smoke.

As with any malt forward lager, a genuine lagering period rather than a rushed cold crash makes a real difference to how rounded the finished beer tastes, so give it the time the style actually needs.

It is also worth pouring next to a genuine Marzen and a Helles in the same tasting, since the three beers share enough of a family resemblance that comparing them directly teaches a drinker more about malt character than any single glass could on its own, particularly for someone new to lager tasting.

A common mistake is treating Vienna lager as simply a lighter Oktoberfest beer. The two share a malt heritage but Vienna lager is drier and more restrained, without the heavier toasted sweetness that marzen carries. Brewing it with a marzen grain bill and just diluting it will not get you there.

Common questions

Is Vienna lager the same as Mexican amber lager?

They share a direct historical link, since Austrian and German brewers carried the style and Vienna malt to Mexico in the 1800s, but the two traditions have since developed somewhat differently.

Why is Vienna lager less common in Vienna today?

Brewing trends shifted over the twentieth century toward paler, lighter lagers, and the style became relatively rare in its home city even as Vienna malt itself remained widely used elsewhere.

What is the main difference between Vienna lager and Marzen?

Vienna lager is generally lighter bodied and slightly lower in strength, while Marzen is fuller and maltier, though both share a similar toasty amber malt character.

Does Vienna lager need special hops?

No. It uses a moderate amount of noble style hops mainly for balance rather than aroma. The malt character, not the hops, defines this style.
