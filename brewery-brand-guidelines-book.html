What a brewery brand book should contain and who should use it

A brand book is the rulebook that keeps a brewery looking and sounding like itself as it grows. Here is what to put in it, who it is for and how to stop it gathering dust.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

What a brand book is for

A brewery brand book (also called brand guidelines) records the decisions behind the brand so other people can apply them without asking the founder every time. It answers practical questions: which logo goes on a keg collar, what colour is the IPA, how do we write an Instagram caption, what never goes on a can.

Small breweries often think they are too small for one. In fact they need it most, because they rely on freelancers, printers and new staff who rotate quickly. Without written rules, each new person invents their own version of the brand.

The strategy pages

Start with the thinking, kept short:

  • Who we are. Mission, values and what the brewery stands for, in plain words.
  • Who we are for. The drinkers you serve. Our guide to beer drinker personas helps here.
  • Positioning. One sentence on how you are different from the beers next to you.
  • Personality and voice. How the brand sounds, with examples of right and wrong. See our guide to brewery brand voice.

Two pages are enough. A designer reads them to understand why the rules exist. A new bar manager reads them to know what kind of place they work in.

The identity pages

  • Logo. Main version, alternatives for small spaces, minimum size, clear space around it and colour versions for dark and light backgrounds. Show the misuses too: stretched, recoloured, placed on busy photos.
  • Colour. Core colours, neutrals and the range colours for each beer, written as Pantone, CMYK, RGB and hex.
  • Typography. Display and body faces, sizes, weights and where each is used.
  • Illustration and photography. The house style, examples and what to avoid.

The application pages

This is the part most brand books skip and most users need. Show the rules applied to real things:

  • Can and label templates with the legal block placed correctly.
  • Keg collars, tap handles and tap list layouts.
  • Menus, table tents and coasters.
  • Social media templates and photo style.
  • Merchandise placement rules.
  • Taproom signage and staff uniforms.

Include a short page on what the brand must never do, for example no imagery that appeals to minors and no claims that drinking improves performance. That keeps a well-meaning freelancer out of trouble with advertising rules.

Who uses it

Write the book for the people who will open it, not only for designers:

  • Designers and printers need specifications and files.
  • Marketing staff and agencies need voice and social rules.
  • Taproom managers need menu, signage and event rules.
  • Sales staff need the positioning line and the one-sentence description of each beer.
  • Brewers need to know how beer names and descriptions get chosen.

A floor detail we like: keep a printed copy of the tap list and menu pages behind the bar. When a chalkboard gets rewritten at 7 pm on a Saturday, nobody opens a PDF.

Format and ownership

Keep it short enough to read. Twenty to forty pages covers most breweries. Share it as a PDF plus a shared folder with logo files, fonts, templates and colour swatches, all in one place with clear names. Give one person ownership, usually the founder or the marketing lead, with the job of updating it when something changes.

Review it once a year and whenever you launch a new range or open a new outlet. A brand book that no longer matches the cans is worse than none, because people stop trusting it.

Add a short change log at the back. When the IPA colour shifts or a new seasonal template is added, note the date and what changed. Printers and agencies working from an old copy can then see at a glance that their version is out of date.

Signs your brand book is working

New freelancers deliver work that needs fewer corrections. Cans from different print runs look like a family. The taproom chalkboard looks like the website. Staff can describe each beer the same way. If those things are not happening, the book is either missing the application pages or nobody knows it exists.

Writing down the strategy part is the hard bit. Logos and colours are easy to specify once you know who the brand is for and how it should feel.

Common questions

How long should a brewery brand book be?

For most small breweries, twenty to forty pages. Enough to cover strategy, identity and real applications without becoming a document nobody reads.

Who should create the brand book?

Usually the brand designer drafts it with the founder. One person inside the brewery should own it and keep it updated.

Does a brewpub need a brand book?

Yes, especially one that relies on freelancers and rotating staff. Menu, signage and social rules save time every week.
