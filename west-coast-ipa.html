West Coast IPA

Before New England IPA turned the category hazy and soft, this was simply what IPA meant in America: clear, aggressively hopped and dry enough to let the bitterness stand out rather than hide.

3 min read · Updated June 2025 · Craft Beer School

The template the whole category grew from

West Coast IPA developed through the 1990s and 2000s around breweries in California and the Pacific Northwest, built on American hop varieties bred for high alpha acid and strong pine, citrus and resin character. It set the pattern, clear, bitter, dry, that most people meant by IPA before the New England style split the category in two.

For a long stretch, this was simply IPA, full stop, and the qualifier West Coast only became necessary once a genuinely different version of the style existed to compare it against.

Sensory profile

Clarity is part of the point. The beer pours bright gold to copper, with a firm white head, and the aroma is pine, grapefruit pith, resin, sometimes a dank or catty edge depending on the hop blend. Bitterness is assertive, usually 50 to 70 IBU, and it lingers rather than fading fast.

None of this is subtle by design. The style rewards a hop blend chosen with intent rather than one thrown together, since pine and citrus character need to sit in balance rather than fighting each other in the glass.

Grain bill built to stay out of the way

A simple base of pale ale or Pilsner malt, sometimes with a small addition of crystal malt for colour and a touch of body, keeps the malt profile clean and slightly bready so the hops have nothing to compete with. ABV usually sits between 6 and 7.5%, with a dry finish, final gravity often 1.008 to 1.014, that keeps the beer from feeling sweet against all that bitterness.

This restraint on the malt side is deliberate. Any extra sweetness left behind would blunt the bitterness the style is built around, so brewers generally mash on the drier side, around 65 to 67 degrees Celsius, rather than chasing body.

Hopping for clarity and pine, not haze

Classic varieties, Cascade, Centennial, Simcoe, Chinook and Columbus, go in for bittering, flavour and aroma, often across multiple additions late in the boil and a whirlpool stand, with dry hopping added after fermentation for aroma. Unlike New England IPA, the goal is not biotransformation-driven juiciness but a clean, resinous hop character that reads immediately as bitter and piney.

Dry hop contact time is usually shorter than a hazy IPA's, a few days rather than a week or more, since the aim is aroma lift rather than the deep, saturated hop flavour a hazy beer chases.

Fermentation and clarity

A clean American ale yeast, fermented around 18 to 20 degrees Celsius, produces minimal fruity ester so the hops stay the focus. Many West Coast IPA brewers also use finings or cold crash aggressively to achieve the bright, clear appearance the style is judged on, unlike hazy styles where cloudiness is intentional.

Any haze in a finished West Coast IPA, unless it is genuine chill haze from cold storage, is usually read as a flaw rather than a stylistic choice, which is the exact opposite of how New England IPA is judged.

Freshness is not optional

Hop aroma compounds degrade steadily from the moment hops are picked, and a West Coast IPA six months past its packaging date tastes noticeably duller and more one dimensional than a fresh one, even if nothing was technically wrong with how it was brewed. This is the main reason serious hop forward breweries obsess over turnaround time from tank to glass rather than over shelf life.

Bitterness holds up better than aroma over time, which is a small mercy, but a West Coast IPA that has lost its top notes and kept only a flat bitterness is not really doing what the style is for any more.

Brewing and drinking it in India

Fresh hops are the real constraint here, since almost all hop varieties used for this style are imported and lose potency in transit and storage before they even reach an Indian brewery. Buying in smaller, more frequent batches and storing hops properly, vacuum sealed and frozen, matters more for this style than for almost any other on this list. Common questions

What is the difference between West Coast and New England IPA?

West Coast IPA is clear, dry and bitterness forward, using classic American hops for pine and resin. New England IPA is hazy, soft and juicy, with lower bitterness and hop character built through biotransformation and heavy dry hopping.

Why is West Coast IPA so bitter?

It is built around high alpha acid hop varieties added generously and often, with a dry malt finish that lets the bitterness stand out rather than being cushioned by residual sweetness.

Does West Coast IPA need to be drunk fresh?

Ideally yes. Hop aroma fades over weeks to months, and this style depends heavily on that aroma, so it is best enjoyed as close to its packaging date as possible.

What hops define the West Coast IPA style?

Cascade, Centennial, Simcoe, Chinook and Columbus are the classic varieties, chosen for pine, citrus and resin character alongside high bittering potential.
