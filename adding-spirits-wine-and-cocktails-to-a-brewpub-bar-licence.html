Adding spirits, wine and cocktails to a brewpub bar licence

Many Indian brewpubs start with house beer only and soon face requests for whisky, wine and cocktails. Adding them can lift the average bill and bring in groups who would otherwise go elsewhere. It also adds a licence, a stock system and a new set of skills. Here is how to think it through.

Why brewpubs add a full bar

The case is usually about groups. A table of six where two people do not drink beer will often choose a venue that also serves spirits, wine or cocktails. A full bar keeps those tables. It can also lift the average spend per head, fill quieter hours with cocktail promotions and give the kitchen a wider range to pair with.

The case against is focus. A brewpub sells its own beer at a margin no bought-in product can match, because it controls the production cost. Every spirit you pour is a product you bought at a duty-paid price. If cocktails cannibalise beer sales, total margin can fall even as revenue rises.

The licensing side, at process level

In most Indian states, a microbrewery or brewpub licence covers making and selling beer on the premises. Selling other liquor usually needs an additional bar or restaurant licence category from the state excise department. Whether you can hold both and on what terms varies by state. So do fees, conditions, hours and the paperwork required. Our guides to the microbrewery licence in India and craft beer rules by state cover the beer side.

The typical process looks like this, though the details depend on the state:

  1. Check the current excise notification for your state on whether a brewpub can add a bar licence.
  2. Confirm your premises meet the conditions, which may cover floor area, seating, kitchen and distance rules.
  3. Prepare the application with property, identity, fire and municipal documents.
  4. Pay the applicable fees and wait for inspection and approval.

Rules and fees change, so work with a licensing consultant who handles your state regularly. Treat any number you hear secondhand with suspicion until you see it in the current notification.

Stock records and daily excise work

Spirits and wine bring their own excise records. Bought-in liquor usually has to come from authorised suppliers with duty paid, often carrying state labels or holograms. You record purchases, daily sales and closing stock, often bottle by bottle. These registers are separate from your brewing records and inspectors will check both. Our guide to the daily excise routine for brewpubs shows what a beer-only routine looks like. A full bar roughly doubles it.

The bar itself

Spirits in Indian bars are commonly sold by the 30 ml or 60 ml peg, measured with a jigger. That measure is where money disappears. Free pouring, generous staff and unrecorded comps all show up as stock that does not match sales. Plan for:

  • Jiggers at every station and a rule that every pour is measured.
  • A bartender who can make a short list of cocktails consistently. Good ones are hard to hire and harder to keep.
  • An ice machine sized for peak service. Ice runs out faster than beer in an Indian summer.
  • Glassware for spirits, wine and cocktails, plus storage and washing capacity for it.
  • Weekly stock counts reconciled against POS sales.

Our guide to reducing beer loss at the bar applies just as much to spirits.

Margins: compare per serve, not per bottle

Work out the contribution of each drink in rupees per serve: selling price minus the cost of what goes in the glass, including garnish, mixers and ice. Compare that with a pint of your house beer. In many brewpubs the house beer earns more per serve than a cocktail, once you count bartender time and wastage. The full bar earns its place by bringing in tables that would not have come, not by beating beer on margin.

A middle path: beer cocktails

Before taking on a full bar, some brewpubs test demand with beer cocktails and shandies made from their own beer, plus non-alcoholic options for guests who do not drink. Check with your licensing adviser what your current licence allows. If guests take to them, you have evidence. If they still ask for whisky by name, you have a clearer case.

Making the decision

Model the full year before applying: extra licence costs, the bartender's salary, stock tied up in bottles, extra excise work and the likely change in covers and spend. Run a pessimistic case where beer sales drop as cocktails rise. If the numbers only work in the optimistic case, wait.

Most founders are good at one half of this, the bar or the spreadsheet. The decision needs both, plus a clear view of excise. That combination is exactly what a short business course is for.

Common questions

Does a brewpub licence allow selling spirits?

Usually not on its own. Most states need a separate bar or restaurant licence category for spirits and wine. Check your state's current excise notification with a licensing consultant.

Are cocktails more profitable than house beer?

Not always. House beer often earns more per serve once bartender time, ice, mixers and wastage are counted. A full bar mainly earns by bringing in groups.

What extra records does a full bar need?

Separate excise registers for purchased liquor, covering purchases, daily sales and closing stock. Requirements vary by state.

Can I serve beer cocktails without a bar licence?

It depends on your state and licence terms. Ask your licensing adviser before putting them on the menu.
