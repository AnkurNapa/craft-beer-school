Witbier

Few beer styles have as dramatic a survival story as witbier. Here is how it is built, and why it very nearly was not around to taste at all.

3 min read · Updated June 2023 · Craft Beer School

A style that nearly vanished

Witbier, meaning white beer for its pale, hazy appearance, is a centuries old Belgian style centred on the town of Hoegaarden, but by the 1950s the last local producer had closed and the style was effectively extinct. Pierre Celis, a former milkman who had worked briefly at that last brewery, revived it in 1966, rebuilding the recipe from memory and founding what became the modern Hoegaarden brewery.

Without that one act of stubbornness, witbier as a widely known style might not exist today. It is a useful reminder that beer traditions are more fragile than they look.

What to expect in the glass

Witbier pours a pale, hazy straw to gold, cloudy from unmalted wheat and often a small proportion of oats, with a soft white head. The aroma leads with coriander seed and orange peel, giving a bright, slightly citrusy, subtly spicy character quite different from any other wheat beer.

Bitterness is low, 10 to 20 IBU, body is light, and the finish is refreshingly tart and dry rather than sweet. This is a beer built for warm weather drinking, which makes it a genuinely good fit for Indian conditions.

Unmalted wheat, not just wheat malt

Unlike hefeweizen, which uses malted wheat, traditional witbier uses a portion of raw, unmalted wheat alongside barley malt, along with oats in many recipes for extra body and a silky mouthfeel. Unmalted wheat lacks the enzymes needed for conversion on its own, so it relies on the barley malt in the mash to provide those enzymes.

This grain bill contributes to witbier's distinctive hazy, almost milky appearance, along with a slightly tart, grainy character that malted wheat alone does not produce in quite the same way.

A mash with this much unmalted grain and oats can run slowly, so a longer rest or a step mash with a protein rest is often worth the extra time to keep the lauter moving smoothly.

Coriander and orange peel: getting the spice right

Crushed coriander seed and dried curaçao orange peel, usually added late in the boil, give witbier its signature bright, citrusy spice character. The amounts matter enormously here: too much and the beer tastes like a spiced novelty drink, too little and the whole point of the style disappears.

Curaçao orange peel specifically, rather than regular orange peel, is traditional because of its more bitter, less sweet character, which suits the beer's dry finish better than a sweeter citrus peel would.

Some Indian brewers substitute dried peel from local citrus varieties when curaçao peel is hard to source, and the results can be genuinely good, though the flavour will shift slightly from the traditional Belgian character and is worth treating as its own variation rather than a straight substitute, and worth labelling honestly as one.

Fermentation adds its own layer

A relatively clean Belgian ale yeast strain is used, fermented on the warmer side, which contributes a light fruity ester character alongside the spice additions rather than competing with them. Some witbier is also brewed with a touch of lactic sourness, either from a small bacterial culture or a lactic acid addition, adding to the refreshing tartness some versions show.

Getting the yeast character right without letting it overpower the coriander and orange is part of the balancing act that makes witbier harder to brew well than its light, easy drinking reputation suggests.

How it compares with hefeweizen, and serving it

Both are hazy wheat beers, but where hefeweizen gets its character from yeast alone, witbier gets its character from deliberate spice additions on top of a cleaner fermentation. Tasted side by side, hefeweizen reads as banana and clove, witbier as citrus and coriander, a genuinely useful comparison for anyone learning to identify wheat beer styles by taste.

Serve witbier cold, around 4 to 7°C, in a tall glass, ideally with a light orange garnish if you want to lean into tradition rather than novelty. It is an easy, forgiving beer to pour for guests who claim they do not like beer.

A common mistake is substituting malted wheat for the raw, unmalted wheat the style calls for. Raw wheat brings a distinct silky, slightly starchy body and a persistent haze that malted wheat cannot fully replicate. Oats are sometimes added in small amounts for extra body, but they should support the wheat character, not replace it.

Common questions

Who brought witbier back from extinction?

Pierre Celis, a former brewery worker, revived the style in 1966 after the last traditional producer had closed, founding what became the modern Hoegaarden brewery.

What spices are traditionally used in witbier?

Coriander seed and dried curaçao orange peel, both usually added late in the boil, giving the beer its characteristic bright, citrusy, subtly spicy character.

Why is witbier cloudy?

The haze comes from unmalted wheat and often oats in the grain bill, along with suspended yeast, none of which is filtered out before serving.

Is witbier the same as hefeweizen?

No. Both are hazy wheat beers, but witbier's character comes from added coriander and orange peel with a relatively clean yeast, while hefeweizen's banana and clove flavour comes entirely from its yeast strain.
