Fortified wine: how port-style and sherry-style wines are made

Fortified wines are made by adding grape spirit to wine. Add it during fermentation and you keep sugar, as in port. Add it after and you get a dry wine that ages under yeast or air, as in sherry.

What fortification means

A fortified wine is wine with distilled spirit added, usually a neutral grape spirit, to raise its strength. Most end up somewhere around 15 to 22% ABV. The extra alcohol does two jobs. It stops or prevents yeast activity and it protects the wine during long ageing.

Port and sherry are protected names tied to regions in Portugal and Spain. A wine made elsewhere by the same methods is described as port-style or sherry-style. Labelling rules decide what you can call it.

Port-style: stop the fermentation early

Port is made mainly from red grapes. The must starts fermenting normally, with intense extraction, traditionally by treading in shallow stone tanks called lagares. Because the fermentation will be short, the winemaker has to pull colour and tannin out quickly.

Then comes the defining step. When roughly half the sugar has fermented, the wine is run off and mixed with grape spirit of around 77% ABV, roughly one part spirit to four parts wine. The jump in alcohol kills or stops the yeast. What remains is a sweet, strong wine, typically around 19 to 22% ABV.

Timing is the whole skill. The cellar team tracks the falling density of the must, often every few hours, then fortifies at the sweetness they want. Late by a few hours, the wine is drier than planned.

Port styles come from ageing

  • Ruby. Aged briefly in large vessels and bottled young. Deep colour, fresh red fruit.
  • Tawny. Aged for years in smaller casks with slow oxygen exposure. Colour fades to amber and the flavour turns nutty and dried fruit.
  • Vintage. From a single good year, bottled young and aged in the bottle for many years. It throws a sediment and needs decanting.

Sherry-style: ferment dry, then fortify

Sherry is made mainly from the Palomino grape, which gives a fairly neutral base wine. It is fermented fully dry first, then fortified. How far it is fortified decides its path.

Fino and manzanilla. Fortified lightly, to about 15 to 15.5% ABV. At that strength a film of yeast called flor can grow on the surface. Flor protects the wine from oxygen and gives the sharp, bready, almond character of a fino, partly through the acetaldehyde it produces. Casks are deliberately not filled to the top, so flor has air to grow.

Oloroso. Fortified higher, to around 17 to 18% ABV, where flor cannot survive. The wine ages with slow oxygen contact, turning darker, rounder and nutty.

Amontillado starts under flor, then loses it and ages oxidatively, so it sits between the two. Sweet sherries come from dried grapes, such as Pedro Ximénez, or from sweetening dry styles.

The solera system

Sherry is usually aged in a solera: rows of casks holding wine of different average ages. Wine for bottling is drawn from the oldest row, which is topped up from the next oldest and so on back to the youngest, which receives new wine. No cask is ever emptied, so each bottling is a blend across many years and the style stays consistent.

Nobody should expect to run a full solera in year one. A small producer can still use the same principle of fractional blending to keep a product steady.

Other fortified styles

Port and sherry are the best known, but there are other approaches. Madeira is fortified and then deliberately heated, traditionally in warm lofts and today often in heated tanks, which gives a cooked, caramel, high-acid wine that keeps for a very long time once opened. The French vins doux naturels, such as some Muscats, are fortified during fermentation in the same way as port, keeping the fresh grape sweetness.

The common thread is a choice about when spirit goes in and how much air the wine sees afterwards. Those two decisions produce most of the variety in the category.

What this means for an Indian producer

Indian heat suits oxidative styles better than delicate flor wines. Flor prefers cool, humid cellars and struggles in warm, dry ones. A port-style wine also uses ripe, high-sugar grapes well, which warm regions produce easily.

The practical questions are regulatory and financial. Fortified wine needs a spirit supply, a clear licence position and a label your state will approve. Wine categories and permitted alcohol levels vary by state, so check the current notification with a licensing adviser. It also ties up stock for years. Common questions

What is the difference between port and sherry?

Port is fortified during fermentation, so it keeps natural grape sugar and is sweet. Sherry is fermented dry first and then fortified. Only some sherry styles are sweetened.

What is flor?

A film of yeast that grows on the surface of fino and manzanilla sherries. It protects the wine from oxygen and gives a sharp, bready character.

Can an Indian winery call its wine port?

Port and sherry are protected names. A wine made the same way elsewhere is usually described as port-style. Labelling rules in each state and market decide the wording.
