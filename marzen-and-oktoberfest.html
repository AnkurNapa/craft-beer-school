Märzen and Oktoberfest beer

Marzen and Oktoberfest have become almost interchangeable names, but they describe two different points in a beer's history, and the difference is worth knowing before you brew one.

3 min read · Updated March 2022 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Why it is called Marzen at all

Marzen means March in German, and the name comes directly from brewing practice before refrigeration existed. Brewers made a stronger, higher gravity beer in March, the last cool month before summer heat made brewing risky, then stored it in cold caves or cellars through the warm months to drink from September onward.

That stored beer became the natural choice to serve at Munich's Oktoberfest celebrations, which is how the style and the festival became linked in most people's minds.

The beer has actually changed since then

Traditional Marzen was a deep amber, fairly strong lager, often above 6% ABV, with a rich, toasty malt character from Vienna and Munich malts. What is served as Oktoberfest beer, or Festbier, at the modern festival is noticeably lighter and paler, closer to 5.1 to 6% ABV, developed because festival organisers wanted something drinkers could manage across a long day of steady consumption.

Both versions are brewed today under different names, traditional Marzen amber and full, modern Festbier paler and lighter, and a good brewery will tell you which one it is actually making rather than using the names interchangeably.

What to expect in the glass

Traditional Marzen pours a deep amber to light copper, with a rich, bready, toasty malt aroma and a clean, moderate bitterness that sits well below the malt sweetness, typically 18 to 24 IBU. There is little to no hop aroma competing with the malt character.

It should taste warming and substantial without being heavy or cloying. A well made Marzen finishes cleaner than its malt forward aroma suggests it will.

Head retention should be good and long lasting, a small but telling sign of a clean fermentation and correctly handled proteins from the mash.

Building the malt bill

Vienna and Munich malts together provide the toasty, biscuity depth that defines the style, often alongside a smaller portion of standard pilsner malt to keep the beer from becoming too dense. A touch of caramel malt is sometimes used for added colour and a light toffee note, though restraint matters here as much as in any malt forward lager.

Mash temperature typically runs a little higher, around 66 to 68°C, to leave enough body behind to support the beer's malt intensity through a long lagering period.

Getting the ratio of Vienna to Munich malt right is largely a matter of taste rather than a fixed rule, and brewers vary it to push the finished beer either toward a lighter, more biscuity character or a deeper, richer one without changing anything else in the recipe.

Fermentation and the long lager rest

A clean lager yeast ferments at the usual cold range, 9 to 13°C, and the beer then benefits from an extended lagering period, traditionally months rather than weeks, which is exactly why the original March brewing schedule existed: to give the beer time to mature before autumn.

A rushed Marzen loses much of what makes the style worthwhile, since the smoothing and mellowing effect of long cold storage is central to how the malt character comes across in the finished beer.

A small brewery without enough tank space to tie one up for months at a time can still get a reasonable approximation with a shorter but genuinely cold lagering period, though anyone who has tasted a properly aged version will notice the difference immediately.

Where it sits next to Vienna lager and bock

Marzen shares its toasty malt character with Vienna lager, though Marzen usually runs a shade stronger and maltier. Compared with bock, Marzen stays noticeably lighter and less intensely malty, sitting in between an everyday lager and a genuinely strong one.

Serve it a little warmer than a pale lager, around 8 to 10°C, in a stein or a simple lager glass, and it holds its character well even without an ice cold pour.

If you are labelling a Marzen for an Indian audience unfamiliar with the name, describing it plainly as a full bodied amber lager alongside the historical context usually does more for a customer's understanding than the German name alone, especially on a first tap list.

Common questions

Is Oktoberfest beer the same as Marzen?

Traditionally yes, but the beer actually served at the modern Munich festival, often called Festbier, is now lighter and paler than historical Marzen. Many breweries still label full strength amber versions as Marzen.

Why is Marzen linked to the month of March?

Before refrigeration, brewing safely stopped once summer heat arrived, so brewers made a stronger batch in March and stored it cold through summer to drink from autumn onward, which is how the beer and the season became connected.

How strong is a traditional Marzen?

Traditional versions often sit above 6% ABV, noticeably stronger than the paler modern Festbier served at the festival today, which usually runs closer to 5.1 to 6%.

Does Marzen need a long lagering period?

Yes, ideally. Extended cold storage smooths the malt character and is part of what originally made the style worth the trouble of brewing months in advance.
