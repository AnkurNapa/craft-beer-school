Training your palate

Palate training is a skill, built the same way any other skill is: deliberate practice, feedback and repetition against known references.

3 min read · Updated September 2024 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. Why casual drinking does not train a palate
  2. Start with single reference compounds
  3. Build vocabulary before you build speed
  4. Practise against a fixed structure
  5. Test yourself, do not just taste
  6. Where a structured course actually helps

Why casual drinking does not train a palate

Drinking beer without paying deliberate attention builds familiarity, not precision. Palate training requires actively naming what you notice, checking that name against a known reference, and getting corrected when you are wrong, the same feedback loop behind learning almost any other sensory skill worth having.

This mirrors how ear training works for musicians: listening to music casually for years does not reliably build the ability to name an interval or a chord, but structured, deliberate practice against known reference notes does, usually far faster than people expect.

Start with single reference compounds

Spiked beer kits, covered properly in our guide to off flavour spiking kits, dose a clean base beer with a known fault at a known concentration. Tasting these gives you a fixed, correct reference for what diacetyl, DMS or acetaldehyde actually taste like in real life, rather than guessing from a written description on a page somewhere.

Journaling alongside tasting, even briefly, forces you to commit to a specific description rather than leaving an impression vague in your head, and the act of writing itself seems to improve recall the next time you meet a similar aroma.

Even five minutes of focused comparison against a real reference beats an hour of unfocused casual drinking, which is worth remembering on a busy week when a full session is not realistic.

Build vocabulary before you build speed

Our guide on building your beer aroma vocabulary covers the family based approach worth using here too, before worrying about being fast. Get comfortable placing a smell in a broad category before worrying about pinning it to one single exact word straight away.

Keep a running personal glossary as you go, noting not just the word but the specific beer and moment you first placed it accurately. Coming back to this list after a few months is one of the clearest ways to see how far your palate has actually come.

A short warm up taste of something neutral, plain water or a mild cracker, before starting a training session helps reset the palate from whatever was eaten beforehand, giving each session a more consistent, comparable starting point.

Practise against a fixed structure

Use the same scoresheet, such as the BJCP scoresheet, every time you taste with intent, even well outside a competition setting. A fixed structure stops you skipping the categories you find harder, which is exactly where most of the real learning tends to happen over time.

Group practice sessions, where several people taste the same beer and compare notes immediately afterwards, tend to accelerate learning faster than solitary tasting, since hearing someone else name something you missed entirely is one of the fastest ways to expand your own working vocabulary.

Revisiting a beer style you found difficult a few months earlier is a useful checkpoint. Noticing details that were simply invisible to you the first time round is one of the clearest, most satisfying signs that the training is actually working.

Test yourself, do not just taste

A triangle test run occasionally against your own tasting notes is a genuine check on whether your palate is actually improving, rather than you simply feeling more confident without becoming any more accurate in practice.

Rotate through different beer styles deliberately during training rather than sticking to favourites, since a palate trained only on IPAs will struggle when it eventually meets a delicate lager or a complex barrel aged sour.

Tasting alongside someone more experienced, even informally over a couple of beers, tends to speed up early progress considerably, since a specific correction offered in the moment sticks far better than a general rule read afterwards.

Where a structured course actually helps

Self-directed practice gets you a long way on its own, but working through calibrated spiked samples with an instructor correcting your identification in real time compresses months of trial and error into a handful of focused sessions. That structured feedback loop is the part that is genuinely hard to build entirely by yourself.

None of this needs expensive equipment or a laboratory. A notebook, a handful of reference ingredients from the kitchen, and a willingness to be wrong occasionally are enough to start, and most of the real progress happens in those early, slightly uncertain sessions.

Common questions

How long does it take to train a decent palate?

Most people notice a real improvement within a few weeks of deliberate, structured practice, though refining it further is an ongoing process for even experienced tasters.

Do I need spiking kits to train my palate?

They help a great deal because they give you a fixed, correct reference for a fault rather than a guess, but structured tasting and note taking on ordinary beer builds real skill too.

Is palate training only useful for judges and brewers?

No, anyone who wants to get more out of drinking beer benefits, though brewers and judges get the most direct professional value from it.

Can palate sensitivity actually improve with practice?

Yes, the ability to detect and name specific compounds improves substantially with deliberate, repeated practice, even though some baseline sensitivity is genetic.
