How to taste strong beers like imperial stout and barleywine

Imperial stouts, barleywines and other strong beers are built for slow, careful drinking. Serve and taste them like wine or whisky and you will find layers that a quick, cold gulp never shows.

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

What counts as a strong beer

Most beer sits between 4 and 6% ABV. Strong beers start around 8% and can reach 12% or more. The common styles include imperial stout, barleywine, Belgian strong dark ale, Belgian tripel, doppelbock and old ale. Our guides to imperial stout and barleywine cover the styles in detail.

At these strengths alcohol is part of the flavour. It brings warmth, sweetness and body. Your job as a taster is to judge whether it is well integrated or harsh and hot.

Serve warmer than you think

Strong beers show their character at cellar temperature, roughly 10 to 14 °C. Straight from a fridge at 3 to 4 °C, the aromas stay locked in the glass and the beer tastes thinner and more bitter than it is.

In practice: take the bottle out of the fridge 20 to 30 minutes before pouring. In a warm Indian room it will reach a good temperature fairly quickly, so do not leave it out for hours. Hold the glass by the stem or base so your hand does not heat it too fast. Our guide to beer serving temperature gives figures for other styles too.

Glass and pour size

Use a snifter, a tulip or a stemmed wine glass. The curved bowl gathers aroma and the narrow rim focuses it towards your nose. A straight pint glass lets the aroma drift away.

Pour small. A 100 to 150 ml serving of a 12% beer contains as much alcohol as a much larger pour of ordinary beer. Sharing a bottle between two or three people is normal for strong beers and makes the evening more enjoyable.

Look at the beer before you smell it. Imperial stouts should be near black with a tan or brown head, though at high strength the head is often thin and fades quickly. Barleywines range from deep gold to dark amber and are usually clear. Swirl and watch the legs on the glass: thick, slow legs suggest high alcohol and residual sugar. A strong beer that pours with almost no carbonation can still be correct. Many are lightly carbonated by design. A soft, low fizz helps the rich flavours come through.

Taste slowly as it warms

Smell before you sip. Swirl the glass gently to release aroma, then take short sniffs. Look for the main families of flavour: roast, chocolate and coffee in imperial stout; toffee, dried fruit, bread crust and sherry in barleywine; and in barrel-aged versions, vanilla, coconut, wood or spirit.

Take small sips and let the beer coat your tongue before swallowing. Notice the body, the sweetness and the warmth. Then wait. Strong beers change as they warm in the glass, often over half an hour or more. Taste again at intervals and note what has changed. Many of the best notes, especially dark fruit and chocolate, only appear once the beer has warmed a few degrees.

Faults to watch for

  • Hot, solvent alcohol. A burning, nail-polish edge rather than a gentle warmth suggests fermentation ran too warm or the yeast was stressed.
  • Cloying sweetness. If the beer is syrupy with nothing balancing it, fermentation probably stopped too early.
  • Wet cardboard or papery notes. These suggest damaging oxidation. Some sherry-like oxidation can be pleasant in an aged barleywine. Cardboard rarely is.
  • Harsh, acrid roast. A little burnt edge suits an imperial stout. An ashtray finish does not.

Strong beers are often aged before release or cellared at home. Ageing can soften harsh notes, but it also increases oxidation, so a beer kept for years is a different beer from a fresh one.

Pairing and pacing

Strong beers work well with rich food or as a dessert in their own right. An imperial stout with a dark chocolate cake or a barleywine with strong aged cheese is a classic match. A barleywine also sits well alongside a rich, caramelised milk cake. Taste the beer on its own first, then with food.

Treat strong beer like a good whisky: something to share and sip, not something to drink in a session. Drink water alongside and plan your evening and journey home accordingly.

If you want to pick apart alcohol warmth from a fusel fault, or judge how well a strong beer has aged, you need reference points. That calibration is what structured sensory training gives you.

Common questions

What temperature should imperial stout be served at?

Around 10 to 14 °C, roughly cellar temperature. Take it out of the fridge 20 to 30 minutes before serving.

What glass is best for barleywine?

A snifter, tulip or stemmed wine glass. The curved bowl concentrates aroma and makes it easier to swirl.

Why does strong beer taste different as it warms?

Aroma compounds become more volatile as the beer warms, so more reach your nose. Sweetness and body also feel different at higher temperatures.
