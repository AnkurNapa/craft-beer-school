Turnkey brewery projects: what the contract should cover

A turnkey brewery project hands you a plant that runs. Whether yours does depends on the scope written into the contract, line by line. This guide lists what to look for before you sign.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Rahul Baliyan

Written byRahul BaliyanBrewing Science Instructor · Delhi & North India
Point of contactChatty GirijaQuestions about this guide? WhatsApp her

What turnkey should mean

A turnkey contract makes one party responsible for delivering a working brewery. You should not need to coordinate the brewhouse maker, the cellar supplier, the utilities contractor and the packaging line yourself. That is the promise.

The word has no legal definition, though. One supplier uses it for the brewhouse and tanks only. Another includes civil work, utilities, effluent treatment and a first brew. Two quotes with the same label can differ by half the plant.

So the first job is not comparing prices. It is writing down, on one page, what the working brewery you want to own looks like on the day of handover. Then hold every bidder to that page.

Scope: the list to test every quote against

Ask each bidder to mark every line below as included, excluded or owner's responsibility.

  • Brewhouse, fermentation and conditioning vessels plus the cold side
  • Utilities: steam or hot water, refrigeration, compressed air, water treatment and electrical supply
  • Packaging: kegging, canning or bottling with a flash or tunnel pasteuriser if the beer needs one
  • Effluent treatment and the pollution control approvals that go with it
  • Civil work, floors, drains and building services
  • Laboratory and quality equipment
  • Installation, commissioning and the first brews
  • Training of your brewers and the documentation you receive

Items marked owner's responsibility are the ones that cause delays. Power connection, water supply, the building itself and statutory approvals usually sit with the owner. Know which ones are yours. Know too the date by which the supplier needs each of them.

Where the supplier's duty ends

Handover is a moment. The contract should define it by a test rather than a date. A good handover clause says the plant must brew a stated number of batches to an agreed specification, hit agreed volumes and pass agreed checks before it is accepted.

Think about what that test covers. Brewhouse efficiency, cold side losses, fill levels and utility consumption can all be measured. If the contract says only that the equipment is delivered and installed, a plant that cannot make saleable beer still counts as complete.

Also settle who owns the recipe work. Scaling a recipe to a new brewhouse takes trial brews. Those brews need malt, hops and yeast. Decide who pays for the raw materials and who signs off the beer.

Payment, delays and changes

Tie payments to milestones you can see: equipment dispatched, installed, commissioned, accepted. Keep a retention until the performance test passes. Avoid large advances that leave you with no hold on the supplier when something is late.

Write down what happens if the supplier is late and what happens if you are late. Owner delays are common, because buildings and approvals rarely run to plan. A contract that is silent on them turns every slip into an argument.

Agree how changes are priced. Brewers always change something once the building is half finished. A written change procedure costs nothing now and saves the project later.

Why an independent eye helps

A supplier's turnkey team knows its own equipment well. It is less motivated to tell you that the tank farm is oversized for your first two years or that the packaging line is the real bottleneck.

We work as an independent adviser on new plants of any size, from small brewpubs to large breweries. That includes reviewing a proposed scope, checking a contract against the plant you need and supporting the project through commissioning. Work runs remotely by video call and shared documents, with site visits by arrangement.

If you are still at the planning stage, the project report comes first. Our guide on a project report for a brewery expansion shows what that document should contain. Our note on an independent review of a project report explains how to test one.

Licensing and approvals

In India, alcohol is a state subject, so licences, excise rules and some approvals differ from state to state and change over time. A turnkey supplier may help with drawings and documents, but the licence is yours. Work with a licensing adviser or a lawyer in your state. Check the current notification before you commit to a site.

Common questions

Does turnkey mean the supplier takes all the risk?

No. It means one party is responsible for delivering the scope written in the contract. Risks that the contract leaves with the owner, such as approvals, site readiness and power supply, stay with the owner.

Should the contract include recipe development?

Ideally it covers trial brews on the new plant and a sign-off against a written specification. Decide in advance who pays for the raw materials used in those trials.

Can you review a turnkey quote we already have?

Yes. We can compare a quote against the plant you need and list gaps in scope, handover tests and payment terms. This can be done remotely from the documents.
