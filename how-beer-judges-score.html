How beer judges score a beer

Competition judging is a structured, repeatable process, not personal taste. Here is what a judge is actually doing behind that scoresheet.

3 min read · Updated March 2024 · By Ankur Napa and Rahul Baliyan

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer
Written byRahul BaliyanBrewing Science Instructor · Delhi & North India

Judging is against a style, not against personal taste

A judge's first job is establishing what style the beer is entered as, then evaluating how well it represents that style, not whether the judge personally enjoys drinking it. A judge who dislikes stouts still has to score a well made stout highly if it hits the marks a good example of the style should hit, setting personal preference aside entirely.

This distinction takes real discipline to hold onto consistently. It is one of the things separating a trained judge from an enthusiastic drinker with strong opinions. Both can describe a beer, but only one is checking it against a written external standard rather than their own taste.

The categories a judge weighs

Most competition scoresheets, including the widely used BJCP scoresheet, break a beer into aroma, appearance, flavour, mouthfeel and overall impression, each weighted differently and scored separately before being added together at the end. This structure forces a judge to evaluate systematically rather than forming one gut reaction and working backward to justify it after the fact.

A judge working through several dozen entries in a single session also has to guard against palate fatigue, the same phenomenon covered in our guide to setting up a home tasting, which is one reason competitions build in water, plain crackers and scheduled breaks between flights as a matter of course.

Two judges, one beer, at the same time

Competitions typically pair two or more judges on the same flight of beers, tasting independently and then comparing notes before finalising their scores together. This catches individual bias or a palate having an off day. It is one reason a single judge's opinion at a bar is worth far less than a trained panel's consensus reached in competition.

Calibration sessions, where a panel of judges tastes the same beers together and compares scores before a competition, exist specifically to narrow the gap between different judges' internal standards, so a beer does not score wildly differently depending purely on who happens to taste it.

Faults are the fastest way to lose points

An obvious process fault, diacetyl, oxidation, an infection, will cost a beer more points than a merely unremarkable but clean example of the same style. Our guide to beer off flavours covers the common faults judges are trained to detect and name specifically rather than simply marking a beer down as off without further explanation.

Some competitions also weigh consistency across a brewer's submitted entries if more than one is submitted, since a brewery capable of reliably reproducing a clean result across multiple batches demonstrates a different kind of skill than a single lucky standout beer.

A judge will also note whether a fault is consistent throughout the glass or fades as the beer warms, since a fault that disappears with a little air and warmth often points at a different cause than one that persists from first sip to last.

Written feedback matters as much as the number

A good scoresheet gives the brewer specific, actionable feedback: this diacetyl suggests an early cold crash, this astringency suggests an over-long sparge during mashing. A brewer who only sees a final number learns nothing about what to fix next time, which is why the written comments are often more valuable to a working brewery than the score itself.

Many home brewing competitions in India now follow this same scoresheet driven format, giving local brewers feedback benchmarked against an international standard rather than only local opinion, which is valuable if you plan to enter a beer beyond your own city.

What this means if you want to judge

Judging is a trained skill built on calibrated tasting, a shared vocabulary and repeated practice against known reference samples, not innate talent someone either has or does not. Our guide on running a triangle test covers one of the exercises used to build and verify that calibration in a rigorous way.

None of this judging structure is designed to intimidate a home brewer entering their first competition. It exists to make sure the feedback that comes back is fair, specific and consistent, regardless of which judge happened to taste the beer that day.

Common questions

Do judges score based on whether they personally like the beer?

No, they score against the style guidelines the beer was entered under, regardless of personal preference for that style.

Why does a competition use more than one judge per beer?

To catch individual bias or an off day and reach a more reliable consensus score than any single palate could produce alone.

What costs a beer the most points?

An obvious process fault, such as diacetyl, oxidation or infection, typically costs more than a beer simply being unremarkable but clean.

Is the written feedback more useful than the score?

For a brewer trying to improve, often yes. Specific comments point at a process cause, while a bare number does not.
