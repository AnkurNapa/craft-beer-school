Company, LLP or partnership as the structure for an Indian brewery

Most founders choose a structure because a friend did, or because one was cheaper to register. For a brewery, the choice affects licences, investors and personal risk for years. Here is how the three common options compare.

Why this decision matters more for a brewery

In most businesses, changing structure later is a nuisance. In the liquor trade it can be a serious problem, because excise licences are generally issued to a specific entity at a specific premises. Move the business from a partnership to a company and you may be applying for licences again, with the delay that brings. That is the main reason to get the structure right before you apply.

None of what follows is legal or tax advice. Take it as the list of questions to bring to a chartered accountant and a lawyer.

Partnership firm

A partnership under the Indian Partnership Act is the simplest to set up: a partnership deed, registration and a bank account. Compliance is light.

The catch is liability. Partners are personally liable for the firm's debts without limit. A brewery carries a lease, equipment loans, supplier credit and excise obligations, so an unlimited personal exposure is a real risk. A partnership also cannot issue shares, which makes outside investment awkward. We rarely see it suit a brewery beyond a very small, family-run start.

Limited liability partnership

An LLP, formed under the Limited Liability Partnership Act, keeps the flexibility of a partnership but limits each partner's liability to their agreed contribution, subject to the usual exceptions for fraud and personal guarantees. Compliance is lighter than a company: annual filings with the registrar and an audit only above certain size thresholds.

LLPs work well for a small group of founders who will fund the business themselves and do not plan to bring in equity investors. The limits show up when you want to raise money. Most angel and venture investors want shares in a company. An LLP cannot issue shares or employee stock options.

Private limited company

A private limited company under the Companies Act gives limited liability, a separate legal identity and the ability to issue shares to investors and options to key staff. It is the structure investors and many banks expect.

It also carries the most compliance: a statutory audit every year whatever the size, board meetings, annual filings, registers and a company secretary's help as you grow. Budget for that cost from the start. For a brewery that hopes to raise money, open more outlets or one day sell, this is usually the right choice despite the paperwork. Our guide to brewery funding options explains why investors care.

How each structure looks to the excise department

Excise applications usually ask for the entity's registration documents, the names of partners or directors and their background checks, proof of premises and financial standing. A company or LLP has a clear record at the registrar, which some applicants find makes the file simpler. A partnership deed needs to show who the partners are and what they contribute.

Changes later matter too. Adding a partner, bringing in a new director or selling shares may need the excise department to be informed or to approve, depending on the state. Ask your licensing adviser how each structure is treated in your state before you register, because the answer can tip the decision.

Tax is a moving target

Companies and LLPs are taxed differently. The way profits reach the owners differs too: salaries, partner remuneration, dividends or profit shares. The rates and rules change through budgets, so any comparison you read online may be out of date. Ask your CA to model the first three years under each structure using your own financial plan, including how you expect to take money out.

Do not let a small tax difference drive the decision. Liability, funding and licences usually matter more.

Personal guarantees cut through all of it

Limited liability protects you from the business's debts, but banks lending to a new brewery usually ask the founders for personal guarantees. Landlords sometimes do too. Whatever structure you choose, read what you are guaranteeing personally. A company with directors who have signed personal guarantees on every loan is not as limited as it looks.

Write the founders' agreement at the same time

The structure is the container. The agreement between founders is what holds it together: who puts in what, who does what, how decisions are made, what happens if someone leaves and how shares are valued then. Write it when you register, while everyone still agrees. Our guide to brewery partner agreements lists the clauses that matter most.

Founders who understand these choices before they meet the CA ask better questions and avoid paying to restructure a year later.

Common questions

Which structure do most Indian breweries use?

Breweries that plan to raise outside money usually choose a private limited company. Founder-funded ventures sometimes choose an LLP for lighter compliance.

Can I change structure after getting excise licences?

It can be difficult, because licences are usually issued to a specific entity. A change may mean fresh applications. Check your state's rules with a licensing adviser before deciding.

Does an LLP protect me from all liability?

It limits liability to your agreed contribution, but personal guarantees you sign for loans or leases still apply, as do exceptions such as fraud.

Is a partnership ever right for a brewery?

Rarely, because partners carry unlimited personal liability and the firm cannot issue shares to investors.
