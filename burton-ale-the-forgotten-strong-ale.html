Burton ale, the forgotten strong ale

Burton upon Trent is remembered as the home of pale ale and IPA. Before that and alongside it for decades, the town made a strong, rich ale that bore its name. We look at what Burton ale was and where its echoes survive.

A town known for strong beer

Burton upon Trent, in the English Midlands, became a brewing centre long before the nineteenth century. Its well water is unusually high in calcium sulphate (gypsum), which helps produce clear, crisp, firmly bitter beers. That water became so famous that brewers elsewhere later added gypsum to copy it, a practice called Burtonisation. Our guide to building a Burton water profile explains the chemistry.

In the eighteenth century, Burton brewers shipped strong ales down the river and on to ports around the Baltic. Those beers, rich and sweet-leaning, were the first Burton ales to win a wide reputation.

What Burton ale was

Burton ale was a strong, malty, well hopped ale, darker than pale ale, usually amber to brown. Strength varied over time and between breweries, but the stronger grades were well above everyday beers. Breweries often sold several grades under letter marks, with more letters meaning a stronger beer.

It was sweet-leaning but not cloying, because brewers hopped it generously, partly to help it keep. Historical recipes suggest a beer built mostly on pale malt, with colour from the long boil and later from some darker malts or sugars. Exact flavour is hard to pin down, because tastes and ingredients changed over two centuries.

Pale ale took the spotlight

In the nineteenth century, Burton brewers turned to pale ale and India Pale Ale. Burton's hard water suited those bright, bitter beers perfectly. They became the town's fame. Burton ale did not vanish. It carried on alongside pale ale for many decades as a winter or premium strong beer.

Over the twentieth century, Burton ale faded. Wartime restrictions pushed beer strengths down across Britain. Drinkers moved to lighter bitters and lagers. The name slipped out of common use, which is why most drinkers today have never heard of it.

Where its echoes survive

Burton ale did not leave a single direct heir, but several modern styles share its DNA:

  • English barleywine: strong, malty, amber to brown, generously hopped.
  • Old ale: a rich, warming dark ale, sometimes aged.
  • Strong bitters and winter warmers: fuller, darker cousins of everyday bitter.

When you drink an English barleywine, you are tasting something close to the strong end of the Burton ale family. Our barleywine guide covers that line in more detail.

What a Burton ale might taste like today

A few modern brewers have recreated Burton ales from historical brewing logs. Expect a strong beer, often well above 6% ABV, amber to chestnut brown, with:

  • A rich malt middle of toffee, bread crust and dried fruit.
  • Firm bitterness and earthy English hops.
  • A clean, slightly mineral dryness from sulphate-rich water.
  • Warming alcohol without heat.

If you cannot find one labelled Burton ale, an English barleywine or a strong old ale is the nearest substitute. Taste it thinking about history: a beer built to travel and to keep.

The water and the yeast behind the flavour

Two things made Burton beers taste like Burton beers. The first was the water. Sulphate sharpens hop bitterness and gives a dry, clean finish, so a strong, sweet ale brewed with Burton water would have tasted less heavy than the same recipe brewed with soft water. Brewers in London, with softer water, made sweeter, rounder strong ales.

The second was yeast and fermentation. Burton brewers became known for the union system, a set of linked oak casks that let fermenting beer push its yeast out and settle clean. It was used mainly for pale ales, but it shows how seriously the town took clean fermentation. Clean fermentation lets malt and hops speak clearly, without the fruity or sulphury notes of a rougher brew. A modern drinker tasting a recreated Burton ale might notice how tidy it feels for its strength.

Why history helps your palate

Knowing where a style came from changes how you taste it. Once you know Burton water was rich in sulphate, you notice that dry, mineral edge in strong English ales. Once you know these beers were brewed to keep, you understand why they carry so much hop bitterness for their sweetness.

That kind of context turns tasting from guessing into reading. You start asking why a beer tastes the way it does, not only what it tastes like.

Taste history responsibly

Strong ales deserve small pours. Serve at 12 to 14 °C in a tulip or a stemmed glass, about 150 ml at a time. Have water alongside, eat something and do not drive afterwards. A strong ale shared between two or three people is a better tasting than one person working through a bottle.

Your first step: pour an English barleywine or old ale, look for the sulphate dryness and the hop bitterness under the malt. Then imagine it leaving Burton by river barge.

Common questions

What is Burton ale?

A strong, malty, well hopped English ale from Burton upon Trent, darker than pale ale, popular from the eighteenth century until it faded in the twentieth.

Is Burton ale the same as IPA?

No. Burton also became famous for pale ale and IPA, but Burton ale was a separate, darker and stronger beer.

What modern beer is closest to Burton ale?

English barleywine and strong old ales are the nearest modern relatives.
