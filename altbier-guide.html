Altbier

Altbier is Dusseldorf's answer to the same brewing puzzle Cologne solved with Kolsch, and the two cities have never quite agreed which answer was better.

4 min read · Updated March 2024 · Craft Beer School

Old in name, deliberate in method

Altbier, meaning old beer, keeps the traditional top fermenting ale brewing method that most German regions largely abandoned in favour of the newer bottom fermenting lager techniques through the 1800s. Dusseldorf brewers kept faith with their ale tradition, but added cold conditioning borrowed from lager brewing to clean up the finished beer, arriving at a genuine hybrid style rather than a straightforward holdout, and one that still divides opinion on which approach actually produced the better beer.

The result sits in the same technical territory as Kolsch, top fermenting yeast plus cold conditioning, but the two cities took the idea in noticeably different flavour directions.

What to expect in the glass

Altbier pours a clear copper to deep amber, with a malty, bready aroma and a firm, clean bitterness, generally 25 to 50 IBU, noticeably more assertive than Kolsch. Fruity ester character is present but restrained, since cold conditioning smooths much of it away, leaving a beer that finishes dry and crisp for its malt intensity.

This balance of amber malt richness with a genuinely firm, lager like clean bitterness is what distinguishes altbier from most other top fermenting styles, which usually carry more noticeable ester character.

The malt and hop balance

A base of pale malt with a meaningful proportion of Munich and sometimes a touch of caramel malt builds the toasty, biscuity depth typical of the style, while German noble hops, often Spalt or Perle, provide the firm bitterness altbier is known for. This hop level is well above Kolsch's more restrained bitterness, part of what gives altbier its drier, more assertive character.

Getting the malt to hop balance right is the main technical challenge, since too much malt sweetness against that firm bitterness can taste unbalanced rather than the clean, dry finish the style is meant to have.

A well hopped altbier can taste deceptively similar to an English bitter on first sip, and the two styles reward a side by side tasting, since the differences in yeast character and cold conditioning become obvious once you know to look for them.

Fermentation and cold conditioning

A top fermenting altbier yeast strain works at a relatively cool ale temperature, generally 15 to 18°C, similar to Kolsch, followed by an extended cold conditioning period, sometimes several weeks near 0°C, that gives the beer much of its clean, rounded character. This step is what separates altbier from an ordinary amber ale using a similar grain bill.

Skipping or shortening the cold conditioning leaves a rougher, more obviously ale like beer that misses the specific clean character the style depends on.

A common mistake is fermenting altbier at ordinary ale temperatures and skipping the cold rest that gives it its name. Ferment cool for an ale strain, around 15 to 18 degrees Celsius, then lager it near freezing for two to four weeks. Skip that step and the beer reads as a plain brown ale rather than the crisp, clean drink Dusseldorf brewers are known for.

A friendly rivalry with Kolsch

Dusseldorf and Cologne, roughly 40 kilometres apart, have a long standing, mostly good natured rivalry over which city's hybrid beer is better, altbier's malty depth against Kolsch's pale crispness. Ordering the wrong beer in the wrong city's traditional beer hall is a reliable way to get some good natured ribbing from the regulars.

Both styles emerged from the same underlying brewing logic, applied to different local preferences, which makes tasting them side by side a genuinely useful lesson in how much a malt bill and hop rate alone can change a beer built on the same fermentation idea.

Serving it well

Serve altbier at 7 to 10°C, traditionally in a small, straight sided cylindrical glass, similarly sized to Kolsch's stange though the style and tradition remain distinct. It suits richer food than Kolsch does, roasted meats and hearty stews, where its firmer malt and bitterness can stand up to stronger flavours.

It also travels a little better than a delicate pale lager in Indian heat, since its fuller malt character and firmer bitterness give it slightly more margin before flavour loss becomes obvious.

This makes it a genuinely sensible choice for a brewery that needs a beer to hold its character through distribution rather than being sold entirely at the tap, without sacrificing the depth of flavour a purely session style might have to sacrifice for the sake of shelf life.

Common questions

Is altbier an ale or a lager?

Technically an ale, brewed with a top fermenting yeast, but cold conditioned afterward in a way that gives it a cleaner finish than most ales, similar in principle to Kolsch.

Why is it called altbier?

Alt means old in German, referring to the traditional top fermenting brewing method Dusseldorf kept using even as most German regions moved toward the newer bottom fermenting lager techniques.

How is altbier different from Kolsch?

Altbier is darker, maltier and more firmly bitter, while Kolsch is pale, light and only mildly bitter, even though both use a similar hybrid ale and cold conditioning process.

What food pairs well with altbier?

Richer, heartier food, roasted meats and stews in particular, since altbier's firmer malt and bitterness can stand up to stronger flavours better than a lighter beer can.
