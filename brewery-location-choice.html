Choosing a brewery location

A brewery site decision is really several decisions at once: is this location legally zoned for it, can it support the utilities, and does the state's excise policy even make sense here. Get these wrong and no rent discount fixes it.

3 min read · Updated December 2025 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

  2. State excise policy shapes the map more than geography does

Zoning comes before everything else

Confirm that your target site's land use classification actually permits manufacturing or, for a brewpub, food service with on-site alcohol production, before you fall in love with the location and start imagining the finished taproom. Industrial or commercial zoning requirements differ by state and by municipality, and a site that looks perfect but sits in a purely residential zone is a dead end regardless of how good the rent is or how much you like the neighbourhood.

State excise policy shapes the map more than geography does

Some states are considerably more brewery-friendly than others in licensing timelines, fee structures and the permitted scale of operation, and this can matter more to your business than being close to any particular city or supply chain. A founder set on a specific state for personal reasons should go in with open eyes about that state's specific excise environment, covered in excise duty on beer in India, rather than assuming all states are roughly equivalent in how they treat a new brewery.

Utilities: the practical filter

Water quality and supply reliability, power load capacity and outage frequency, and effluent discharge options together often eliminate more candidate sites than rent or zoning do, once you actually test them thoroughly rather than trusting a landlord's description. See brewery utilities: water, power and steam for what to test before committing, and treat any site that fails on utilities as disqualified, however good it looks otherwise on paper.

Access for both supply and sales

A brewhouse needs road access wide enough for grain deliveries and CO2 or keg trucks, which rules out some otherwise charming, tucked-away locations that look appealing for entirely different reasons. If you are running a taproom or brewpub on the same site, footfall and visibility matter for direct sales, but this pulls in a different direction to industrial access, since manufacturing zones are rarely high-footfall areas by design.

Decide which need is primary for your specific business model, since a site cannot maximise both at once without real compromise.

Weighing it all together

Rank zoning legality and utility viability as disqualifying factors, checked first, then weigh rent, access and footfall against each other for the sites that pass that initial filter. A slightly more expensive site that passes water, power and zoning cleanly beats a cheap one that needs retrofitting on all three, since retrofitting almost always costs more than the rent saved.

Since zoning and excise rules vary by state and municipality and change over time, confirm current requirements directly with the local authority before signing anything at all.

Visiting a site more than once

A single daytime visit misses a lot: check the site again after dark if you plan late trading hours, check it during peak local traffic if delivery access matters, and check it after heavy rain if drainage or flooding could be a concern in that area. Talk to neighbouring businesses about noise complaints, past flooding or power problems, since they will tell you things a landlord may not think to mention or may prefer not to.

The extra visits cost an afternoon each. A site that fails on one of these conditions after the lease is signed costs considerably more than an afternoon to fix.

Bring your brewer or a technically minded partner along on at least one of these visits, since they will notice practical problems, a low doorway, a narrow drain, an awkward loading angle, that a founder focused on rent and layout can easily miss.

A site that survives this level of scrutiny and still feels right is a far safer bet than one chosen quickly on a single good impression during a single, well-timed visit, and the time invested rarely feels wasted once the brewery is actually running and the same doorway or drain matters every single day for as long as the brewery occupies that site.

Visit during the worst weather your city has, not just a pleasant afternoon. A site that looks perfect in January can flood its loading bay in the monsoon, or lose power for hours in peak summer when the grid is under strain. A generator quote taken in fair weather often underestimates what a real dry season demands.

Common questions

What is the first thing to check about a potential brewery site?

Zoning. Confirm the land use classification actually permits manufacturing or, for a brewpub, on-site food and alcohol service, before evaluating anything else about the site.

Does the state matter more than the city for a brewery?

Often yes. State-level excise policy on licensing, fees and permitted scale can shape your business more than which city within that state you choose.

Can a brewery site fail on utilities alone?

Yes. Poor water quality or supply, unreliable power, or no viable effluent discharge option can disqualify an otherwise good site regardless of rent or location.

Should I prioritise footfall or industrial access?

It depends on your model. A packaging-focused brewery prioritises supply and logistics access, while a brewpub needs footfall too, and the two needs often pull toward different types of site.
