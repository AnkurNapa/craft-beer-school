Regenerative barley and sustainable malt: what a brewer can ask for

A brewery's footprint does not stop at the gate. Most of what goes into the mash tun was grown in a field. How that field was farmed is starting to matter to buyers, investors and export partners. You do not need a sustainability team to start asking better questions.

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Malt is the largest ingredient decision you make

A brewery can cut its power bill and recycle every carton and still leave its biggest raw material untouched. By weight, malt is the main thing a brewery buys.

Run the numbers. An all-malt beer around 12 °Plato takes roughly 16 to 18 kg of malt per hectolitre. A brewpub making 1,000 hl a year therefore buys about 17 tonnes of malt, all of it grown on farmland somewhere. Emissions from growing and malting that grain fall in what carbon accounting calls Scope 3, the emissions in your supply chain rather than your own building.

That is why the question of how barley is farmed has moved from agronomy journals into brewers' purchase conversations.

Regenerative is a set of farming practices, not a label

There is no single legal definition. In practice regenerative farming means a handful of habits aimed at building soil rather than mining it: less ploughing, cover crops between harvests, crop rotation, organic manure in place of some synthetic fertiliser and careful water use.

The case rests on soil carbon. Cultivated soils have lost 50 to 70% of their original carbon, reported in The New Brewer, the Brewers Association's journal. Practices that keep roots in the ground and residue on the surface put some of it back.

Barley fits well. It is a short-season crop that slots into rotations. In India it is grown as a winter (rabi) crop, sown around October and November and harvested by March or April.

Indian barley already has a story to tell

Most Indian malting barley comes from Rajasthan, Haryana, Punjab and western Uttar Pradesh. It generally needs fewer irrigations than the wheat it competes with for the same fields, which matters in districts where groundwater is falling.

In the rice and wheat belt of Punjab and Haryana, the big environmental problem each autumn is paddy straw burning. Sowing the next crop straight into the residue with machines such as the Happy Seeder is a reduced-tillage practice that keeps carbon in the field and smoke out of the air. A barley crop sown that way is part of the solution to a problem every north Indian has breathed.

The catch is traceability. Grain from many farms usually meets in one silo, so a maltster may know the region but not the farm. Our guide on sourcing malt and hops in India explains how supply chains work here.

Ask five questions your maltster can actually answer

Start with what a maltster can tell you today rather than a certificate they cannot give you.

  1. Which district and season was this lot grown in?
  2. Which barley variety, two-row or six-row?
  3. Can you keep a lot from named growers separate from the general silo?
  4. Do those growers use reduced tillage, residue retention or a legume in the rotation?
  5. Can you share fertiliser and irrigation figures per hectare for that lot?

Then hold the malt to the same specification as always: moisture around 4 to 5%, protein, extract and colour inside your usual limits. A sustainable malt that wrecks your brewhouse yield is not sustainable for the brewery. Read the malting process guide if those numbers are new to you.

The premium is smaller per litre than it sounds

Identity-preserved malt costs more because it has to be stored and tracked separately. Work it out per litre before you decide.

Use your own invoice price. For the arithmetic, take malt at ₹60 a kilo and 17 kg per hectolitre. That is ₹1,020 per hectolitre, or about ₹10.20 per litre of beer. A 10% premium on the malt adds about ₹1 per litre. On a 400 ml pour that is roughly 40 paise.

It depends on three things: the premium your maltster quotes, the share of malt in your grist (adjunct-heavy beers use less) and whether anyone, from an export buyer to an investor, is asking for the data.

Data is the real product

The barley itself may look identical. What you are paying for is the record: where it grew, how it was farmed and what that did to emissions per tonne. Without the record, a regenerative claim on a label is a promise you cannot back.

Keep it modest. Say what you know, such as the district, the variety and the practice. Do not convert it into carbon numbers you cannot verify.

Your first step: at your next malt order, ask question one and question three from the list above. Write down the answers.

Common questions

What is regenerative barley?

Barley grown with practices that rebuild soil, such as reduced tillage, cover crops, crop rotation and less synthetic fertiliser. There is no single legal definition, so ask what practices sit behind the claim.

Does regenerative malt taste different?

Not in any reliable way. Malt flavour comes from the variety, the growing season and the malting process. Judge it on the usual specification sheet.

Where is malting barley grown in India?

Mainly Rajasthan, Haryana, Punjab and western Uttar Pradesh, as a winter crop sown around October and November and harvested by early spring.

Can a small brewpub ask for sustainable malt?

Yes. Start by asking where a lot was grown and whether it can be kept separate. Even a small brewery buying 15 to 20 tonnes a year is a customer whose questions shape what maltsters offer.
