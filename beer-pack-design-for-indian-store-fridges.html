Designing beer packs for Indian liquor store shelves and fridges

A design that looks sharp on a laptop often vanishes in a real Indian wine shop. Here is what the shelf and the fridge do to your pack and how to design so it still gets picked.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

What a real store looks like

Walk into a typical liquor store in Bengaluru, Pune or Gurugram and look at the beer. The fridges are glass-door units packed tight, often with condensation on the inside of the glass. Shelves behind the counter hold rows that staff can reach, not rows a shopper can browse. Lighting is a mix of tube lights and fridge LEDs. Many shoppers point and ask rather than pick up a can.

That means your pack is usually seen from one or two metres away, through glass, at an angle, mostly by its top third. Supermarkets with open chillers give you more, but even there the shopper sees a face, not the whole can.

Design for the top third

When cans sit in rows, only the top of the front face is reliably visible. Put the brand mark and the beer's name there. Put the style name close by in a size that reads from a metre. The middle and bottom of the can can carry illustration and colour, but they may be hidden by the can in front or the fridge shelf lip.

Test it simply: line up six printed mock-ups in a row behind a sheet of glass with a little water misted on it. Stand two metres away. If you cannot tell which beer is which, neither can the shopper.

Contrast beats detail

Fine line illustration and subtle tints look lovely on screen. Behind a misted door they turn grey. Strong contrast between the brand block and the background carries further than any amount of detail. Dark cans with dark type are the most common failure we see on Indian shelves.

Range colour helps too. If each beer in your range has its own strong colour band in the same place, a shopper can find "the green one" again without remembering the name. More on that in our guide to can design.

Size, format and the stocking clerk

Indian beer shelves were built around 650 ml bottles and 500 ml cans. A 330 ml can sits low and gets lost between them. Staff often stack by size, so small formats end up on the bottom shelf. You cannot always change the format, but you can help:

  • Make the brand readable on the can top if the store stacks cans flat or in trays.
  • Print outer cartons and four-pack sleeves so the brand faces out when they are stacked in a storeroom or on a floor display.
  • Give the sales team a one-page planogram suggestion for the stores they visit. Staff follow pictures.

Heat, sweat and scuffing

Indian distribution is hard on packs. Cans travel in warm trucks, get loaded into a fridge, come out sweating when a shopper picks one, go back in. Paper labels wrinkle and peel. Cheap shrink sleeves scuff at the shoulder. Matt finishes pick up fingerprints and water marks.

Ask your printer about wet-strength labels or sleeves rated for condensation. Then rub-test a sample with a damp cloth. A pack that looks worn after a week in the trade quietly tells the shopper the beer is old too, which matters most for hoppy beer where freshness is the point.

Register the label before you print thousands

Most states require beer labels to be approved or registered with excise before sale. FSSAI rules fix what must appear. A design change after approval can mean re-registration. Build the approval time into your launch plan. Print a short run first if your format allows. Rules and timelines differ by state, so check the current process with a licensing consultant. Our guide to beer label rules in India lists the usual mandatory items.

A store check before you sign off

  1. Mock up the final can at true size, printed on the real substrate if possible.
  2. Put it in an actual store fridge with the owner's permission and photograph it from two metres.
  3. Ask three people who have never seen it to find it.
  4. Chill it, take it out and leave it on the counter for ten minutes, then check the print.

That last test is the one teams skip. The design brief rarely mentions condensation, but the shopper's hand always does.

Then go back after a month. Visit the same stores, see where staff actually put your beer and how the packs look after a few weeks of handling. Note which stores keep you in the fridge and which leave you on a warm shelf. That record is useful for the next print run and for the sales team, who can ask for better placement with a photo in hand instead of a complaint. Small breweries rarely do this follow-up, though it is the cheapest market research there is.

Common questions

Is a 330 ml can a bad idea in India?

Not at all, but it is easier to lose on a shelf built for larger packs. Plan carton design and in-store placement with the store rather than relying on the can alone.

Should every beer in the range share one design?

Share a system, not identical cans. Keep the brand block in the same place and give each beer its own strong colour so shoppers can tell them apart from a distance.

Matt or gloss finish for Indian fridges?

Gloss handles condensation and handling better. Matt looks premium but shows water marks and fingerprints, so rub-test a sample before you choose it.
