Grassy and vegetal notes from too much dry hop contact

Grassy, vegetal and green tea notes are among the most common complaints about modern hoppy beer. They are rarely a hop quality problem. They usually come from how long, how warm and how hard the hops sat in the beer.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

What the fault tastes like

The descriptors run from freshly cut grass and green leaves to hay, spinach water, green tea. It often comes with a harsh, drying bitterness on the finish that lingers more than clean hop bitterness.

It masks fruit. A beer dosed with plenty of tropical varieties can taste of a garden shed instead. Panels often describe it simply as "green".

Where it comes from

Hop cones are plant material. Besides the oils and resins brewers want, they carry chlorophyll, polyphenols and other leafy compounds. In a short dry hop most of the oil extracts within the first day or two at fermentation temperature. Leave the hops longer and the beer keeps pulling out the leafy fraction, while the bright oil character has already peaked and begins to fade.

Polyphenols in particular add astringency and that green, tannic finish. Pellets break apart completely in beer, which speeds extraction of everything, wanted or not.

The variables that push it over

  • Contact time. Most of the aroma is in the beer within roughly 24 to 72 hours. A week or more on the hops is where grassy notes tend to build.
  • Temperature. Warmer dry hopping extracts faster, both good and bad. A charge at 20 °C behaves differently from one after a crash to 4 °C.
  • Dose. Very heavy rates, the kind used in many New England IPAs, bring more vegetal matter per litre.
  • Agitation. Rousing hops with CO2 or recirculation speeds extraction, so it shortens the safe window.
  • Hop age and storage. Pellets that warmed up in transit or storage lose oil, leaving relatively more plant matter. In India, where imported hops can sit in hot godowns, this is a real risk.

Contact during active fermentation has its own effects. Our guides on biotransformation and hop creep explain why the timing changes both aroma and final gravity.

Tasting it apart from other faults

Grassy notes are easy to confuse with three other things. Hop burn is a hot, peppery scratch at the back of the throat, usually from suspended hop particles in young beer. Acetaldehyde is green apple rather than green leaf. Oxidised hops smell of cheese or old cardboard rather than fresh grass.

A useful bench check: take a sample from the fermenter each day after the dry hop goes in, filter it through a coffee filter and taste the series side by side. The day the fruit stops rising and the grass starts is your contact limit for that recipe, at that temperature, in that vessel.

How to set contact time in practice

Plan the dry hop around removal, not addition. If your fermenter has a dump valve, drop the hop sludge from the cone after two or three days. In a unitank, a crash to near 0 °C settles hops quickly and slows further extraction. Some breweries split one large charge into two smaller ones, each with a short contact, to get more aroma with less leafy pickup.

Keep the cone cold if you cannot remove hops promptly. A layer of hop matter sitting warm at the bottom of a tank is extracting the whole time. Taste the beer before packaging and again a week after. Grassy character sometimes softens slightly in the package, but harsh polyphenol bitterness usually does not.

Training the panel to call it

Grassy notes are subjective until a panel shares a reference. Steeping a few pellets in a neutral lager for several days at room temperature makes a crude but useful example. Taste it next to the same beer with a one-day contact. Everyone on the panel should be able to name the difference blind before you rely on their release notes.

That calibration step is where most brewery panels are weakest. It is also the kind of training that changes what reaches the taproom.

Record the dry hop details on the batch sheet next to the panel score: variety, lot, rate in grams per litre, temperature, contact hours and how the hops were removed. After a few batches the pattern usually becomes obvious. Then you can change one variable at a time instead of guessing at all of them.

Common questions

How long should dry hops stay in the beer?

Many brewers find most aroma extracts in one to three days at fermentation temperature. Taste a daily series for your own recipe and vessel to find the point where fruit peaks and grassy notes begin.

Does cold dry hopping prevent grassy notes?

It slows extraction of everything, so the window before grassy character builds is longer. It also extracts aroma more slowly, so contact time needs adjusting rather than ignoring.

Can old hops cause grassy flavours?

Hops that have lost oil through warm storage leave relatively more plant matter in the beer, which can tip it towards hay or grass. Badly stored hops more often smell cheesy.
