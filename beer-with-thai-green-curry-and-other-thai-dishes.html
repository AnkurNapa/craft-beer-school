Beer with Thai green curry and other Thai dishes

Thai food balances hot, sour, salty and sweet in nearly every dish, with plenty of fresh herbs. That balance gives beer a lot to work with. Here is how we would pair beer with the most popular Thai dishes.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Hot, sour, salty and sweet together

Thai cooking is built on balance. Chilli brings heat. Lime juice and tamarind bring sourness. Fish sauce brings salt and savoury depth. Palm sugar brings sweetness. Then come fresh aromatics: lemongrass, galangal, makrut lime leaf, Thai basil and coriander.

That makes Thai food friendlier to beer than you might expect. The sweetness in the dish softens the edge of hop bitterness. The fresh herbs suit hop aroma. The one trap is heat: Thai bird's eye chillies are small but fierce. Strong, bitter beers make them hotter. Lighter, softer beers make them easier to enjoy.

Green curry wants a wheat beer

Thai green curry is made from a paste of fresh green chillies, lemongrass, galangal, garlic, shallot and makrut lime, cooked in coconut milk with chicken or vegetables and Thai basil. It is often hotter than red curry, despite the gentle colour.

Coconut milk is rich, so carbonation helps. The chilli means bitterness should stay low. A Belgian witbier is our top choice: its orange peel and coriander seed are natural partners for lime leaf and lemongrass. A German hefeweizen at 8 to 15 IBU is equally good, especially with chicken. Serve either at about 5 to 7 °C.

Red curry, massaman and panang

Red curry uses dried red chillies, so it is rounder and less herbal than green. Panang is thicker, sweeter and nuttier. Massaman, with its cardamom, cinnamon, potato and peanuts, shows Persian and Indian influence and is the mildest of the three.

For red curry, an amber lager or a Vienna lager brings toasty malt that matches the dried chilli. With massaman, a brown ale or even a Belgian dubbel in a small glass suits the warm spice and peanuts. Panang falls between the two: try an amber ale at around 5% ABV.

Tom yum and som tam need a crisp edge

Tom yum is a hot and sour soup with lemongrass, galangal, lime leaf, chilli and lime juice, often with prawns. Som tam, the green papaya salad, pounds papaya with chilli, garlic, lime, fish sauce and palm sugar. Both are sharp, hot and fresh.

These want a crisp, clean beer. A German Pilsner at 4.5 to 5% ABV works well. A light lager is fine too, since its job is mostly to refresh. A tart beer such as a gose can echo the lime, though with a very hot som tam we would stick with a gentle lager.

Pad thai, larb and the grilled dishes

Pad thai, stir fried rice noodles with tamarind, fish sauce, egg, peanuts and bean sprouts, is sweet, sour and savoury with little heat. Larb, a minced meat salad with toasted rice powder, mint, lime and chilli, is fresh and punchy. Grilled dishes such as gai yang (grilled chicken) bring char.

Pad thai is easy: a Helles or a pale ale. The pale ale's citrus hops suit the tamarind and lime. Larb is a good place for a hoppy beer if the chilli is moderate. Its mint and lime pair well with an aromatic pale ale at 30 to 40 IBU. For grilled chicken, a Vienna lager matches the char.

Thai restaurants in India often adjust the heat for local tastes, sometimes upwards. Ask how hot a dish is before you choose the beer. A green curry made mild suits a pale ale. The same curry made Thai hot needs the witbier or a hefeweizen.

Where IPA fits and where it does not

A fresh, hoppy IPA is tempting with Thai food because tropical and citrus hop aromas echo lime and lemongrass. With milder dishes like pad thai or chicken satay, it can be a great match. With green curry or som tam at full heat, 60 IBU of bitterness plus bird's eye chilli is a harsh combination.

If you want hop aroma without the bitterness, look for a hazy IPA or a pale ale with lower bitterness. Freshness matters a lot too, because faded hops lose the bright aromas that make the match work. Our guide to beer and food pairing basics explains how bitterness and heat interact.

Taste the herbs in the beer

Here is a small exercise. Crush a makrut lime leaf and smell it. Then smell a witbier. Then smell a Pilsner. You will find the witbier's orange and coriander notes sit close to the lime leaf, while the Pilsner smells more grassy and floral. That aroma link is what makes a pairing feel natural. If something in the beer smells off, like green apple or nail varnish, our guide to acetaldehyde explains one common fault.

Your first step: order a green curry and a pad thai, pour a witbier and a pale ale and taste each beer with each dish. You will find one combination you prefer. You will also know why.

Common questions

What beer goes best with Thai green curry?

A Belgian witbier or German hefeweizen. Their low bitterness calms the green chillies. The witbier's citrus and coriander match lime leaf and lemongrass.

Does IPA go with Thai food?

With mild dishes like pad thai, yes. With very hot curries or som tam, high bitterness plus chilli is harsh, so choose a lower bitterness beer.

What beer suits tom yum soup?

A crisp German Pilsner or light lager. Its clean finish refreshes after the hot and sour broth.
