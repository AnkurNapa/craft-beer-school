Building a brewery brand that tourists remember

Tourists are a different audience from regulars. This guide shows how to design the visit, the pack and the follow-up for people who may never return.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa and Chatty Girija

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer
Written by and point of contactChatty GirijaQuestions about this guide? WhatsApp her

On this page

  1. Know what visitors are looking for
  2. Anchor the brand in a place
  3. Make the visit easy
  4. Design for the memory
  5. What to take home
  6. Keep the relationship going
  7. Staff are part of the brand
  8. Partnerships with the local trade

Know what visitors are looking for

Visitors want something local, something they can tell a friend about and something they can take home. They have limited time and little context. They do not know your styles, your town's history or which beer to order first. Your job is to make those choices easy and pleasant.

Start by listening. Ask a few visitors why they came. Count how many arrive from hotel recommendations, map listings or walking past. That tells you where to focus.

Anchor the brand in a place

A brand that could be anywhere is easy to forget. Use what is real about your location: a local ingredient, a neighbourhood name, a building, a view, a food that goes well with your beer. Never invent a legend or a history that is not true. Visitors can tell. Local customers will say so.

One strong, honest story is enough. Put it on the wall, on the menu and in one sentence your staff can say.

Make the visit easy

Signs should be readable from the street in at least the languages your guests speak. A tasting flight of small pours with a card that names each beer, the style and one flavour word does more than any brochure. Offer a non-alcoholic option, water and food, so that groups with mixed tastes can stay together. Always serve responsibly and check ages.

Your website should work for someone searching on a phone in a foreign city: hours, map, what to try and how long a visit takes.

Design for the memory

People remember a distinct image, a simple name and a feeling. Think of a bold can, a clever tap handle or a mark on a glass. Keep one visual idea and repeat it everywhere. A name that is easy to say in several languages helps, so read naming a beer for more than one language.

Photographs matter. Create one corner of the taproom that looks great in a phone picture, with good light and a clear view of your name.

What to take home

A souvenir should be small, light and not breakable. Glasses, caps, stickers and prints do well. Cans or bottles are an obvious choice, but remember airline limits on liquids in hand luggage and local import rules for alcohol, which differ by country. A shop that can pack and ship to a visitor's home country needs licences that vary widely, so check before you offer it.

Do not make the souvenir the whole business. The point is a reminder of a good afternoon.

Keep the relationship going

Offer a sign-up to your newsletter with a clear promise, collected only from adults and with consent. Invite visitors to tag the brewery or leave a review. Reviews on map services bring the next visitors. Thank guests who write them.

If the name travels, protect it. Read protecting a beer name in more than one country for the process.

Staff are part of the brand

A visitor remembers the person who poured the beer. Train bar staff to explain three beers in one sentence each, to suggest a first pour and to recommend somewhere good to eat nearby. A friendly person who knows the answer is worth more than a laminated sheet.

Teach them how to refuse service politely when someone has had enough. Teach them to say no to anyone who cannot show proof of age. Responsible service protects your licence and your name. Give staff a day to taste every beer on the list, so that their enthusiasm is real.

Partnerships with the local trade

Hotels, guesthouses, tour guides and restaurants send visitors your way when they trust you. Visit them. Bring a flight, leave a card and ask what their guests ask for. Offer a short tour at a set time so that a guide can book a group.

Pair with a local food maker for a tasting plate. It gives visitors a story about the place and gives you someone to share the marketing with. Check whether the licensing rules treat joint events differently.

Count visits by week. Ask visitors how they found you. Track which souvenirs sell. Revisit the story every year with fresh eyes.

Turning visitors into a community is a skill in its own right, with a mix of events, service and marketing. A taproom marketing and community course gives you worked examples and the chance to test your plan with other brewers.

Common questions

Do tourists matter more than locals?

They are a different audience, so serve both. Locals give steady custom. Visitors give discovery and word of mouth.

Is a souvenir shop worth it?

It can be, if the items are small and sell without much effort. Track what sells.

Can I ship beer to visitors' home countries?

It is heavily regulated in most places. Check national rules and ask a licensing adviser.

What is the single most useful thing to do?

Make the first five minutes of a visit easy: a clear sign, a simple menu and a friendly welcome.
