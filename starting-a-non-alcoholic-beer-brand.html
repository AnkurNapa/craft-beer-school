Starting a non-alcoholic beer brand

Non-alcoholic beer has become a serious category. It can be sold in places where regular beer cannot. This guide covers how it is made, what goes wrong and what to settle before you launch.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa and Chatty Girija

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer
Written by and point of contactChatty GirijaQuestions about this guide? WhatsApp her

Three ways to make it

Brewers reach a low alcohol level by one of three routes. The first is to limit fermentation, stopping early or using a yeast that ferments little of the wort sugar. The second is to brew a normal beer and remove the alcohol, with vacuum distillation or a membrane process. The third is to blend. Each has costs and flavour effects.

Limited fermentation leaves a sweet, worty character if not handled carefully. Dealcoholisation needs expensive equipment, or a contract partner who has it. It can strip aroma along with the alcohol.

Blending is often used to fine-tune the result. You mix a dealcoholised beer with a little regular beer or with wort to restore body, then check the final alcohol carefully. The quality depends on the base beer you start with, so brew a clean one.

Worty, thin and flat: the flavour problem

Alcohol carries flavour and gives body. Take it away and the beer can taste thin, sweet or like unfermented wort. Good non-alcoholic beers use hops, specialty malt and careful mashing to rebuild body and balance. Higher carbonation can help.

Run tasting panels with the beer next to a regular one. If your taster describes cooked vegetables, cereal or honey, the wort character is showing. Our guide to non-alcoholic beer where alcohol is restricted covers the market side.

Hops are a tool here. Late additions and dry hopping add aroma without adding alcohol. A little acidity from a lactic fermentation or acid addition can lift flavour and add a sense of dryness. Trial small changes one at a time and write them down.

Stability and safety

Alcohol helps protect beer from spoilage. Without it, residual sugar can feed bacteria and yeast. Non-alcoholic beer usually needs tight hygiene, a low pH, pasteurisation or sterile filtration and careful packaging. Low dissolved oxygen matters because stale flavours appear quickly.

Test every batch for microbiology and packaged shelf life. Test the alcohol level too, with a method accurate at low values.

Pasteurise in the can or bottle if you can. Tunnel pasteurisation is a standard way to protect low-alcohol beer, though it needs equipment or a service. Flash pasteurisation before filling is an alternative. Whichever you choose, track the heat treatment and keep samples for shelf-life testing.

The labelling question

The words allowed on the label depend on the country. A common cut-off for non-alcoholic is 0.5 per cent ABV, but some countries use a different figure. Some reserve alcohol-free for products with no measurable alcohol. Terms such as low alcohol, alcohol-free and non-alcoholic can each carry a legal definition. Check with the national regulator or a licensing adviser.

Where alcohol is banned, local law comes first. Read what a beer label must show for the wider label rules.

The rules also cover how you describe the product in advertising. A term allowed on a label may be restricted in a promotion. Where alcohol advertising is limited, a non-alcoholic brand that looks too much like a beer brand may be treated as one. Ask before you print.

Equipment and where to make it

You can make it in your own brewery, with a contract partner or with a mobile dealcoholisation service where one exists. Compare the capital cost of owning equipment with the per-litre fee of a service. Think about batch sizes and minimum runs. See starting a beer brand with contract brewing and importing brewing equipment.

Whichever route you choose, run a cost per litre on a real batch before you commit. Include losses in dealcoholisation, extra packaging checks and testing. A realistic number from a pilot is worth more than a supplier estimate.

Pricing and positioning

Do not assume the price is lower because the duty may be lower. The cost of production is often higher per litre than a regular beer. Position the beer on taste and occasion: the driver, the person with an early start, the person who wants a drink at a social event without alcohol. Avoid health claims.

Check shelf space. Many shops and bars have few non-alcoholic lines, so a clear spot on the menu matters more than a big range.

Think about your first customers. Restaurants, hotels, gyms, offices and online sales all work differently. See starting a microbrewery in Africa for how local conditions shape a start-up. Read importing brewing equipment if you will buy a dealcoholisation unit.

Common questions

Is non-alcoholic beer completely free of alcohol?

Not always. Many are under a legal limit such as 0.5 per cent ABV. Some countries use other limits and some products are truly 0.0. Check your market.

Do I need a brewery to make it?

No. A contract partner can brew or dealcoholise your beer. Your brand and your quality control stay with you.

Is it cheaper to make than regular beer?

Usually not. Dealcoholisation or controlled fermentation adds cost. Shelf stability needs more care.
