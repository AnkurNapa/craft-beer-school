Oxidised beer

Oxidation is the most common fault in beer that never gets blamed correctly. Learn to spot it and you will drink better beer, because you will send back the wrong bottle instead of the wrong brewery.

3 min read · Updated June 2025 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. What oxidised beer actually tastes like
  2. Where the oxygen actually gets in
  3. Why heat makes it so much worse
  4. How brewers try to prevent it
  5. What you can do as a drinker
  6. Training your palate to catch it early

What oxidised beer actually tastes like

In a pale beer, oxidation shows up as wet cardboard or wet paper, sometimes with a honey or sherry note creeping in underneath. In a darker or stronger beer it can taste almost pleasant at first, a dried fruit or sherry character, before you notice the hop aroma has gone flat and the beer just tastes tired.

The giveaway is not one flavour so much as an absence. A hoppy beer that should smell bright and citrusy but smells like nothing in particular is very often oxidised, even before the cardboard note arrives.

Texture changes too, though it is subtler. Carbonation can feel a little duller on the tongue, and the finish loses the crispness that a fresh beer has. None of this shows up on the label. You only learn it by tasting a lot of beer, some fresh and some not, and comparing notes.

Where the oxygen actually gets in

Beer is not oxidised during fermentation. Yeast consumes oxygen faster than it can do any damage, and a healthy fermentation is close to oxygen free. The risk starts the moment fermentation finishes: at transfer between tanks, during filtration, at the point of carbonation, and at packaging, where every splash and every bit of foam is oxygen being folded into the beer.

Once it is in, oxidation is a slow chemical reaction, not an instant one. That is why a can can taste fine on day one and tired by day sixty, even if it was packaged correctly, and much sooner if it was not.

Why heat makes it so much worse

Temperature accelerates the reaction. A beer held cold from the day it is packaged to the day it is opened will hold its hop aroma for weeks longer than the same beer left in a warm godown or on the back of a delivery van in Indian summer heat.

This is the part that usually gets missed. Most Indian craft beer does not travel in a proper cold chain from brewery to shop to fridge, and a can that spends a few hours at 38 degrees on a loading dock has aged more in that afternoon than it would in a month sitting cold. The brewer did the work. The supply chain undid it.

How brewers try to prevent it

Prevention is the only real fix, because oxidation cannot be reversed once it has happened. Breweries purge tanks and kegs with carbon dioxide before filling them, fill cans and bottles so the beer meets almost no headspace air, and try to minimise every point where beer moves from one vessel to another.

Dissolved oxygen meters have become common kit in serious breweries for exactly this reason: you cannot manage what you do not measure, and a few parts per billion of oxygen picked up at packaging is the difference between a beer that tastes fresh at six weeks and one that does not.

What you can do as a drinker

Buy from a fridge, not a shelf that sits in the sun. Check the packaging or best before date if the can shows one, and prefer the freshest stock rather than the bottle at the back that has clearly been there longest. Cans protect against light entirely and generally hold up better than clear glass, so when you have a choice, take the can.

If you want to taste the difference for yourself, a good exercise is comparing a fresh can of a hoppy beer against one that is a few months past its best. You will not need anyone to tell you which is which.

Training your palate to catch it early

Oxidation is progressive, so the earliest sign is usually the mildest one: hop aroma that reads as muted rather than obviously faulty. Beer lovers who learn to notice that flatness, rather than waiting for cardboard to show up, catch tired beer before it becomes actively unpleasant, and get better at knowing which shops and which brands keep their stock properly cold.

It is a skill you build with repetition, the same way you learn to spot a lightstruck beer after smelling one just once. Keep a couple of cans back from a batch you like, taste one now and one in six weeks at room temperature, and you will have your own reference point rather than someone else's description of one.

Common questions

Can oxidised beer make you ill?

No. Oxidation is a flavour and aroma fault, not a safety issue. The beer simply tastes tired, flat or cardboard like rather than being harmful.

Does oxidation happen the same way in bottles and cans?

The chemistry is the same either way. Cans have the advantage of blocking light completely, but both can pick up oxygen at packaging if the brewery's process or the supply chain lets it in.

Why does my favourite IPA taste different from batch to batch?

Hop aroma fades fastest of all the flavours in beer, so an IPA is often the first style to show oxidation. A batch that travelled or sat warm will taste noticeably flatter than one that stayed cold throughout.

Is a sherry note always a fault?

Not always. In strong, aged styles like barleywine or old ale, a light sherry character can be an accepted part of the style as the beer matures. In a fresh pale ale or lager, the same note is a defect.
