Pricing beer for retail shelves

A can on a shelf carries a price made of taxes, margins and your own cost. This guide shows how to work backwards from the shelf price, so you know what you actually earn before you commit to it.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa and Rahul Baliyan

Start from the shelf, not from your cost

Many founders price by adding a margin to their cost. That is a fair start, but it hides what happens next. Between your invoice and the customer's receipt sit a distributor, a retailer, taxes and perhaps a platform fee. Each takes a share. Work from the shelf price backwards and you see what reaches you.

In India alcohol is a state subject. In many states the pricing of beer, the trade margins and the way taxes are applied are set or controlled by the state. Some require prices to be registered. This differs by state and changes, so confirm the rules for your market with the excise authority or a licensing adviser before you plan a price.

Walking back from the shelf price

Here is an illustration with hypothetical numbers in currency units, for a single can. The shelf price is 200. Say taxes and duties take 60 of it, leaving 140. The retailer keeps 20 per cent of that, 28, so the distributor receives 112. The distributor keeps 15 per cent of what it receives, about 17, so you receive 95.

Your cost is 70 for liquid, can, label and carrier. Contribution is 25 per can. That is a 26 per cent margin on your price. It has to cover your fixed costs. See brewery break-even. Before you set the price, ask which of those layers the state fixes and which you can negotiate. Only then does the arithmetic tell you something you can act on.

Cost-plus, competitor and value

Three views of price matter. Cost-plus sets a floor: do not sell below the cost that keeps you alive. Competitor pricing shows what the shelf will bear for similar beers. Value pricing asks what your customer would pay for what is special about yours: a limited seasonal, a distinctive style, local ingredients.

The honest price lies between the floor and what the best-regarded comparable beers sell for. A craft beer priced at the cheapest end of the shelf makes a statement about quality that the brand may not want to make.

Pack size, multipacks and promotions

A larger pack lowers the cost per litre on packaging and handling. A four-pack can raise the spend per customer. A deep discount, though, spoils the price ladder and teaches buyers to wait. If a retailer wants a promotion, ask who funds it and for how long. Model the promoted price through the whole chain before you agree. At 10 per cent off the shelf in the example above, the contribution falls from 25 to about 12, if taxes stay fixed and every layer keeps its percentage. Half your margin has gone for a 10 per cent price cut. Ask the retailer to share the cost, or decline.

Check the price against the competition

Walk the shelves of the shops you hope to be in. Note the price of every beer in your style, its pack size and its brand position. Convert everything to a price per litre so you compare like with like. A 330 ml can at 200 is 606 per litre. A 500 ml can at 250 is 500 per litre. The bigger can is cheaper per litre and may look better value even at a higher price.

Do the same for the taproom pint and the keg. Your own channels should not undercut each other. See distribution models.

Test before you commit

Pick one shop or one city and run a price for a few weeks. Watch the rate of sale, not only the margin. A price that earns a high margin but sells slowly leaves stock in a warehouse and cash in a stranger's hands. Review the numbers every month. Prices should move with your costs, so recheck contribution each time a supplier raises a rate. Raise prices in small, planned steps at the start of a season or with a new pack. Tell your distributors early. A surprise rise in the middle of a promotion damages trust faster than the extra margin repays it. Keep a log of every price you have charged and the date, so you can see what each change did to volume.

What to write down

Keep one sheet per product: shelf price, taxes, retailer margin, distributor margin, net price, cost, contribution and the volume you need. Update it every quarter. If you want to build that sheet with guidance, the course below covers it from first principles. For the next step, see a 12-month cash flow forecast.

Common questions

Should I price my beer below competitors to win shelf space?

Rarely. A low price teaches buyers that your beer is cheap and erodes margin through every layer. Compete on difference, not on being cheapest.

Who sets the shelf price of beer in India?

That differs by state and changes. In many states prices and margins are controlled or registered. Check with the excise authority or a licensing adviser.

How do I price a seasonal beer?

Price it on its own cost and its own value. A limited release can usually carry a higher price than the core range if the quality shows.

Do I need a different price for online sales?

Often the rules differ for online alcohol sales, which are limited in many places. Check what is allowed in your state before planning.
