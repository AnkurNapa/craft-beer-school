New product development for drinks, from brief to launch

New product development in drinks is less about inspiration than about catching problems early. A short, written process from brief to launch stops a promising recipe dying on a label rejection, a costing surprise or a shelf that is warmer than the lab.

3 min read · Updated September 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

  2. Check the regulatory route before the recipe
  3. Bench trials and the pilot
  4. Shelf life in Indian conditions
  5. Scale-up and costing
  6. Who owns the project

Write the brief first

A brief is one page that everyone signs off before anyone brews. It should say:

  • Who drinks it and when: the occasion, not just the age band.
  • Where it sells: taproom, bars, retail or all three.
  • Target ABV, format and serving temperature.
  • Target cost per litre and the shelf price it has to hit.
  • What it must not be. A low bitterness lager brief that says no hop haze saves an argument later.

In consulting work we often see projects start from a recipe someone loves and work backwards. That is how a drink ends up costing more than its shelf price allows.

Check the regulatory route before the recipe

In India the category of a drink decides which licence covers it, how it is taxed and which label rules apply. A seltzer, a cider, a flavoured beer or a low alcohol beer may each land in a different category. States differ. Check FSSAI labelling and your state's excise rules for label and brand registration before you design the pack. Rules change, so get the current position from a licensing adviser.

Label registration takes time in most states. A new product often needs it in every state where it will be sold. Put that lead time into the launch plan from day one.

Bench trials and the pilot

Start small. Run several variants side by side, changing one thing at a time. Record every input. For beer and cider that means a pilot kit or small fermenters. For RTDs and seltzers it may be blending trials in measuring cylinders.

Taste with a small trained panel, blind. A triangle test, where tasters pick the odd sample out of three, tells you whether two versions are actually different before you argue about which is better. Our guide on how to taste beer covers the basics a panel needs.

Shelf life in Indian conditions

This is the stage most often skipped. A drink that tastes right on day one may taste stale, separated or oxidised after a month in a warm truck and an un-air-conditioned shop.

  • Hold samples cold and warm for several weeks and taste them side by side.
  • Check carbonation, haze, colour and dissolved oxygen over time.
  • For anything with residual sugar or juice, confirm the stabilisation step works.
  • Ask your can or bottle supplier whether the pack suits an acidic or flavoured drink.

A warm room around 30 to 35 °C is a reasonable stress test for most Indian routes to market. Hoppy beers and citrus flavours usually fade first.

Scale-up and costing

Pilot recipes rarely scale one for one. Hop utilisation, heat transfer, fermentation temperature profiles and oxygen pickup all change in bigger tanks. Plan for at least one production trial batch you are prepared to treat as an experiment. Our guide on scaling a pilot recipe to production covers the adjustments.

Recost the product at production scale: raw materials, packaging, tank time, losses, excise and distribution. If it no longer fits the brief, change the recipe or the price now, not after launch.

Who owns the project

Give every new product one owner with authority to stop it. In small breweries the head brewer often ends up running development alongside daily production. The new product slips every time a tank needs attention.

Use simple gates. The brief is approved before trials start. Trials pass sensory and shelf-life checks before the pack is designed. Costing at production scale is approved before label registration is filed. Each gate takes one short meeting with numbers on the table, not a feeling.

Launch and review

Before launch, write the final specification: recipe, process limits, release tests and shelf life. Brief the sales team on what the drink is and how it should be stored and served. If someone else will produce it for you, put the specification into a written quality agreement.

Set a review date, three months after launch, with numbers agreed in advance: sales against forecast, complaints, returns and repeat orders. Decide then whether to keep it, change it or stop it. A stop decision that fires on a date is easier to keep than one waiting for someone to feel ready.

Common questions

How long does drinks new product development take?

It depends on the drink and the label approvals needed. Label and brand registration in each state is often the longest step, so start it early.

What is a triangle test?

A sensory test where tasters get three samples, two the same and one different. They must pick the odd one. It shows whether two versions are distinguishable before you judge which is better.

Why test shelf life warm?

Indian distribution is often warm. Holding samples at around 30 to 35 °C shows how flavour, haze and carbonation will hold up before customers find out.
