How to store beer at home

You cannot undo a bad supply chain, but you can stop making it worse once a beer reaches your fridge. These habits cost nothing and take seconds.

3 min read · Updated July 2026 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. Cold, consistently, is the single biggest factor
  2. Keep it out of light
  3. Store it upright, most of the time
  4. Do not assume older is better
  5. A few habits that make the biggest difference
  6. Reading the beer for signs it is past its best

Cold, consistently, is the single biggest factor

Heat is the main driver behind both oxidation and general flavour decline, and the damage is cumulative. A beer that goes warm, cold, warm again as it moves between a shop shelf, a car boot and your kitchen counter ages faster than one kept at a steady cold temperature the entire way.

A normal fridge, around 3 to 5 degrees Celsius, is cold enough for virtually every style. You do not need specialised equipment, just consistency.

Indian summers make this harder than it sounds, since a car parked outside for even twenty minutes can reach temperatures well past what any beer should sit in. Plan the beer as the last thing you load and the first thing you unload.

Keep it out of light

Any beer in a clear or green bottle is at risk of turning lightstruck, sometimes called skunky, within minutes of direct light exposure, a fault covered in detail in our guide to lightstruck beer. Store bottles in their box or a dark cupboard rather than on an open shelf near a window.

Cans avoid this problem entirely, which is one more reason to reach for a can when the choice is otherwise equal. Even bright kitchen lighting left on for hours can contribute over time, so a dark cupboard beats an open shelf even indoors.

Store it upright, most of the time

For beer with a cap or a crown seal, standing the bottle upright reduces the surface area of beer in contact with the cap liner, which slightly lowers the risk of any off flavour transferring from the seal over long storage. For cans, orientation makes no real difference.

The exception is some bottle conditioned beers with a yeast sediment, which a few brewers recommend storing on their side for a period before standing upright again to let the sediment settle just before pouring. Check the label if a brewery specifies this, since the right approach genuinely varies by beer rather than being a single universal rule.

Do not assume older is better

Outside of a small group of intentionally aged styles, like barleywine, imperial stout or some strong Belgian ales, freshness is almost always the better bet. A hoppy pale ale or IPA, a crisp lager, or a wheat beer are all built to be enjoyed young, and holding onto them for months does nothing but let the hop aroma and general freshness fade.

If a label does not clearly say a beer is meant to be aged, treat it as one to drink soon rather than to save. Wine and whisky ageing habits do not transfer to beer, and applying them here usually just means drinking a tired version of what could have been a great beer a few weeks earlier.

A few habits that make the biggest difference

Buy from a fridge rather than a warm shelf wherever you have the choice. Get beer into your own fridge as soon as you reasonably can after buying it, rather than leaving it in a hot car or on a counter for hours. And once it is cold, try to keep it that way rather than repeatedly warming and re-chilling it.

None of this requires any special equipment. It is entirely about consistency and reducing the number of times a beer goes through a temperature swing. Small habits, repeated every time, matter far more here than any single clever trick.

Reading the beer for signs it is past its best

A flat aroma on a style that should smell vibrant, or a faint cardboard note, are both signs a beer has aged past where it should be, whatever the label date says. Learning to notice this for yourself is a genuinely useful skill, and it pairs well with knowing how to read a beer label properly in the first place, since a packaging date tells you far more than a distant best before date ever will.

Once you can tell a tired beer from a fresh one by smell alone, storage habits stop feeling like an abstract rule and start feeling like something worth doing for a reason you can actually taste.

Common questions

Is it fine to store beer at room temperature?

For most styles, no, not for long. Warmth accelerates oxidation and flavour decline. A cool, dark cupboard is a reasonable short term option, but a fridge is better for anything you plan to drink within weeks.

Does freezing beer to chill it quickly damage it?

A brief chill in the freezer to bring down temperature quickly is generally fine if you remember it. Actually freezing solid can affect texture and carbonation and risks a burst can or bottle.

Do all beers benefit from ageing?

No. Most modern craft styles, especially hoppy and pale beers, are meant to be drunk fresh. Only a small group of strong, dark or barrel aged styles genuinely improve with time.

Should beer be stored standing or lying down?

Upright is the safer default for capped bottles and all cans. Lying beer on its side is only useful for certain bottle conditioned beers with sediment, and only for part of the storage period.
