Pairing wine with Indian food

Indian food has a reputation for being hard to pair with wine. It is not hard once you know what spice, fat and heat do to the wine in your glass. A few rules cover most of the menu, from a Sunday thali to a corporate dinner.

3 min read · Updated September 2026 · Craft Beer School

Pairing is about structure, not flavour

Most pairing advice talks about matching flavours, such as fruity wine with fruity sauce. With Indian food it is more useful to think about structure: the sweetness, acidity, tannin and alcohol of the wine against the heat, fat, salt and sweetness of the dish. Those are the elements that clash or cooperate. If those terms are new, our guide to tasting wine without the jargon explains each one.

Salt is a friend to most wines, because it softens the feel of tannin and acidity. Sweetness in the food works the other way. It makes a dry wine taste thinner and more sour. Plenty of Indian dishes carry a little sugar or jaggery, such as many Gujarati dals and some tomato-based gravies, so an off-dry wine is often the safer choice there.

Chilli heat changes everything

Chilli heat is the first thing to plan around. Three rules hold up well:

  • Alcohol makes chilli burn hotter. A 15 percent red with a fiery vindaloo is a punishment. Lower-alcohol wines are kinder.
  • Tannin and chilli fight. Heat makes tannin feel harsher and more bitter. Big, grippy reds suit gentle dishes better than hot ones.
  • A touch of sweetness calms heat. An off-dry white, such as an off-dry Riesling or Chenin Blanc, takes the edge off chilli while its acidity keeps it fresh.

So the safest wine for a hot, spicy dish is usually a lower-alcohol white or rosé with some fruit, good acidity and perhaps a little sweetness.

Fat, fried food and acidity

Indian cooking uses plenty of ghee, cream, oil and frying. Acidity in wine cuts through richness the way a squeeze of lime does. Pakoras, samosas and fried fish suit crisp, high-acid whites and sparkling wine. A dry sparkling wine with a plate of hot bhajiyas is one of the easiest wins on any menu. The bubbles help scrub the palate clean between bites.

Creamy dishes such as a korma or a butter chicken, which are rich but not very hot, can take a fuller white, a fruity rosé or a soft, low-tannin red.

Where reds work

Reds come into their own with meat dishes that are rich and savoury rather than fiery. Seekh kebabs, mutton dishes cooked slowly with whole spices and tandoori meats with smoky char can stand up to Shiraz or a softer Cabernet blend. The fat and protein soften the tannin. The rule is to watch the chilli. If the dish is very hot, choose a fruitier, lighter red and serve it slightly cool.

Regional pointers

  • Coastal seafood curries with coconut and tamarind: aromatic, fresh whites such as Sauvignon Blanc or Chenin Blanc, or a dry rosé.
  • Tandoori and grills: rosé is a reliable all-rounder, with a lighter red if the meat is rich.
  • Biryani: a fruity, low-tannin red or a rosé, depending on the heat.
  • Vegetarian thali: many dishes at once, so pick a flexible wine such as an off-dry white or a dry rosé rather than chasing one dish.
  • Desserts: the wine should be at least as sweet as the dessert, or it will taste sour. Most dry wines lose this fight with mithai.

Serving it right

Temperature changes everything in an Indian dining room. Whites, rosés and sparkling should be properly chilled, around 6 to 12 °C. Reds suit around 14 to 18 °C, which means a short spell in the fridge in most Indian homes and restaurants. A warm red with a spicy dish combines high alcohol and heat, the worst of both rules above.

For anyone in sales or marketing, pairing advice is one of the most useful things a wine brand can offer, because many Indian customers drink wine with home food. Recommendations should always be framed around responsible enjoyment with a meal.

Trying it yourself

The rules above are a starting point. Taste a high-acid white, an off-dry white and a tannic red against one mild and one hot dish, small sips with water on hand. The effects are obvious within minutes. It is one of the most convincing exercises we run with teams, because everyone at the table gets the same result.

Common questions

What wine goes with spicy Indian food?

Usually a lower-alcohol white or rosé with good acidity and some fruit. An off-dry white such as Riesling or Chenin Blanc calms chilli heat well.

Why do red wines taste harsh with spicy food?

Chilli heat makes tannin feel more bitter and drying. Alcohol amplifies the burn. Big, high-alcohol reds suffer most.

What wine goes with biryani?

A fruity, low-tannin red or a rosé works for most biryanis. If it is very spicy, lean towards rosé or an aromatic white.

Can wine be paired with Indian desserts?

Only if the wine is at least as sweet as the dessert. Dry wines taste sour next to most mithai.
