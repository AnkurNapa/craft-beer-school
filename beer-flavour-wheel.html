Using the beer flavour wheel

The flavour wheel is a map of beer aroma and flavour built by sensory scientists, organised from broad families down to specific notes. Here is how to use it without getting lost.

3 min read · Updated November 2022 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  2. Work from the centre out
  3. The wheel is not just for pleasant notes
  4. Where it helps most
  5. Building your own shorthand from it

What the wheel actually is

The beer flavour wheel groups aroma and flavour into broad categories near the centre, such as fruity, floral, malty or off flavour, and gets more specific as you move outward, from fruity to citrus to grapefruit, for example. It was built by sensory researchers decades ago to give the brewing industry a shared vocabulary, so a description means the same thing in Bangalore as it does anywhere else in the world.

Versions of the wheel have been refined over the decades since, but the underlying logic, broad families splitting into specific notes, has stayed the same, which is part of why it has remained useful across generations of brewers and tasters.

Work from the centre out

When you taste a beer, do not scan the whole wheel looking for a matching word straight away. Start at the broad categories, pick the one or two that fit best, then move outward only within that slice to find something more specific. Trying to match a smell directly to an outer ring word usually fails, because you have skipped the step of placing it in the right family first, which is where most of the useful narrowing actually happens.

A useful discipline is to say the category out loud before reaching for the specific word, even mentally. This single habit slows down the guessing and noticeably improves accuracy, especially for tasters still early in building their vocabulary.

The wheel is not just for pleasant notes

A full flavour wheel includes an off flavour section covering faults like diacetyl, DMS and oxidation. These are as much a part of the vocabulary as fruity or floral notes, and recognising them is arguably the more commercially useful half of the skill, since it is what lets a brewer or judge diagnose a process problem rather than simply describe a pleasant beer.

Sensory scientists behind the original wheel deliberately placed off flavours on equal footing with pleasant aromas, rather than as an afterthought tacked onto the edge, because a beer's quality assessment genuinely depends on both halves working together as one system.

Where it helps most

The wheel is most useful in two situations: when you are stuck trying to describe something and need a prompt to get unstuck, and when you are comparing your notes with someone else's and need a shared word to check you mean the same thing. It is less useful as a checklist to run through on every single beer, which turns tasting into homework rather than a skill you are actually building.

New tasters sometimes try to find a word for every single sensation in a beer, which usually produces a cluttered, unfocused note. Two or three well chosen words from the wheel say more than a long list of half fitting ones.

Some breweries print a small pocket version of the wheel for bar staff, since being able to point a curious customer towards a rough category, even without perfect specificity, makes for a noticeably better conversation across the counter.

Building your own shorthand from it

Most experienced tasters end up using a personal shortlist of ten to fifteen words drawn from the wheel that cover most of what they actually encounter in practice, rather than reaching for the full wheel every time they taste. Build yours by noting which words you reach for most often across a few months of deliberate tasting.

Some brewers pin a simplified version of the wheel, cut down to the fifteen or twenty words they use most, on the wall of the tasting room, which is a good compromise between the full formal wheel and relying purely on memory during a busy production day.

Where the wheel sits next to other tools

The wheel describes what you perceive through aroma and flavour. Mouthfeel and body and bitterness perception cover the physical sensations that sit alongside flavour and aroma, and a complete tasting note usually draws on all three together rather than treating them as separate exercises.

If two tasters disagree on which slice of the wheel a smell belongs to, that disagreement itself is useful information, worth discussing rather than papering over, since it often reveals that one taster is noticing something the other genuinely is not.

Common questions

Do I need to memorise the whole flavour wheel?

No. Learn the broad categories first and build familiarity with the specific words you encounter most often in your own tasting.

Is the flavour wheel only for professionals?

It was built for the industry but works just as well for a curious home taster wanting more precise language than nice or not nice.

Why does the wheel include unpleasant smells?

Because recognising a fault is as much a sensory skill as recognising a pleasant note, and it is often the more useful one for anyone involved in making beer.

How is the flavour wheel different from just building vocabulary?

Building vocabulary is the underlying skill. The wheel is one structured tool for organising that vocabulary so it is easier to recall and to compare with other tasters.
