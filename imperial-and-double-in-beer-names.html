Imperial and double in beer names: what the words really mean

Imperial stout, double IPA, triple, doppelbock. Beer names borrow grand words to signal strength, but each word has its own history and none of them is a legal promise. Here is what they mean and how to read them before you order.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

A rough rule first

When you see imperial or double in a beer's name, assume it is a stronger, fuller version of the base style. An IPA might be 6 to 7% ABV. A double IPA is often 7.5 to 10%. A regular stout might be 4 to 6%. An imperial stout usually starts around 8% and can go well past 10%.

That rule works most of the time. The exceptions are where the history gets interesting and where a menu can mislead you.

The opposite word is session. A session IPA or session ale is a lighter version built to be drunk over a long evening, usually under about 5% ABV. Between session at one end and imperial at the other, most beer names are quietly telling you how much malt went into the mash.

Where imperial comes from

The word goes back to strong stouts brewed in London in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and shipped to the Baltic and to Russia, where they found favour at the imperial court. Russian imperial stout became the name for this rich, high-strength, heavily roasted beer. It is dark, often with chocolate, coffee and dried fruit flavours and a warming alcohol finish. Our imperial stout guide covers it in detail.

Modern brewers then borrowed the word. Imperial IPA, imperial pilsner and imperial red ale simply mean a scaled-up version of the original. There is nothing royal about them beyond the extra malt and hops.

Double, triple and the Belgian story

The Belgian abbey and Trappist tradition uses dubbel and tripel for a family of strong ales. A dubbel is dark, malty and around 6 to 7.5% ABV. A tripel is pale, dry and spicy and usually 7.5 to 9.5%. The names suggest a simple multiplication, but they do not mean twice or three times the strength of anything specific. Our guides to dubbel and tripel explain each one.

Quadrupel, or quad, came later for even stronger dark ales. It is a marketing step up the same ladder rather than a precise measure.

Doppelbock and the -ator names

German brewing gives us doppelbock, literally double bock. Bock is already a strong lager. Doppelbock is richer and stronger again, often 7 to 10% ABV with deep bready, caramel malt flavours. Monks in Munich brewed a strong beer to sustain them through fasting. Many doppelbocks still carry names ending in -ator as a nod to the first famous example.

Double IPA

Double IPA, also sold as imperial IPA, came out of the American craft scene in the 1990s. Brewers pushed both malt and hops well beyond a normal IPA: more alcohol, more bitterness and a huge hop aroma. The style is common on Indian brewpub menus now, often in hazy form. Our double IPA guide describes what to expect.

Triple IPA exists too, but again the name means bigger rather than any exact figure.

Why the label matters more than the name

None of these words is regulated in India as a statement of strength. A brewer can call a 6.5% beer a double and nothing stops them. What is regulated is the alcohol content on the label, which beer labels in India must declare. Read that number. Our guide to reading a beer label shows where to find it.

This matters for more than curiosity. A 10% imperial stout carries roughly twice the alcohol of a standard lager in the same glass. In a warm taproom, a big beer can catch you out. Order smaller measures, drink water alongside and treat strong beers as something to sip and share rather than a pint to work through. Many brewpubs serve them in tasting glasses for exactly that reason.

Tasting strength, not just reading it

With practice you can taste strength. Alcohol shows as warmth in the throat, a fuller body and sometimes a sweet or solvent edge if the beer is young or poorly made. A well-made imperial beer hides its strength under malt and hops. A clumsy one tastes hot. Learning to tell the two apart is one of the most useful skills a drinker can build. It takes structured tasting rather than guesswork.

Common questions

Does imperial always mean stronger?

Almost always. Imperial is used for scaled-up versions of a style, with more malt, more alcohol and usually more hops or roast. Check the ABV on the label to be sure.

Is a double IPA twice as strong as an IPA?

Not exactly. A double IPA is usually 7.5 to 10% ABV, compared with about 6 to 7% for a regular IPA. The word signals bigger, not an exact multiple.

What is the difference between dubbel and doppelbock?

Dubbel is a Belgian dark ale fermented with ale yeast. Doppelbock is a German strong lager. Both are malty and fairly strong, but they come from different traditions and taste different.
