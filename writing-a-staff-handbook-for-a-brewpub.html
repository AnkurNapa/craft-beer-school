Writing a staff handbook for a brewpub

Every brewpub runs on unwritten rules until the day two staff remember them differently. A short handbook writes those rules down once. Done well, it saves the founder from answering the same question forty times a month.

What a handbook is for

A handbook does three jobs. It tells new staff how the place works before their first shift. It gives managers a reference when a question comes up at 11 pm. It shows, if there is ever a dispute, that the rules were written and shared.

It is not the employment contract. The contract covers the individual: pay, role, probation, notice. The handbook covers everyone: how we work, what we expect and what happens when things go wrong. Keep the two consistent. If the contract says one month's notice and the handbook says two weeks, you have a problem.

Keep it short enough to read

Aim for 15 to 20 pages, in plain language, with short sections and headings people can scan. Write it the way you would explain things to a new server on their first evening, not the way a lawyer would. A 60 page handbook gets signed and never opened.

Translate it if your team needs that. Many brewpub kitchens and housekeeping teams are more comfortable in Hindi, Kannada, Tamil, Marathi or another local language than in English. A handbook your dishwasher cannot read is not doing its job. A one page summary in each language your team uses is a good start.

What to include

Most brewpub handbooks need these sections:

  • Welcome: who we are, the beers and what we care about.
  • Working hours, shifts, weekly offs, leave and how to request it.
  • Pay day, how service charge or incentives work and how to raise a pay query.
  • Uniform, grooming and personal hygiene, especially for kitchen and bar.
  • Staff drinking policy, including tasting for quality and shift drinks.
  • Responsible service: the legal drinking age in your state, checking ID and refusing service.
  • Safety: the brewhouse and cold room rules, caustic and gas handling, first aid and fire exits.
  • Food safety basics for anyone who handles food or glasses.
  • Conduct, harassment and how to raise a complaint safely.
  • Discipline and what counts as serious misconduct.

Make the safety section specific

Generic safety text gets ignored. Make it about your building. Name the places where the risks are: the brewhouse, where wort is near 100 °C on brew days; the cellar, where caustic cleaning runs at 70 to 80 °C; the cold room, where carbon dioxide can build up from leaking fittings or open tanks; the kitchen, with hot oil and knives.

For each, write the rule in one line. Nobody enters a tank. Goggles and gloves for caustic and acid. Report a gas smell or a hissing fitting at once and leave the room. Our guide to day one safety training covers what to teach alongside the handbook.

Write the complaint route clearly

Every brewpub needs a safe way for staff to raise problems, including harassment. Name two people a staff member can go to, so nobody has to report a problem to the person causing it. Workplaces in India with ten or more employees must set up an internal committee to handle complaints of sexual harassment. Check the current requirements with your lawyer and name the committee members in the handbook.

Say plainly that nobody will be punished for raising a concern in good faith. Then make sure that is true. Late nights, alcohol and a mixed team of guests and staff make this section matter more in a brewpub than in most offices.

Discipline: fair, stepwise and written

Set out a simple, stepped process: a conversation, a written warning, a final written warning, then dismissal. List the serious misconduct that skips straight to the last steps after a fair hearing. In a brewpub that usually includes theft of stock or cash, working drunk, violence, harassment, falsifying records and deliberately breaking safety rules.

Write that every step will be recorded and that the staff member can give their side before a decision. A fair process protects good staff from bad managers as much as it protects the business.

Cover the money rules plainly

Handbooks often skip the questions staff care about most: when they get paid and how. Say which date salaries arrive, how overtime or late closes are paid and how service charge or incentives are shared. Explain what happens to tips left in cash. Unclear rules on money cause more resentment in brewpubs than almost anything else.

Include the rules on staff meals, staff discounts and what staff may take home. If unsold food goes in the bin at close, say whether staff can eat it. Small, clear rules prevent big arguments.

Launch it properly and keep it alive

Walk every new starter through the handbook on day one, in person, for 30 minutes. Ask them to sign a single page saying they received and read it. Keep the signed page in their file. For existing staff, hold a short team meeting to introduce it rather than leaving copies in the staff room.

Review it once a year and whenever something changes: a new outlet, a new menu, a change in licensing hours. Keep a version number and date on the front. A handbook is one of the quiet systems that lets a brewpub run without the founder on the floor every night. Our guide to writing job descriptions pairs well with it.

Your first step: list every question new staff asked you in the past month. That list is your table of contents.

Common questions

How long should a brewpub staff handbook be?

About 15 to 20 pages, in plain language. Longer handbooks tend to be signed and never read.

What is the difference between a contract and a handbook?

The contract covers one person's pay, role and notice. The handbook covers rules for everyone. Keep the two consistent.

Should a brewpub handbook be translated?

If your team includes people more comfortable in another language, yes. At least provide a one page summary in each language used on the team.

How often should a staff handbook be updated?

Review it once a year and whenever something important changes, such as a new outlet, new licensing hours or new equipment.
