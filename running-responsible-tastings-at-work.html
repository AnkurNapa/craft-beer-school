Running responsible tastings at work

Tasting the products your company makes is the fastest way for an office team to understand them. Done carelessly, it is also the fastest way to an HR problem. Here is how we run tastings that are useful, opt-in and safe.

3 min read · Updated September 2026 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  2. Start with policy and permission
  3. Opt-in, with non-drinkers welcome

Why tasting belongs in the training plan

An analyst can model a brand for a year without knowing what it tastes like. That gap shows. A marketer who has never tasted the difference between a lager and a wheat beer writes vaguer briefs. A planner who has never compared a young whisky with an older one finds it harder to see why aged stock is valuable.

A tasting closes that gap in an hour. It is a learning session with a product in the glass, not a party. Every decision below follows from treating it that way.

Set one learning goal per session and write it on the invite. For example: tell our three core lagers apart by aroma, or explain to a customer why the aged whisky costs more. A goal keeps the hour focused and gives you a way to judge whether it worked. If people leave able to describe the products in their own words, it did.

Start with policy and permission

Before anything else, read your company's alcohol policy and talk to HR and facilities. Many drinks companies already have rules on when, where and how product can be served at work. Follow them exactly.

Then check the legal side. Serving alcohol on office premises or at a venue may need a permission or an occasional licence from the state excise department. The rules vary by state and change. Your compliance team or the venue will know what applies. Avoid declared dry days. Keep everything within the legal drinking age for the state you are in.

Opt-in, with non-drinkers welcome

Attendance is voluntary and nobody is asked why they are not drinking. People skip alcohol for religion, health, pregnancy, medication, driving or simple preference. None of that is anyone else's business.

Design the session so a non-drinker learns as much as anyone else. Smelling is most of tasting, so a person can nose every sample and still follow the whole session. Have non-alcoholic options on the table, such as a non-alcoholic beer, sparkling water or a grape juice next to the wine flight. Never tie a tasting to performance reviews, team scores or anything that makes declining feel costly.

Small pours and spit cups

A tasting pour is a few sips, a fraction of a normal serve. Six small samples should add up to less than one ordinary drink. Professional tasters spit. We teach everyone to do the same: put an opaque cup or a spittoon at every seat, plus a dump bucket for leftovers. Spitting is normal in a tasting room. The session leader should do it first so nobody feels awkward.

Serve food and plenty of water. Plain crackers or bread reset the palate between samples. Spirits deserve a word of their own: pour whisky in small measures, offer still water to add a few drops and keep the flight short.

Order the flight so people can actually taste

Here is the practical detail that makes or breaks the hour: serving order and temperature. Go from light to strong and from delicate to intense. A crisp lager before a stout, a white wine before a red, a young whisky before an older one. A lager served straight from the fridge at 3 or 4 °C tastes of very little, so let it warm a few degrees. In an Indian office in summer, the opposite problem arrives fast and hoppy beer left on the table goes flat in aroma within the hour.

Give each sample a reason to be in the glass. Tie it to the work: this is our biggest brand, this is the style a competitor is growing, this is the fault our quality team found last month. Our guide to how to taste beer shows the method we teach.

Getting everyone home

Plan the end before the start. Schedule tastings so they finish with time to spare, arrange cabs or company transport and make it clear that nobody who has had a drink drives. Close the bar when the session closes. A tasting that drifts into an evening of drinking undoes everything that made it a training session.

Running all of this well takes practice. Most teams only do it a few times a year. That is a good reason to bring in someone who leads tastings every week.

Common questions

Do we need a licence to serve alcohol at an office tasting?

Possibly. Many states require a permission or occasional licence to serve alcohol at an event or on certain premises. The rules vary by state, so check with your compliance team or the venue.

How can non-drinkers take part?

Fully. Most of tasting is smell, so they can nose every sample and follow the discussion. Put non-alcoholic options on the table and never ask anyone to explain why they are not drinking.

Should people spit?

Yes, it is what professional tasters do. Give everyone an opaque spit cup and have the session leader spit first so it feels normal.
