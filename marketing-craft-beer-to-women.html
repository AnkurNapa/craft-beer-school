Marketing craft beer to women without pink labels or tired cliches

Many breweries say they want more women in the taproom, then launch a pink-labelled fruit beer and wonder why it flops. We look at what goes wrong and what a better approach looks like in practice.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

The pink label trap

Every few years a brewery somewhere launches a "beer for women": lighter, sweeter, fruit flavoured, with a pastel label and a name that sounds like a nail varnish. It usually fails. It fails because it starts from an assumption about what women want rather than from asking any.

Women who drink beer drink the whole range: stouts, IPAs, lagers, sours. Some like fruit beers, as do plenty of men. Treating half the population as one taste profile is lazy segmentation. Drinkers notice.

What women drinkers often say puts them off

The problem is rarely the beer. It is everything around it. Talk to women who drink craft beer in Bengaluru, Pune or Delhi and similar points tend to come up:

  • Staff who hand the beer list to the man at the table and the wine list to the woman.
  • Servers who assume she wants "something light" before she has spoken.
  • Advertising that uses women as decoration rather than as drinkers.
  • Taprooms that feel like a boys' club: loud, crowded and with no comfortable way to sit and talk.
  • Getting home safely late at night, which shapes where and when people choose to go out.

None of these is solved by a label colour. All of them are within a brewery's control.

Market to drinkers, not to stereotypes

A better starting point is drinker personas built on behaviour and motivation, not gender. A curious explorer who wants to try a new sour every month might be a 28-year-old woman in tech or a 45-year-old man who travels for work. A social drinker who wants an easy, sessionable pint after work exists in every demographic.

When you design for motivations, women are included by default. Your imagery shows a mix of people actually drinking and enjoying the beer. Your tasting notes describe flavour plainly. Your events are ones anyone might want to attend.

What changes in practice

Imagery. Show women holding and drinking the beer, not standing beside it. Show groups of friends, couples and solo drinkers at the bar. If your Instagram grid has twenty photos of bearded men and one woman posing with a glass she is not drinking, fix it.

Language. Describe beers by taste: crisp, roasty, tart, bitter. Drop words like "ladies' choice" or "girly". "Easy-drinking" is a fine description of a beer, as long as it is not code for "for her".

Service. Train staff to hand the menu to whoever asks, to offer tasters to everyone and to recommend based on what the guest says they like. This costs nothing and changes the experience more than any campaign.

Space. Good lighting, clean toilets, seating where people can talk, music at a level that allows conversation. Help with getting a cab home late. These make a taproom better for everyone.

Women behind the beer

The most credible signal is who makes and sells the beer. Women work as brewers, quality managers, sales leads and founders across Indian brewing. Our guide to women in Indian brewing looks at their paths. If your team includes women in production or leadership, let them speak for the beer in videos, at launches and at tastings. Do it because they are good at their jobs, not as a token.

Events that welcome people in

Some breweries run women-only tasting sessions. When they are led by knowledgeable people and framed as "learn to taste beer" rather than "beer for ladies", they can work well as a first step. Many women simply prefer a mixed beginners' tasting with no assumptions. Offer both and see which fills.

Keep tastings responsible: small pours, water and food on the table with no pressure on anyone to drink more than they want. Make sure non-alcoholic options are good, not an afterthought.

Measuring whether it is working

You cannot improve what you do not watch. Look at who follows and engages with your social accounts, who comes to tastings and who buys at the bar. If the mix stays narrow, ask your customers why, informally or in a short survey. The answers are usually specific and fixable: a rude server, a sticky table, an advert that made someone wince.

Getting this right is part of a wider question: who your brand is for and how it speaks to them. That is brand positioning work. It pays back across every customer, not just one segment.

Common questions

Should breweries make a beer specifically for women?

Generally no. Women drink every style. Gendered products tend to feel patronising. Better service, imagery and descriptions reach women and everyone else at once.

Are women-only beer tastings a good idea?

They can be, if led by knowledgeable people and framed as learning to taste beer. Offer mixed beginners' sessions too. Keep pours small and responsible.

What is the quickest fix a taproom can make?

Train staff to hand menus and offer tasters to everyone equally. Recommend beers based on what each guest says they like.
