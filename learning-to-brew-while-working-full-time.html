Learning to brew while working full time

Most people who switch to brewing keep a job while they learn. This guide gives a weekly routine, a budget of hours and the traps to avoid.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa and Rahul Baliyan

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer
Written byRahul BaliyanBrewing Science Instructor · Delhi & North India

Be realistic about hours

Count the hours you can spare. Five to eight a week is a reasonable target for many people. That might be two evenings of study, one half day of practice and a little reading on commutes. Write the plan in your calendar as fixed appointments, not hopes.

A slow, steady pace beats a burst. Three months of consistent effort beats a weekend of intense work followed by silence.

Tell the people you live with what the plan is. Their support matters more than any app.

The three parts of a weekly routine

  • Study. Read or watch one topic: mashing, fermentation, sanitation, water. Make notes in your own words.
  • Practise. Brew, taste or measure. Even without a brew kit, you can practise tasting with commercial beers and calculating ABV from gravity readings.
  • Connect. Talk to a brewer, post questions in a forum or visit a taproom.

Alternate heavy and light weeks. Keep a log of what you learned.

A simple log can be a page per week: date, hours, topic, one thing I got wrong, one question. In six months you will have a record that tells you where you are weak.

Choose a format that fits

Live classes at weekends suit people with fixed weekday jobs. Recordings let you catch up when work or family intervenes. Weekly assignments keep you honest. Avoid courses that pile up unfinished videos and ask nothing of you.

If you join our Brewing Fundamentals course, classes run on Saturdays and Sundays, 12:00 PM to 2:00 PM India Standard Time (UTC+5:30). Students join from their own time zone. Recordings cover a missed class.

Pick one resource per topic and finish it. Collecting ten half-read articles feels like progress and delivers none.

Practise with what you have

You do not need a brewhouse on day one. Taste a different style each week with a notebook: colour, aroma, flavour, body and finish. Read how to taste beer for a method. Calculate the ABV of beers you drink from published gravities. Visit a brewery on tour days and ask one precise question.

When you are ready, brew small batches. Check the rules on home brewing in your country, since they differ.

Water is a good first topic. If you know your local water report, you can already say something sensible about why a pale lager in your town might taste different from one made elsewhere. Look up the report and note calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate.

Find a brewery to learn beside

Ask a local brewery whether you can help on a Saturday brew day. Offer to wash kegs or label cans. Bring your own boots and arrive when you said you would. Over a few months you will see a full cycle: mash, boil, ferment, condition, package.

Be careful with your employer. Check any contract clause about outside work and about alcohol sales.

Do not hand over your own judgement either. If a volunteer task feels unsafe, ask a question before you start.

Protect your energy and your health

Brewing practice is physical and so is the day job for many. Do not overload. Enjoy beer in moderation. Never treat tasting as an excuse to drink more. Use spit cups when you sample many beers, drink water and never drive after tasting.

If you miss a fortnight, restart with the next small step. Keep a record of finished tasks instead of a list of tasks missed.

Sleep and meals count as study time in the sense that a tired taster misreads a beer. Plan tastings for when you are fresh and have not eaten anything strongly flavoured.

What to do when you are ready to move

After six to nine months, you should have notes from batches, a log of tastings and a few hours of real brewery work. That is enough to apply for a junior role. Read working in beer sales if you prefer a customer-facing path.

If you want structure for the next step, Brewing Fundamentals gives four weeks of live teaching, assignments and a certificate. Reserve your seat and start the assignments while you still hold your job.

Keep your CV updated as you go. Add each course, batch and volunteer shift the week it happens, while you still remember the details.

Common questions

How many hours a week do I need?

Five to eight hours is a practical target for most people with a full-time job.

Can I learn brewing only online?

You can learn the theory well online. Pair it with real practice, at home where legal or at a brewery.

Will my employer mind?

Check your contract and be open if there is any conflict, for example with alcohol-related work.

What if I fall behind?

Use recordings and restart with the smallest next task. Steady progress matters more than catching up.
