Dunkel and schwarzbier

Both of these are dark German lagers, and both surprise people who expect a dark beer to taste like a stout. Here is what actually separates them.

3 min read · Updated October 2022 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Two dark beers with very different intentions

Munich Dunkel is the older of the two, a dark lager built around Munich malt's bready, slightly chocolatey character long before pale lager existed at all. Schwarzbier, meaning black beer, developed with the addition of darker roasted malts, giving it a noticeably blacker colour, but the brewing intent stays gentle rather than roast forward.

Both styles are frequently misjudged by people expecting a stout or porter's roast bitterness. Neither delivers that. Both stay smooth, moderate in strength and easy drinking despite the dark colour.

What to expect in the glass: Dunkel

Munich Dunkel pours deep brown to reddish brown, with a bready, malty aroma carrying notes of light chocolate and toast, but no roast bitterness to speak of. Bitterness stays low, 16 to 25 IBU, and the finish is smooth and slightly sweet rather than dry.

The overall impression should be warm and comforting rather than intense. This is a beer built for the malt, not for making a statement.

A clean, tight head that lasts through the glass is a good sign, and a beer that goes flat quickly usually points to a protein or carbonation issue upstream rather than anything wrong with the recipe itself.

What to expect in the glass: Schwarzbier

Schwarzbier pours a much deeper black to very dark brown, closer in appearance to a porter, but tastes noticeably lighter and cleaner, with a mild roasted coffee or cocoa note sitting on top of a still fairly light body. Bitterness runs slightly higher than Dunkel, 20 to 30 IBU, though never sharp.

The trick with schwarzbier is restraint with the roasted malt: enough for colour and a whisper of roast character, not so much that it starts competing with a genuine stout.

A useful test is to hold the beer up to light: even at its darkest, a well made schwarzbier often shows a faint reddish edge at the rim rather than the truly opaque black of a stout.

The malt bills behind each style

Dunkel relies mostly on Munich malt, sometimes with a small addition of a darker Munich variant or a touch of chocolate malt purely for colour. Schwarzbier adds a genuinely dark roasted malt, often a small proportion of debittered black malt or carafa type malt, chosen specifically to avoid harsh roast bitterness while still delivering a black colour.

Getting schwarzbier's roast malt addition right takes some care. Too much and the beer drifts toward tasting like a black lager version of a stout, which misses the point of the style entirely.

Debittered dark malts are worth seeking out specifically for this style, since a standard black malt not intended for this purpose will usually bring more harshness than the recipe wants, however carefully the amount is measured.

Fermentation stays standard lager technique

Both styles use a clean bottom fermenting lager yeast at 9 to 13°C with a genuine lagering period afterward, the same fundamental process as pilsner or Helles. There is no unusual fermentation trick here. The malt bill, not the yeast, is doing the work of making these two styles distinct.

This makes both approachable next styles for a brewery already comfortable with basic lager fermentation, since the technique transfers directly and only the grain bill really changes.

Telling them apart from bock and from each other

Against bock, both Dunkel and schwarzbier stay noticeably lower in strength and gentler on the malt intensity, built for session drinking rather than a stronger, warming beer. Between the two, colour is the quickest visual tell, Dunkel a rich brown, schwarzbier close to black, but the clearer distinction on the palate is that schwarzbier carries a mild roast note Dunkel never has.

Serve both around 7 to 10°C in a standard lager glass. They are genuinely versatile food beers, comfortable next to roasted vegetables, grilled meats or even mild Indian curries where a hoppier beer might clash.

Both styles also make a genuinely useful teaching pair for a tasting session, since holding a Dunkel and a schwarzbier side by side against a black stout makes the point about roast intensity clearer than any written description could manage on its own.

Common questions

Does schwarzbier taste like a stout?

No. Despite the dark, almost black colour, schwarzbier stays light bodied and only mildly roasted, using dark malt carefully to avoid the roast bitterness a stout typically has.

What is the main difference between Dunkel and schwarzbier?

Dunkel uses Munich malt for a bready, chocolatey character without roast, while schwarzbier adds a genuinely dark roasted malt for colour and a mild coffee like note, giving it a blacker appearance and slightly more roast character.

Are these strong beers?

No, both are session strength, generally 4.4 to 5.6% ABV, built for easy, repeated drinking rather than intensity.

Can I brew schwarzbier with the same process as a pilsner?

Largely yes. The fermentation and lagering technique is the same standard lager process. The grain bill, adding dark roasted malt in the right small proportion, is what actually creates the style.
