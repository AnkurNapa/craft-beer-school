What beer packaging costs

This guide breaks down what actually drives packaging cost, without inventing specific rupee figures that change constantly by supplier, volume and region.

3 min read · Updated June 2024 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. Why packaging costs surprise new breweries
  2. Cans versus bottles versus kegs

  4. Closures, labels and the small parts add up

Why packaging costs surprise new breweries

Ingredient cost is the number most new brewers research carefully before opening, because recipes are built around it from day one. Packaging is often treated as an afterthought until the first supplier quote arrives, and by then decisions about format and branding have usually already been made without the cost in mind.

Because supplier pricing changes with volume, region, material cost and the exchange rate on imported components, this guide describes what drives the cost rather than quoting figures that would be out of date within months. Get current quotes from at least two or three suppliers before committing to a format, and revisit those quotes periodically because material costs and freight rates shift more often than a brewery's own recipe does.

The earlier packaging is priced into a business plan, the fewer surprises show up once the brewery is actually running and cash flow is tighter than it was on paper, especially in the first year when every rupee of working capital is already spoken for.

Cans versus bottles versus kegs

Cans generally cost more per unit than returnable glass bottles at small volume, but they protect beer from light completely, which glass only partly does, and they are far cheaper to ship without breakage. Bottles can be cheaper if a returnable deposit system is genuinely in place and functioning, which is less common in Indian craft beer than it once was in traditional bottling.

Kegs carry a high upfront capital cost because they are a durable asset rather than a consumable, but the ongoing cost per litre delivered is very low once the keg fleet exists, which is why taproom-only breweries often favour kegs heavily before investing in a can line at all, and only move to packaged formats once distribution beyond the taproom becomes the priority.

Artwork and print runs drive cost more than the container itself

A short print run of a custom can design costs considerably more per unit than a long run of the same design, because print setup is a largely fixed cost spread across however many units are ordered. A brewery running frequent seasonal releases, as covered in our seasonal releases guide, pays a real premium for that variety unless print runs are planned well ahead and batched sensibly.

Simpler label designs with fewer colours are also generally cheaper to print accurately and consistently than complex, colour-heavy artwork, which matters for a small brewery trying to control cost without looking cheap on the shelf next to better funded competitors.

Closures, labels and the small parts add up

Can ends, bottle caps, crowns, shrink sleeves and secondary labels are each individually small costs that are easy to overlook when budgeting, but together they can add up to rival the cost of the primary container once a full order is tallied. Get quotes on the full bill of materials for a packaged unit, not just the can or bottle itself, before comparing formats side by side.

Minimum order quantities shape what a small brewery can actually do

Most can and bottle suppliers set a minimum order quantity that is far larger than a small brewery's typical batch size, which forces a choice between ordering more stock than one batch needs, sharing a print run across several beers, or working with a distributor who already holds generic stock. Each option has a real cost and cash flow implication that should be modelled before a launch date is set, not discovered afterwards, as covered in our launch guide. Sharing a print run across two beers scheduled close together is a common way to hit a minimum without overcommitting cash to a single design.

Where packaging spend should actually go first

For a small brewery on a limited budget, spending more on a confident, well designed label with a cheaper container generally beats spending more on an unusual container with a weak design. The label is what a customer actually reads on a shelf. Our positioning guide is worth reading before finalising any packaging brief, so the spend actually reflects a decision rather than a guess made under a supplier's deadline with a print run already booked.

Common questions

Are cans always more expensive than bottles?

Not always, and the gap depends heavily on volume, whether bottles are genuinely returnable, and current material prices, which change often enough that a specific comparison needs a fresh supplier quote rather than a fixed rule.

What is the biggest hidden packaging cost for a new brewery?

Small parts like closures, secondary labels and shrink sleeves are the most commonly underestimated cost, because they are rarely priced alongside the primary container in early planning.

Does a shorter print run always cost more per unit?

Generally yes, because print setup is a largely fixed cost. Batching artwork across releases or planning a longer run ahead of time usually brings the per-unit cost down.
