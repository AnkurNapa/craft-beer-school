When mascots and illustration styles help a brewery brand

Mascots and illustration styles can make a small brewery memorable, or make every can look like a different company. Here is how to tell which way yours is heading.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

On this page

  1. Why breweries reach for illustration
  2. Mascot or style?
  3. Signs that a mascot is helping
  4. Indian imagery, used with respect
  5. Briefing an illustrator for a range

Why breweries reach for illustration

Craft beer cans are full of illustration for good reasons. A brewery with ten beers and no advertising budget needs each can to be recognisable on a shelf and shareable on a phone. Illustration does that faster than type alone. It can also carry a mood that is hard to put in words: playful, local, strange, calm.

The risk is that illustration becomes the whole brand. If every beer gets a different artist and a different style, the shelf shows variety but no brewery. Shoppers remember one can and cannot find the next.

Mascot or style?

A mascot is a recurring character: an animal, a person, a creature. It works when it stands for something about the brewery, appears on every pack in a consistent pose or role and can be used at the taproom, on merchandise and online. It struggles when it is too detailed to shrink to a coaster or a social icon.

A house illustration style is a consistent way of drawing: line weight, colour treatment, level of detail, subject matter. Each beer can show a different scene while the shelf still reads as one family. This is often more flexible than a mascot for a growing range.

Some breweries do both: a mascot in the logo, a house style on the cans. That works if the two clearly belong together.

Signs that a mascot is helping

  • Regulars refer to beers by the character ("the one with the tiger").
  • Merchandise with the mascot sells without discounting.
  • The mascot still reads when printed in one colour at thumbnail size.
  • Staff can describe what it stands for in a sentence.

Signs it is hurting: people mistake the beer for a soft drink, the mascot feels aimed at children or the joke behind it has worn thin after two years. That second point is not only a matter of taste. Alcohol branding that appeals to minors is a real regulatory and reputational risk in India, so cartoon styles need extra care. See our guide to alcohol advertising rules in India.

Indian imagery, used with respect

Many Indian craft brands draw on local animals, festivals, mythology, folk art and city landmarks. Done well, it gives a brand roots that an imported style cannot. Done carelessly, it can offend. Religious figures and sacred symbols on an alcohol pack are a clear risk. Some regional art forms belong to communities who may object to their use on beer. Commission artists from the tradition you draw on, credit them and ask before borrowing.

Briefing an illustrator for a range

The first can is easy. The twelfth is where a style shows whether it was built to last. When you brief an illustrator, ask for a system, not a single picture:

  1. A short style guide: line weights, palette, how backgrounds are treated, what never appears.
  2. Two or three sample cans showing how the style handles different beers.
  3. Versions of the mascot or key motif at small sizes and in one colour.
  4. Clear ownership of the artwork, in writing, including use on merchandise and future packs.

The last point is often forgotten. If the contract only licenses the art for one can, every reuse becomes a new negotiation.

When to leave illustration out

Not every brewery needs it. A lager-focused brewery may be better served by strong type and colour, like the classic European lager labels. A premium or export range may want restraint. Illustration also costs money every time a new beer launches, so a brewery that releases many seasonals should weigh that against a template-based system.

The question to ask is simple: what will the shopper remember? If the answer is the drawing, make sure the drawing points back to the brewery. That link between a picture and a name is what packaging design is mostly about. Our guide to beer label design covers how to build it on the face of a can.

One practical shelf test before you commit: print three cans in the style, put them next to each other in a fridge and ask someone who has never seen them whether they come from the same brewery. If they hesitate, the system is not strong enough yet.

Common questions

Does every craft brewery need a mascot?

No. A mascot helps when it stands for something real about the brewery and can be used everywhere. Strong type and colour can do the same job for many breweries.

Who owns the artwork on my cans?

Whoever the contract says. Get written ownership or a broad licence covering packs, merchandise and future use before the first can is printed.

Can I use cartoon characters on beer?

Be careful. Styles that look aimed at children are a regulatory and reputational risk for alcohol in India. Keep the tone clearly adult.
