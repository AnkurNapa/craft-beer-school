Brewing a blonde ale for friends who drink lager

Blonde ale is a pale, clean and lightly hopped ale. It is the easiest bridge from mass-market lager to homebrew. Brewed cool and served cold, most lager drinkers will happily finish a pint.

Familiar enough for a lager drinker

Blonde ale is pale gold, clean and crisp, with a light malt sweetness and gentle bitterness. Typical numbers: original gravity 1.038 to 1.054, about 3.8 to 5.5% ABV, 15 to 28 IBU and colour around 3 to 6 SRM. It is an ale, but the clean profile comes close to a lager.

For Indian drinkers used to cold lager, the key features are clarity, high carbonation and no strong fruit or hop flavour. If you have the cold space, a true lager is another route. Our guide to your first lager versus your first ale covers that choice.

It is also one of the quickest beers to turn around. A blonde ale can go from brew day to glass in about three weeks: ten days in the fermenter, a few days of cold crashing and a week or so to carbonate. That makes it a sensible first batch for a party, as long as you plan the date backwards from serving.

A simple 20 litre recipe

For 20 litres at about 1.046:

  • 3.6 kg pilsner or pale malt (86%)
  • 0.3 kg Vienna malt (7%)
  • 0.2 kg light crystal or carapils type malt (5%)
  • 0.1 kg wheat malt for head retention (2%)
  • Mash at 65 °C for 60 minutes

For about 18 IBU, use roughly 15 g of an 8% alpha hop at 60 minutes and 15 g of a mild, floral hop at 10 minutes. Avoid strong citrus or tropical hops here. They push the beer toward pale ale and away from what your friends expect.

Cool fermentation is the whole game

Use a clean American ale yeast. Pitch at 16 to 17 °C and hold that temperature for the first four days. Then let it rise to 19 to 20 °C to finish.

Most lager drinkers notice fruitiness immediately. A blonde ale fermented at 28 °C, as it would be in many Indian rooms in summer, tastes of banana and pear. That is the most common reason a homebrewed blonde does not convert friends. A fridge with a controller or a water bath with frozen bottles is enough. Clarity matters as much as flavour

Lager drinkers expect a bright beer. Haze reads as wrong, even if it tastes fine. To clear a blonde ale:

  • Add a kettle fining such as Irish moss or Whirlfloc in the last 10 minutes of the boil.
  • Cold crash at 0 to 2 °C for three to five days after fermentation.
  • Add gelatin finings during the cold crash if you can.

Packaged drinking water or RO water with a little calcium chloride helps both clarity and a soft taste.

Transfer gently off the yeast and leave the last centimetre behind. A cloudy pour from the bottom of a bottle undoes all the work. Tell friends to pour bottle-conditioned beer in one smooth motion and leave the sediment.

Carbonate high and serve cold

Carbonate to about 2.6 to 2.7 volumes, more than a typical ale. For bottle conditioning, use around 7 g of sugar per litre and use bottles in good condition. Keg brewers can set the regulator according to a carbonation chart for their serving temperature.

Serve at 3 to 5 °C. A beer that tastes good cold but harsh when warm usually has too much bitterness or harsh water. Blonde ale should taste clean at both temperatures.

Pouring for friends

Pour into a clean, clear glass with an inch of head. Let your friends try it next to their usual lager if they like comparisons. Many people discover they prefer a fresher beer once they taste one. Serve water alongside, keep portions reasonable and make sure nobody drives after drinking.

When it does not convert them

Fruity means warm fermentation. Grainy or harsh usually means hard water. Too bitter means the hop charge is too high for lager drinkers. Flat or dull means low carbonation or oxidation. Each fix is small, but identifying the right one needs a trained palate. That is where structured sensory and style training saves batches.

Ask your friends before you design the beer

Before you brew, ask two lager-drinking friends what they like about their usual beer. Most will say cold, crisp and easy. Write those three words on your brew sheet and check every decision against them, from hop choice to carbonation.

Common questions

Is blonde ale the same as lager?

No. It is an ale fermented cool, but the clean, crisp profile is close enough that many lager drinkers enjoy it.

What temperature should I ferment blonde ale at?

Around 16 to 17 °C for the first four days, then 19 to 20 °C to finish.

How carbonated should blonde ale be?

Around 2.6 to 2.7 volumes, higher than most ales, to give a crisp, lager-like feel.
