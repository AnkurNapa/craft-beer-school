Negotiating your first brewer salary and joining terms

Most first-time brewers accept the first offer without asking anything. You can negotiate politely without risking the job. This guide covers what to ask, what to look for in the offer and what to get in writing.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Know what you are worth first

Before any negotiation, find out what similar jobs pay. There is no reliable public salary table for Indian brewing jobs, so you will have to ask. Talk to brewers you know, ask in brewing groups and check what equivalent roles in food and beverage plants pay in your city.

Pay varies a lot by city, brewery size and role. A brewpub in a large city may pay differently from a production brewery outside town. An assistant brewer role at a small brewpub is not the same as a shift brewer role at a large plant. Compare like with like.

Look at the whole package

A salary figure on its own can mislead. Ask about everything that comes with it:

  • Fixed pay and variable pay: what is guaranteed and what depends on targets.
  • Shift and overtime: whether early starts, late cleans and weekend brew days are paid extra or expected.
  • Statutory benefits: provident fund and other benefits that apply to your employment. Ask whether they are included in the figure quoted.
  • Health insurance: whether it is provided and who it covers.
  • Meals and accommodation: some brewpubs provide staff meals. Some breweries outside cities provide housing.
  • Travel: transport for early or late shifts if the brewery is hard to reach.

Two offers with the same headline salary can be very different once you add these up.

The joining terms that matter

Read these carefully before you sign:

  • Job title and duties: make sure they match what was discussed. Our guide to the assistant brewer job shows what is normal for the entry role.
  • Probation period: how long it is and what happens at the end of it.
  • Notice period: how much notice you must give and the employer must give.
  • Working hours and weekly off: brewing involves long days, so know your weekly off and how it is decided.
  • Non-compete and confidentiality: clauses that limit where you can work later or what you can share.
  • Training: whether the brewery supports courses or certification.

If a clause is unclear, ask. If something looks unreasonable, it is fine to ask for it to be changed or to take advice before signing.

How to ask without sounding difficult

Negotiation in a brewery is usually informal. The head brewer or owner often makes the offer in person. Thank them, show that you want the job and then ask your questions calmly.

Phrases that work: "I'm keen to join. Could you tell me how overtime and weekend brew days are handled?" or "I was hoping for a little more on the fixed pay. Is there flexibility? Could we review it after probation?" Asking for a review date is often easier for a small brewery than asking for more money now.

Never invent a competing offer. Brewing is a small industry and people talk.

Training as part of the deal

For a first brewing job, training can be worth more than a small pay rise. Ask whether the brewery will pay for or contribute to a course, give you time off to attend classes or let you shadow the head brewer on recipe and planning work.

Some breweries will agree to training support more easily than to higher pay, because it improves their own team. If they do, ask whether there is a condition attached, such as staying for a set period. Get that in writing too.

Get it in writing

Ask for a written offer letter or appointment letter before you resign from your current job. It should state your role, pay, benefits, working hours, probation, notice period and joining date. Verbal promises are easily forgotten when the person who made them leaves.

Keep copies of everything. If anything is promised later, such as a pay review after probation, ask for an email confirming it. Our guide to the brewer CV and interview covers the steps before the offer.

Thinking beyond the first job

Your first brewing job is mostly about learning. A slightly lower salary at a brewery where the head brewer teaches well, the equipment is good and you will touch every part of the process can be a better deal than a higher salary somewhere you will only clean tanks.

Ask yourself what you will be able to do after a year in this job. If the answer is a lot more than now, the offer is probably worth taking.

Common questions

Is it acceptable to negotiate a first brewing job offer?

Yes, if you do it politely. Asking about overtime, shift pay, probation and training is normal and shows that you take the job seriously.

What should a brewery offer letter include?

Your role, pay, benefits, working hours, weekly off, probation, notice period and joining date. Any training support or review promises should also be written down.

Should I accept lower pay for better training?

Often yes for a first job. Good training and wide experience usually lead to better pay later.
