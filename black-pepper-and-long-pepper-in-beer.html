Black pepper and long pepper in beer

Black pepper and long pepper are among India's oldest spices and among the least used in beer. They bring two things, aroma and pungency. The art is getting more of the first than the second.

Pepper gives aroma and bite from different compounds

The bite of black pepper comes mainly from piperine. Its aroma comes from volatile oils, including rotundone, the compound behind the peppery note in Syrah wine. The two behave differently. Piperine dissolves poorly in water and better in alcohol. The aroma oils are volatile and fade with heat and time.

That means a beer can be hot but not peppery, or peppery but not hot. Most brewers want a fresh, spicy aroma with only a gentle warmth at the back of the throat. Freshly cracked pepper, added late, gets you there.

Black pepper and long pepper are not the same spice

Black pepper is the dried berry of Piper nigrum, grown along the Malabar coast in Kerala and Karnataka. The larger berries are often sold under the Tellicherry name. Long pepper, or pippali, is Piper longum: small catkins that look like grey-black cones. It is hotter, sweeter and more aromatic than black pepper, with notes some describe as clove or nutmeg.

Long pepper was common in European cooking before chillies arrived and is still used in Indian kitchens and pickles. In beer it gives a rounder, warmer spice than black pepper. Buy it whole. Ground long pepper loses aroma fast.

How much to use

Spice strength varies by harvest and age, so treat these numbers as a first trial:

  • Black pepper: 3 to 8 grams of freshly cracked peppercorns for 20 litres.
  • Long pepper: 2 to 5 grams of crushed catkins for 20 litres.
  • Green or pink peppercorns: softer and fruitier, so up to double the black pepper rate.

The safest route is a tincture. Crack 10 grams of pepper into 100 millilitres of vodka, leave for three days, strain, then add measured drops to a 100 millilitre sample of beer until it tastes right. Scale to the batch. Alcohol pulls out piperine and aroma well. You also control the dose to the millilitre.

When to add pepper

For aroma, add cracked pepper in the last two to five minutes of the boil, or after fermentation in a mesh bag for two to four days. For more warmth than aroma, add it earlier in the boil, but expect the fresh spice note to fade.

The one floor detail that makes the difference: crack the pepper the day you use it, just enough to split each berry. Pre-ground pepper from a packet has lost much of its aroma and tastes dusty in beer. A mortar or a rolling pin in a clean bag does the job.

Styles where pepper belongs

Pepper works best where something else already hints at spice:

  • Saison: many saison yeasts make peppery phenols. A little real pepper underlines them.
  • Witbier: a pinch alongside coriander adds lift.
  • Stout and porter: long pepper with roasted malt gives warmth, like spice in a dark chocolate. Our roasted malts guide covers the base side.
  • Winter ales: pepper with ginger, cinnamon and orange peel.

Pepper rarely suits hop-forward IPAs, where its bite piles onto bitterness. If you want serious heat instead of spice, our guide to chilli in beer is the better place to start.

A worked long pepper saison for 20 litres

To try long pepper in its natural home, here is a simple saison aiming for about 1.052 original gravity, 25 IBU and around 6% ABV:

  • 3.8 kg Pilsner malt (86%)
  • 0.4 kg wheat malt (9%)
  • 0.2 kg table sugar in the boil (5%)
  • A clean bittering hop at 60 minutes to about 25 IBU
  • 3 grams crushed long pepper at two minutes before flameout
  • A saison yeast, pitched at 20 °C and allowed to rise to 25 to 26 °C

Mash at 64 to 65 °C for a dry, highly fermentable wort. Saison yeasts often attenuate to 1.004 or lower, which gives roughly (1.052 minus 1.004) x 131.25, or about 6.3% ABV.

The dry finish is what makes pepper work here. Residual sweetness would round off the spice; a dry beer lets it snap. If the pepper is too quiet after fermentation, add a few millilitres of tincture to the fermenter rather than more whole spice.

Reading the finished beer

Taste the beer at serving temperature, then again a week later. Pepper aroma fades in the keg or bottle while the bite tends to stay, so a beer that tastes balanced at packaging can turn hot and flat after a month. Aim slightly over on aroma, slightly under on heat.

Knowing which style a spice suits, at what level and from which point in the process, is what turns a novelty beer into a recipe worth repeating. That is a style design skill. It is what our longer course works on.

Your first step: make a black pepper tincture and a long pepper tincture tonight, then dose two glasses of the same saison or witbier and decide which spice you prefer.

Common questions

Does black pepper make beer spicy hot?

Only a little at normal doses. Most of the heat comes from piperine, which dissolves poorly in water, so freshly cracked pepper added late gives more aroma than burn.

What is long pepper?

Long pepper, or pippali, is Piper longum, a relative of black pepper with small cone-like catkins. It is hotter, sweeter and more aromatic than black pepper.

How much black pepper goes into 20 litres of beer?

Start with 3 to 8 grams of freshly cracked peppercorns, or use a vodka tincture to dose precisely.

Which beer styles suit pepper?

Saison is the classic match because many saison yeasts already taste peppery. Witbier, stouts and winter spiced ales also work.
