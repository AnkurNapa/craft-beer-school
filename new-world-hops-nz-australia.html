New Zealand and Australian hops

New Zealand and Australian hop breeding programmes produced some of the most distinctive aromatic hops available today, built around tropical fruit and thiol compounds that American and European hops rarely reach.

3 min read · Updated February 2024 · Craft Beer School

A younger, more deliberately bred hop family

Where noble hops descend from centuries of European tradition and American hops emerged through decades of selection, most New Zealand and Australian varieties are the product of relatively recent, deliberately targeted breeding programmes aimed specifically at intense, distinctive tropical aroma. This is part of why the family, as a whole, tends toward more unusual, sometimes polarising flavour profiles than the more familiar American varieties covered in our American hops guide.

Nelson Sauvin: the hop named after a grape

Nelson Sauvin, from New Zealand, is famous for a flavour profile tasters consistently compare to Sauvignon Blanc wine: gooseberry, white grape and a distinct tropical fruitiness. At a moderate 12 to 13 percent alpha acid, it is potent enough that most recipes use it in a supporting rather than dominant role, often blended with a milder hop to avoid the wine-like character overwhelming the beer.

Motueka and Riwaka: citrus with a lime edge

Motueka brings a bright lime and tropical citrus character at a lower 6.5 to 8.5 percent alpha acid, useful where a cleaner, more citrus-forward profile is wanted without Nelson Sauvin's wine-like intensity. Riwaka, related to Motueka but more concentrated, pushes the same citrus and passionfruit character further, typically used in smaller quantities as a result.

Galaxy and Vic Secret: the Australian tropical fruit hops

Galaxy, Australia's most widely planted aroma hop, delivers intense passionfruit and citrus at 13 to 15 percent alpha acid, and has become a standard component of hazy IPAs worldwide precisely because of that tropical fruit intensity. Vic Secret, also Australian, leans more toward pineapple and pine, a slightly different tropical profile that pairs well alongside Galaxy or Citra in a blended dry hop.

Using thiol-forward hops well

Much of the intense tropical character in this family, and in Citra from the American list, comes from volatile thiol compounds that are especially sensitive to oxidation and heat exposure. These hops reward late, cool handling: whirlpool and dry hop additions rather than long boils, and careful attention to the storage and freshness practices that keep those fragile compounds intact between harvest and brew day.

Why thiols behave differently from ordinary hop oils

Most hop aroma comes from oils already present in the raw hop, released during dry hopping or late boil additions. Thiols work differently: many exist in a bound, odourless form in the raw hop and are only released as free, aromatic compounds through yeast activity during fermentation, which is part of why the same hop can smell different depending on the yeast strain and fermentation conditions used alongside it. This yeast interaction is a genuinely active area of ongoing hop and yeast research, and it is part of why thiol-forward hops like Nelson Sauvin and Galaxy can produce noticeably different results across different breweries using identical hop lots.

Working New World hops into an existing recipe

Because their character is so distinctive, New World hops are often used as a smaller accent alongside a larger amount of a more neutral American hop from our American hops guide, rather than as the entire hop bill. A common approach adds 20 to 30 percent of the total late and dry hop weight as Nelson Sauvin or Galaxy for character, with the remainder made up of Citra or Mosaic for volume and a more familiar tropical backbone, giving a distinctive but not overwhelming result.

Sourcing these hops fresh in India can be harder than sourcing established American varieties, since supply chains for New Zealand and Australian hops are smaller globally. Where availability is limited, buying the smallest useful quantity and checking a supplier's stated harvest date, following the same freshness logic covered in our guide to hop storage and freshness, matters even more than usual given how much of this family's character depends on being used while genuinely fresh.

None of these varieties are inherently better than the American hops most brewers already know, they simply occupy a different part of the aroma spectrum. Treating them as an addition to a hop vocabulary rather than a replacement for Citra or Cascade is the more useful mental model for actually using them well in a recipe.

As with any unfamiliar hop, the cheapest way to learn one is a small single-hop batch built specifically to showcase it, rather than folding it into a complex recipe alongside three or four other varieties where its specific contribution is hard to isolate and judge on its own.

Common questions

Why is Nelson Sauvin named after a wine grape?

Because its aroma, gooseberry, white grape, tropical fruit, closely resembles Sauvignon Blanc wine, a comparison tasters made consistently enough that the name stuck.

What is the difference between Galaxy and Citra?

Both are intensely tropical, but Galaxy leans more toward passionfruit and citrus while Citra brings a broader mango and citrus profile. Many hazy IPA recipes use both together.

Why do New World hops need cooler, later additions?

Their signature tropical aroma relies partly on thiol compounds that degrade with heat and oxygen exposure, so late whirlpool and dry hop additions preserve more of the character than a long boil would.
