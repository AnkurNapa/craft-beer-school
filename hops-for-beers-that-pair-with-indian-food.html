Hop varieties that work in beers made for Indian food

A beer brewed to go with Indian food needs different thinking from one brewed to stand alone. Bitterness, aroma and carbonation all interact with spice, fat and chilli. Choosing hops with that in mind makes the beer a better partner on the table.

Why bitterness and chilli clash

Bitterness and capsaicin heat amplify each other. A strongly bitter beer makes chilli feel hotter. Chilli in turn makes bitterness feel harsher. Many drinkers have had the experience of a big IPA turning a pleasant curry into a painful one. Our guide to beer and spicy food heat explains why.

So the first rule for food-focused beers is moderate bitterness. For beers meant to sit beside spicy dishes, a range of around 15 to 30 IBU is a sensible starting point, depending on malt sweetness and body.

Aroma that matches the food

Indian dishes carry a lot of aroma: citrus from lime and lemon, herbs from coriander and mint, warm spices such as cumin, coriander seed and cardamom, coconut and curry leaf on the coast. Hops can echo or contrast these.

  • Citrus hops such as Citra, Cascade and Mandarina Bavaria echo lime, orange and lemon in chaats, tandoori marinades and coastal dishes.
  • Lemony and herbal hops such as Sorachi Ace, with lemon and dill notes, can work alongside coriander and fresh herbs.
  • Spicy and floral noble-type hops such as Saaz, Tettnang and Hallertau suit lighter lagers served with grilled and tandoori food.
  • Tropical hops such as Mosaic and Galaxy give a fruity lift that can suit richer, creamier curries.

These are suggestions, not rules. The best way to judge is to taste the beer with the actual dish.

Using hops late, not early

Most of the aroma you want comes from late kettle, whirlpool and dry hop additions. Most of the bitterness comes from early boil additions. For food beers, shift the hop load late.

A practical approach for a food-friendly pale ale is to bitter lightly with a clean high-alpha hop, then put the bulk of the hops in the whirlpool at around 80 °C and in a modest dry hop. That gives plenty of aroma with limited bitterness.

Our guide to how hops change aroma covers how each addition point shapes the result.

Matching beers to dishes

  • Tandoori and grilled dishes: a crisp lager or Kölsch-style beer with noble hops. Carbonation cuts through charred fat. The gentle hop spice sits under the smoke.
  • Biryani: a low-bitterness pale ale with citrus or tropical hops, or an amber with gentle hops, to stand up to whole spices and rich rice.
  • Coastal curries with coconut: a wheat beer or a pale ale with citrus hops. Coconut and lime suit citrus hop aroma.
  • Chaat and street food: a light, citrusy, low-bitterness beer that matches tangy and sweet notes.
  • Rich north Indian gravies: a malty amber or a softer pale ale, with hops in a supporting role.

Our Indian food and beer pairing guide goes into more dishes.

Hops to use with care

Some hops carry strong onion, garlic or catty notes in certain conditions, often described as dank or sulphury. In a beer meant for food already rich in onion and garlic, these can pile up. Use them in small proportions if at all.

Very resinous, piney hops at high bitterness tend to clash with chilli, as described above. Save them for beers meant to be drunk on their own.

Testing with real food

Build a small tasting with dishes from your own menu. Serve three or four candidate beers in small pours alongside each dish. Ask tasters to rate how the beer and dish work together, not just whether they like each beer on its own. Run it responsibly, with water, small pours and plenty of food.

Note which hops and bitterness levels come out best with which dishes. Over a few rounds, a pattern usually emerges that can guide recipe design for the whole tap list. This is sensory work. It improves with practice and training.

Carbonation and serving temperature

Hops are only part of the picture. Higher carbonation scrubs fat and spice from the palate and helps a beer feel refreshing with rich food. Serving too cold mutes hop aroma, while serving too warm with spicy food makes the beer feel heavy. Around 4 to 7 °C for lagers and pale ales is a good starting range.

Common questions

Do IPAs go well with spicy Indian food?

Strongly bitter IPAs often make chilli feel hotter. Lower-bitterness beers with citrus or tropical hop aroma usually pair better.

Which hops suit tandoori food?

Noble-type hops such as Saaz, Tettnang or Hallertau in a crisp lager are a good match for grilled and tandoori dishes.

How bitter should a food-pairing beer be?

Around 15 to 30 IBU is a sensible starting point for beers meant to go with spicy food, adjusted for malt and body.
