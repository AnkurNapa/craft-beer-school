English IPA

Before India Pale Ale became shorthand for citrus and pine, it was an English style built on earthy, floral hops and a firm malt backbone. This guide covers what the original template actually tastes like and why it never disappeared.

3 min read · Updated October 2025 · Craft Beer School

The original IPA, before the American version

English IPA developed in the 18th and 19th centuries as a strongly hopped pale ale brewed to survive shipment to British colonies, most famously India. The extra hops and higher alcohol acted as natural preservatives for a beer that spent months at sea, long before anyone thought about hop aroma as a selling point in its own right.

The style nearly disappeared through the 20th century as English brewing trended toward milder, less hoppy beers, and it was really the American craft brewing revival that pushed IPA back into the mainstream, albeit with a very different hop character.

What sets it apart from American IPA

English IPA runs a touch lower in ABV, typically 5 to 7.5% against 6 to 7.5% or higher for American versions, with a firmer malt backbone that keeps the beer feeling balanced rather than hop dominated. Bitterness sits moderate to firm, 40 to 60 IBU, but reads earthier and more restrained than the sharp, resinous bitterness of an American IPA.

Drinkers coming from American IPA sometimes find English IPA underwhelming at first, expecting the citrus punch of the American version and instead getting something quieter and more savoury. It rewards a second taste more than a first impression.

Hop character: earthy and floral, not piney or citrusy

Traditional English varieties, East Kent Goldings, Fuggles or Challenger, contribute earthy, floral, sometimes slightly spicy or marmalade-like aromas, a world away from the grapefruit and pine of Cascade or Simcoe. These hops are typically added for both bittering and late aroma, but rarely in the dry hop quantities common to American styles.

English hop varieties also tend to run lower in alpha acid than their American counterparts, which means brewers need larger quantities to hit the same bitterness target, changing the whole shape of the hop schedule rather than just the flavour.

A grain bill built to hold its own

English pale malt forms the base, often with a touch of crystal malt for colour and a caramel note that supports rather than competes with the hops. This malt presence is deliberate: English IPA is meant to taste like a balanced beer with strong hop character, not a hop delivery vehicle with malt as an afterthought.

Some traditional recipes also include a small proportion of invert sugar, which ferments cleanly and helps the beer finish reasonably dry despite the fuller malt bill feeding into it.

Yeast and fermentation

An English ale yeast, fermented around 18 to 20 degrees Celsius, contributes a light fruity ester, sometimes a touch of diacetyl in traditional examples, that rounds out the hop character rather than staying entirely neutral the way many American IPA yeasts do.

This yeast character is part of what gives English IPA its distinct personality even when the hop bill is broadly similar to an American recipe, since the fermentation itself is adding flavour rather than staying invisible.

Why the style never went away

Even as American IPA became the dominant global reference point, English IPA held on as the style brewers return to when they want hop character without the aggressive citrus punch, particularly for food pairing where a gentler bitterness works better alongside spiced or rich dishes. It also travels well historically, in the most literal sense, which is part of why it existed in the first place.

A well made English IPA can sit comfortably next to a curry or a roast in a way a heavily dry hopped American IPA sometimes cannot, since the earthier hop character does not clash with strong spice the way sharp citrus can.

Where it sits for Indian drinkers

Given the name's own history with British India, it is a style with an obvious local hook, though most Indian craft breweries today lean toward the American IPA template because that is what imported hop supply and drinker expectation favour. A brewery wanting to make the historical connection explicit has to hunt down English hop varieties specifically rather than substitute American ones and call it the same style. For the bigger, hop forward American version of this same lineage, see our double IPA guide.

It is also a genuinely useful reference point for anyone learning the IPA family as a whole, since brewing an English IPA well forces attention on malt and yeast balance rather than letting a big hop addition cover for a weaker recipe underneath.

Common questions

Is English IPA the original IPA style?

Yes. The IPA category began as an English pale ale brewed with extra hops to survive long sea voyages, well before American brewers developed their own more citrus and pine forward version.

Why does English IPA taste less bitter than American IPA?

It uses earthier, more restrained hop varieties and keeps a firmer malt backbone, so the bitterness reads as balanced rather than sharp and dominant.

Can I substitute American hops in an English IPA recipe?

You can, but the result will taste more like an American IPA. English varieties like Goldings and Fuggles give the earthy, floral character the style depends on.

Is English IPA weaker in alcohol than American IPA?

Generally a little, though the ranges overlap. English IPA typically runs 5 to 7.5% ABV, often at the lower end of what American IPA covers.
