Sustainability in brewing

For a small brewery, sustainability is less a marketing claim and more a set of operating habits around water, energy and waste that also happen to save money. Here is what is realistic at small scale in India.

3 min read · Updated July 2025 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

On this page

  1. Water reuse: the biggest lever at small scale
  2. Energy: chilling is usually the biggest draw
  3. Waste as a resource, not a problem
  4. Packaging choices carry a real footprint
  5. Sustainability without overclaiming
  6. Sustainability as a staff habit, not a policy document

Water reuse: the biggest lever at small scale

Brewing uses several litres of water for every litre of finished beer, and much of that is used for cleaning and cooling rather than becoming beer itself. Recapturing hot water from wort cooling for the next brew's cleaning cycle, and reusing final rinse water as an initial rinse for the next tank, are simple, low-cost habits that cut total water draw meaningfully without any new equipment purchase at all.

See brewery utilities: water, power and steam for the wider water picture and how it interacts with your site choice.

Energy: chilling is usually the biggest draw

Glycol chilling for fermentation and lagering typically consumes more electricity over a brewing cycle than heating the mash or boiling the wort, particularly in Indian ambient temperatures where the chiller is fighting the climate constantly rather than only during the boil. Insulating tanks and glycol lines properly, and sizing chillers correctly for the climate rather than for a cooler-climate spec sheet, reduces this load without touching the recipe at all.

A well-insulated brewery can run a meaningfully smaller chiller for the same result, which pays for the insulation many times over across a year of operation.

Waste as a resource, not a problem

Spent grain as livestock feed and, where feasible, using spent hops or yeast as compost inputs turn waste streams into small revenue or cost offsets rather than pure disposal costs that only ever drain the budget. This overlaps directly with brewery waste and spent grain, where the compliance side of the same streams is covered in more depth.

Packaging choices carry a real footprint

Cans are broadly more efficiently recycled at scale in India than glass, and lighter to transport per litre, which reduces fuel used in distribution across every trip a truck makes. Refillable kegs, reused for years, carry a lower lifecycle footprint per litre served than any single-use format, precisely because the same physical object does the job hundreds of times over.

Neither claim should be overstated into a marketing slogan, since the honest comparison depends on your specific transport distances and local recycling infrastructure, but the general direction is genuine and worth acting on.

Sustainability without overclaiming

Avoid vague sustainability claims on packaging or marketing that you cannot back with a specific, measurable practice, since India's advertising standards and increasingly aware customers both push back on greenwashing when they spot it. Say precisely what you do: this water is reused, this grain goes to a named farm, this can is a stated percentage of recycled aluminium, rather than a general claim of being eco-friendly.

Precision reads as more credible than adjectives, and it also happens to be true, which matters more than the phrasing ever will.

Sustainability as a staff habit, not a policy document

None of this needs to wait for scale or spare budget. The practices that actually reduce a brewery's footprint, closing a valve promptly, logging water use, sorting waste streams correctly, live or die on whether staff do them consistently on a busy brew day, not on whether they are written into a policy somewhere. Build these checks into the same batch record sheet your team already fills in for quality control, rather than as a separate sustainability form nobody reads after the first month.

A habit tracked alongside the numbers a brewer already cares about, gravity readings, temperatures, cleaning times, survives far longer than one tracked in isolation.

Involve the cellar and packaging team in setting these targets rather than handing down a policy from the office, since the people running the taps and the hose reels every day usually know exactly where the easiest savings actually sit.

Review the numbers quarterly rather than once a year, since small, regular course corrections catch a slipping habit long before it becomes an expensive one, whereas an annual review only reveals the problem after twelve months of waste has already gone unnoticed. Even a modest, well-run brewery that takes this seriously usually finds it has more room to improve than it expected once it actually starts measuring rather than assuming, and the improvement tends to show up on the utility bill within a month or two of paying proper attention.

Common questions

What is the easiest sustainability improvement for a small brewery?

Water reuse, particularly capturing wort-cooling water for the next cleaning cycle, is usually the lowest-cost, highest-impact change available at small scale.

Are cans more sustainable than bottles?

Generally yes on recycling efficiency and transport weight in the Indian context, though the full picture depends on your specific distances and local recycling access.

Does sustainability cost more for a small brewery?

Many of the most effective practices, like water reuse and proper insulation, reduce operating costs rather than add to them, so sustainability and cost control often align.

Is it safe to advertise a brewery as eco-friendly?

Only with specific, verifiable claims. Vague sustainability language without backing detail risks both credibility and, potentially, advertising standards scrutiny.
