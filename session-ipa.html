Session IPA

Getting real hop flavour and aroma into a beer under 5% ABV, without it tasting watery or hollow, is a genuine brewing challenge, not a marketing shortcut. Here is how the better examples manage it.

3 min read · Updated January 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

  2. The honest challenge: flavour without body
  3. Grain bill tricks that help
  4. Hopping proportionally harder
  5. Fermentation stays simple

A lower strength answer to a real problem

Session IPA emerged as brewers and drinkers wanted the hop character of a full strength IPA without committing to 6 or 7% ABV on a beer meant for a long evening. Typical strength runs 3.5 to 5% ABV, considerably lower than the 6 to 7.5% of a standard IPA.

It sits closer in strength to a pale ale than to the IPA family it borrows its hop intensity from, which is part of why some drinkers argue the name undersells how different the two experiences actually are.

The honest challenge: flavour without body

Lower starting gravity means less malt to carry flavour and body, which is exactly what makes this style harder to brew well than its ABV suggests. A poorly executed session IPA tastes thin and hollow, all hop aroma sitting on top of a beer with nothing underneath it, while a well made one still feels complete despite the lower strength.

This is also why session IPA has a reputation, deserved in some cases, as a style breweries use to stretch expensive hops across a cheaper base beer, which shows immediately to an attentive drinker as thinness rather than genuine balance.

Grain bill tricks that help

A modest amount of crystal malt or a small proportion of oats can add just enough body and mouthfeel to stop the beer feeling watery, without pushing the gravity or sweetness up meaningfully. Some brewers also mash at a slightly higher temperature, around 67 to 68 degrees Celsius, to leave a bit more unfermented dextrin behind for body at a lower final ABV.

A small amount of wheat malt can also help here, contributing to head retention and a fuller mouthfeel without much added sweetness, similar in principle to its role in a New England IPA but at a much smaller dose.

Hopping proportionally harder

Because there is less alcohol and body to carry flavour, session IPA often uses a hop rate per litre closer to a standard IPA than the lower gravity would suggest, concentrated in late boil, whirlpool and dry hop additions rather than long bittering boils that would make the beer taste harshly bitter relative to its size. Bitterness usually sits moderate, 30 to 45 IBU, letting flavour and aroma lead over raw bitterness.

Getting this ratio right, enough hops for real character without tipping into harsh bitterness on a thin base, is really the entire technical challenge of the style in one sentence, and it is the reason a mediocre session IPA is far more common than a mediocre standard IPA.

Fermentation stays simple

A clean American ale yeast at standard ale fermentation temperatures is all this style needs, since there is no high gravity stress to manage the way there is with a double IPA or imperial stout. The main fermentation goal is simply a clean base that lets the hop additions be the entire story.

Because there is so little else going on in the fermentation, any flaw, an underpitched yeast or a fermentation temperature swing, shows up more clearly in a session IPA than it would in a bigger, more forgiving beer with more flavour to hide behind. Consistency here matters more than experimentation.

Why it suits Indian drinking occasions well

Lower ABV beers pair naturally with the kind of long, food-heavy social drinking common at Indian gatherings, where a 7% double IPA can end an evening early in a way a 4.5% session IPA will not. It is also more forgiving of the country's patchy cold chain, since a slightly warm 4.5% beer is a smaller disappointment than a slightly warm 8% one. If you want the fuller, stronger end of the same hop family for comparison, see our pale ale guide.

It is a sensible style for a brewpub to keep on tap alongside its bigger, more intense offerings, giving a hop forward option to drinkers who want flavour without committing to a long, heavy evening.

A common mistake is treating session IPA as simply a diluted IPA. Cut the grain bill proportionally without adjusting mash temperature and the beer turns thin and watery rather than light and drinkable. A slightly higher mash temperature, sitting nearer the top of the usual range, helps retain body at a lower starting gravity.

Common questions

Is session IPA just a weaker regular IPA?

Not exactly. A well made session IPA is specifically engineered, through grain bill and hopping choices, to deliver real hop character despite the lower strength, rather than simply being a diluted version of a stronger recipe.

Why does some session IPA taste thin or watery?

Usually the grain bill lacked enough body-building ingredients like crystal malt or oats to compensate for the lower starting gravity, leaving hop aroma with nothing underneath it.

What ABV range counts as session IPA?

Typically 3.5 to 5% ABV, noticeably lower than a standard IPA's 6 to 7.5%.

Does session IPA use less hops than a regular IPA?

Not necessarily by weight relative to volume. Many recipes actually use a hop rate close to a standard IPA to compensate for the smaller malt backbone.
