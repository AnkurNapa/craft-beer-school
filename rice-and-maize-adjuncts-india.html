Rice and maize adjuncts in Indian brewing

Rice and maize adjuncts have shaped the flavour of mainstream Indian lager for decades. Understood properly, they are a legitimate brewing tool rather than a cost-cutting compromise.

3 min read · Updated October 2022 · Craft Beer School

What an adjunct is actually doing in the grain bill

Rice and maize (corn) bring starch and very little else, no significant protein, colour or flavour of their own once processed. Used alongside a barley base malt, covered in our guide to base malts, they dilute the malt character and body of a beer while adding fermentable extract, which produces the light, crisp, highly drinkable profile associated with most mainstream Indian lagers. This is a deliberate stylistic choice as much as an economic one: rice and maize were, and remain, more available and often cheaper than imported specialty malts in much of India.

Why they need a cereal mash

Unlike malted grain, raw rice and maize starch will not gelatinise, become accessible to enzymes, at typical mash temperatures. Rice starch gelatinises around 61 to 78 degrees Celsius depending on variety, and maize starch around 62 to 72 degrees, both often above a standard single infusion mash temperature, and both need a proper boil in a separate cereal cooker to fully open up. Flaked rice and flaked maize (corn) are pre-gelatinised during processing specifically to skip this step, which is why most small breweries and homebrewers use the flaked form rather than raw grits.

How much a grain bill typically carries

Mainstream Indian lagers commonly run somewhere between 15 and 40 percent adjunct, with the remainder being barley base malt, though the exact figure for any commercial brand is proprietary. Craft breweries experimenting with the style tend to use flaked rice or maize in the 10 to 20 percent range, enough to lighten body and sharpen the finish without pushing the beer toward tasting thin.

Enzyme headroom matters more than usual

Because rice and maize contribute starch but no enzymes of their own, the barley base malt in the grist has to supply enough diastatic power to convert both its own starch and the adjunct's. A highly modified Pilsner malt with strong enzyme reserve handles this comfortably up to fairly high adjunct percentages; a lower enzyme base malt, or one already carrying a large percentage of low-enzyme specialty malt from our guides to crystal and caramel malts or roasted malts, may struggle to convert a large adjunct addition fully.

The flavour trade-off, honestly stated

Adjuncts genuinely reduce malt complexity, that is the point of using them, and a beer built around 40 percent rice will never taste as characterful as an all-malt Pilsner. What they buy in exchange is a lighter body, a drier finish and lower cost, all of which suit a hot climate and a market used to that style of lager. Used deliberately, in a recipe designed around that trade-off, rice and maize are a legitimate tool rather than a compromise forced by circumstance.

A style choice with a long history, not a modern shortcut

Adjunct lager brewing is not a recent cost-cutting trend in India, it reflects decades of established brewing practice shaped by what was locally available and affordable at scale, long before the current wave of craft breweries began experimenting with all-malt recipes as a point of differentiation. Both traditions are valid brewing approaches aimed at different outcomes, a light, easy-drinking lager suited to a hot climate on one hand, and a more characterful, malt-forward beer on the other, and neither is inherently the correct way to brew.

Craft brewers choosing to work with adjuncts today are usually doing so deliberately, either to hit a lighter, more sessionable style intentionally or to manage ingredient costs in a market where imported specialty malt carries real freight and duty costs that vary and should be checked directly with a supplier rather than assumed.

If sourcing flaked rice or maize locally proves difficult, plain rice flakes (poha) or cornflakes without added sugar or flavouring, found in most Indian grocery stores, can work as a starting substitute for small scale experimentation, though a dedicated brewing-grade flaked adjunct will behave more predictably at a larger scale.

Cost comparisons between adjuncts and imported specialty malt change with exchange rates, freight costs and import duty, all of which shift over time, so any specific rupee figure quoted for either ingredient is only accurate at the moment it was checked. Get current pricing directly from a supplier rather than relying on a number from an older source.

Common questions

Do rice and maize adjuncts always mean lower quality beer?

No. They are a deliberate stylistic choice that produces a lighter, crisper lager. The quality question is whether the recipe and process around them are executed well, not whether adjuncts are present.

Can I use raw uncooked rice in a mash?

Not effectively. Raw rice starch needs a cereal mash, a separate boil, to gelatinise before enzymes can convert it. Flaked (pre-gelatinised) rice skips this step and mashes in directly.

Why do adjunct lagers taste lighter than all-malt lagers?

Rice and maize dilute the proportion of barley malt in the grist, and malt is where most of a beer's body and flavour complexity comes from, so a higher adjunct percentage means a lighter, simpler result.
