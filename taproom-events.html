Taproom events that bring people back

Most taproom events are planned the week before, run once and never repeated in the same form. This guide is about building a calendar people can rely on instead.

3 min read · Updated September 2023 · By Ankur Napa and Chatty Girija

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer
Written by and point of contactChatty GirijaQuestions about this guide? WhatsApp her

On this page

  1. Repetition builds the habit
  2. Design the event around the beer, not around the entertainment
  3. Food changes who shows up
  4. Capacity and licensing come first
  5. Use the event to introduce what is next
  6. Merchandise turns a good night into a lasting one

Repetition builds the habit

A one-off event asks people to remember something new. A recurring one asks them to remember a day of the week, which is a much smaller ask. Live music every Friday, a quiz on alternate Thursdays, or a new-release tasting on the first Saturday of the month all work because regulars stop deciding whether to come and start simply showing up, the same way people know which evening the gym class runs without checking a calendar.

The format matters less than the reliability. A modest event that happens every time beats an ambitious one that happens once and is never repeated because it was too much work to organise a second time. Pick something your current staff can run without extra hires, because a format that depends on one exhausted manager collapses within a few months.

Announce the recurring slot everywhere at once, the door, the menu, the social feed, so that a first-time visitor learns about next week's event while they are still at this week's.

Design the event around the beer, not around the entertainment

Live music and quizzes fill a room, but they do not teach anyone anything about your beer. A room full of people who came for the band may never taste past their usual order. Build at least one recurring event that puts your beer at the centre: a guided tasting of the current seasonal release, a meet-the-brewer evening where the head brewer talks through what changed in a recipe, or a small vertical tasting of the same beer across a few batches to show how a recipe evolved.

These events cost little beyond staff time and a few extra pours. They turn casual drinkers into people who can describe why they prefer one beer over another, which is exactly the kind of customer who becomes a repeat buyer and an unpaid advocate among friends.

Food changes who shows up

A taproom that pairs its beer with real food, even a simple in-house kitchen or a rotating local vendor, draws a different and often larger crowd than one serving beer alone. Indian drinkers frequently treat beer as something to have alongside food rather than instead of it. An empty stomach also shortens a session considerably, which is bad for the till and bad for the atmosphere you are trying to build.

A pairing menu tied to your current beer list, even a short one, gives staff something specific to recommend rather than a generic snack list. It gives the kitchen a reason to rotate dishes alongside your seasonal releases rather than running the same fixed menu all year.

Capacity and licensing come first

Before building a calendar, confirm what your specific licence actually allows: music after certain hours, outside vendors on the premises and maximum occupancy are all governed by state and local rules that vary considerably from one city to the next, sometimes even between neighbouring municipalities. An event that has to be cancelled at the door because of a licensing gap costs you more in trust than never announcing it at all.

Talk to your local authority before printing posters, not after. Keep a written note of what was confirmed in case the person you spoke to changes roles later.

Use the event to introduce what is next

A taproom event is a natural moment to tell your most engaged customers about a beer before it launches more widely. Pour a small taster of the next seasonal release at an existing event rather than saving it for a separate announcement and you get honest, immediate feedback along with word of mouth that starts before the can is even printed. Our guide to launching a new beer covers how to sequence that properly so the event and the launch reinforce each other instead of competing for attention.

Merchandise turns a good night into a lasting one

A well timed cap or glass sold at the door of a popular recurring event moves better than the same item sitting on a shelf, because the person buying it is already having a good time and wants to remember it. See our brewery merchandise guide for what is actually worth stocking behind the bar. Treat event nights as the moments to sell it rather than the quiet Tuesday afternoon when nobody is thinking about the brand at all.

Common questions

How often should we run events?

A recurring event every one or two weeks, kept simple enough to run without heroics, works better than an elaborate monthly event that exhausts staff and is easy to let slip.

Do events need a licence beyond our normal one?

Often yes, for things like live music after certain hours or outside vendors. Check with your local authority and your state excise department before committing to a format publicly.

What is the cheapest event that still works?

A guided tasting of a beer you are already pouring, led by whoever brewed it, costs almost nothing beyond staff time and gives people a reason to come that a generic happy hour does not.
