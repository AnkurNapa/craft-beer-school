Tasting yeast character: banana, clove and pepper in beer

Some of the most distinctive flavours in beer are made by yeast alone. Banana, clove and black pepper show up in German wheat beers, Belgian ales and saisons with no fruit or spice added. Here is where they come from and how to taste them.

Flavour without fruit or spice

Most people assume a beer that tastes of banana has banana in it. In a German hefeweizen it does not. The recipe is malted wheat, barley malt, a few hops and a very particular yeast. Everything else comes from fermentation. That makes these beers the clearest place to learn what yeast contributes.

Banana: an ester

The banana note is mostly isoamyl acetate, an ester. Esters are fruity compounds yeast makes as it ferments. Different strains make very different amounts. Hefeweizen yeast is a heavy producer of isoamyl acetate.

It smells of ripe banana, sometimes banana bread or pear drops. If you have eaten the small, sweet Elaichi bananas sold across India, that perfumed ripeness is close to what a good hefeweizen gives. Our guide to esters and fusel alcohols covers the wider family.

Clove: a phenol

The clove note is 4-vinyl guaiacol, a phenol. Only certain yeast strains can make it. They convert ferulic acid, which comes from the malt, into this clove-scented compound. Brewers call these strains phenolic off-flavour positive, or POF+, a slightly awkward name for a trait that is entirely wanted in wheat beer.

Some brewers hold the mash at around 43 to 45 °C for a short ferulic acid rest to release more of it and push the clove. In a pale lager or an IPA, the same compound from a stray yeast would be a fault. Our guide to phenolic off-flavours explains when phenols are wanted and when they are not.

Temperature sets the balance

In a hefeweizen, banana and clove compete. Fermentation temperature is the main dial. Warmer fermentation, around 20 to 22 °C, tends to push banana. Cooler fermentation, around 17 to 18 °C, tends to bring clove forward. Pitch rate and wort oxygen matter too, which is why two breweries using the same strain can make quite different beers.

In Indian conditions a fermenter that runs hot can tip a hefeweizen into a banana and bubblegum bomb, with a hot fusel edge on top. Temperature control matters as much here as for any lager. Our hefeweizen guide has the details.

Pepper: the saison signature

Saison yeast gives a peppery, spicy dryness. Much of that comes from phenols, including 4-vinyl guaiacol, alongside fruity esters and a very dry finish, because saison strains ferment almost all the sugar. It reads as cracked black pepper rather than clove, partly because there is less banana around it and the beer is drier and more bitter. Our saison guide covers the style.

Belgian ale strains add another layer: fruit like pear, apple and plum, often with a light spice.

A three-glass yeast tasting

Set up three small pours of about 100 ml each, served at 7 to 9 °C in tulip or wheat beer glasses:

  1. A hefeweizen. Look for banana and clove. Which is louder?
  2. A witbier. Spicy and orangey too, but here most of the spice is added coriander and orange peel, not yeast. Compare the two kinds of spice.
  3. A saison. Look for pepper, dryness and fruit.

Smell each one, then go back to the first. Swirl, because yeast aromas rise as the beer warms. Write down the single strongest note in each glass before you look for anything else. Drink water between glasses and keep the pours small.

When yeast character becomes a fault

Too much of a good thing is still a problem. A hefeweizen fermented far too warm can smell of bubblegum and solvent rather than banana, with a hot alcohol edge from fusels. A saison that stalls before it finishes can be sweet and flabby instead of dry and peppery. Clove that turns into plaster, smoke or medicine has gone past spice into a phenolic fault, often from wild yeast.

The test is balance. In a good wheat beer you notice banana and clove, then bread and a light sharpness from the wheat. In a poor one the yeast notes shout over everything else.

Yeast or added spice?

The witbier comparison is the useful one. Yeast clove is soft and blends into the beer. Added spice tends to sit on top: coriander is citrusy and floral, real clove is sharp and woody. With practice you can tell a beer where the yeast made the spice from one where the brewer added it.

Few people can do this reliably on their first try. It takes reference samples and repetition until the compounds become familiar, the same way you learned to recognise cardamom or cumin in food.

Common questions

Is there banana in hefeweizen?

No. The banana flavour comes from isoamyl acetate, an ester produced by the yeast during fermentation.

Why does my hefeweizen taste more of banana than clove?

Warmer fermentation usually pushes banana. Fermenting around 17 to 18 °C tends to bring out more clove.

Where does the pepper in saison come from?

Mostly from the yeast, which produces spicy phenols and ferments the beer very dry. Some brewers also add spices, so check the label.

Is clove a fault in beer?

Only when it is not intended. In hefeweizen and many Belgian styles it is expected. In a lager or IPA it usually points to wild yeast or contamination.
