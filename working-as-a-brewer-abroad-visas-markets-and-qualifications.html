Working as a brewer abroad: visas, markets and qualifications

Working abroad can give a brewer experience that is hard to get at home. It also involves immigration rules that change often and employers who need proof you can do the job. Here is how to think it through.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Why brewers look abroad

The reasons are usually practical. Some want exposure to large, highly automated breweries. Some want to learn lager brewing in Germany or the Czech Republic, cask ale in the UK or the hop-forward styles of the US and Australia. Others want to work in a market where craft beer has existed for decades and roles are more specialised.

It is worth being clear about which of these you want, because each points to a different country, a different type of employer and a different route in.

Markets that hire brewers

Germany, the UK, Ireland, the US, Canada, Australia and New Zealand all have large brewing industries with many small breweries. Smaller breweries in these markets often hire locally and rarely sponsor visas, because sponsorship costs time and money. Larger breweries, multinational brewing groups and equipment or ingredient suppliers are more likely to sponsor a skilled hire.

Another route is internal: joining an Indian operation of a multinational brewer and moving through an internal transfer later. That path is slower but avoids many of the visa obstacles of applying cold.

Qualifications employers recognise

Employers abroad want evidence you can brew safely and consistently. Commonly recognised options include:

  • The Institute of Brewing and Distilling (IBD) examinations, from the General Certificate in Brewing up to the Diploma in Brewing and Master Brewer.
  • University degrees in brewing and distilling, such as the programmes at Heriot-Watt University in Edinburgh or the brewing courses at TU Munich at Weihenstephan.
  • Practical brewing schools such as VLB Berlin and Doemens in Germany.

A degree or diploma alone rarely lands the job. Documented brewery experience matters more, so log your production work, the size of the brewhouse you ran and the styles you made. Our guide to brewing certifications compares the main options.

Visas: what to understand before you apply

Immigration rules differ by country and change frequently, so treat anything you read secondhand, including here, as a starting point. Some general patterns hold:

  • Most skilled work visas need a job offer from an employer approved to sponsor, plus proof of qualifications and sometimes language skills.
  • Some countries run points-based or occupation-list systems where brewing may or may not appear. Check the current official list, not a forum post from three years ago.
  • Student visas let you study brewing abroad. Some countries allow a period of post-study work afterwards. Read the conditions on the official government site before paying fees.

Use official government immigration websites and, if your case is complicated, a registered immigration adviser. Be wary of agents who promise brewery jobs for a fee.

Language and culture

In Germany, Austria and the Czech Republic, working German or Czech makes a big difference on the brewery floor, even if the office speaks English. Safety briefings, SOPs and shift handovers are often in the local language. In English-speaking markets the language barrier is smaller, but terms and habits differ: a cellarman in the UK, a cellar operator in Australia.

Money, timing and a plan B

Budget for the whole process, not only the visa fee. Qualification exams, translations of certificates, health insurance, flights and the first months of rent in an expensive city add up quickly. Some countries also ask for proof of funds. Work out the total in rupees before you resign from a job at home.

Give yourself a realistic timeline too. Exams, applications and visa processing can each take months. A sensible plan B is to keep building experience in India while you apply, ideally in a brewery with good documentation and a mix of lager and ale styles. That experience strengthens your application whether the first attempt works or not.

Building a strong application from India

Start with solid fundamentals and a clear record of what you have brewed. Get your CV into the format used in the target country. Our guide to the brewer CV and interview covers what hiring brewers look for.

Many applicants come from homebrewing or a short taproom job and lack a structured grasp of the process: why a mash rest exists, how yeast health shows in a fermentation curve, how to troubleshoot a slow lauter. Interviewers abroad test exactly those things, often with a practical question on the spot.

Common questions

Can I get a brewing job abroad without a degree?

Sometimes, especially with strong production experience. Recognised qualifications like IBD exams help an employer and an immigration officer see that you are skilled.

Which country is easiest for Indian brewers?

There is no single answer, because visa rules change and depend on your profile. Check the official immigration sites for the countries you are considering and focus on employers that already sponsor skilled workers.

Should I study brewing abroad first?

It can open doors, but it is expensive. Compare the cost against the post-study work rights and the job market in that country before you commit.
