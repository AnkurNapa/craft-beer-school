Imported or Indian-made brewhouse compared on cost and service

Indian founders can buy a brewhouse from European or Chinese manufacturers or from fabricators in India. Each route has strengths. Here is how we compare them on what actually matters over ten years of brewing.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer
Point of contactChatty GirijaQuestions about this guide? WhatsApp her

On this page

  1. Compare total cost, not the quote
  2. Build quality and steel
  3. Lead time and delivery
  4. Service and spares decide the real cost
  5. Commissioning and handover
  6. Controls and automation
  7. Sizing and layout
  8. Making the decision

Compare total cost, not the quote

The ex-works price is only the first line. An imported system adds freight, insurance, customs duty, port charges, clearing, inland transport and often the cost of flying in a technician to commission it. An Indian-made system may need more of your own time to manage the build and check the details.

Then add the costs of the next ten years: spares, service visits, downtime and energy. A brewhouse that is cheaper by a few lakhs but stops for two weeks waiting for a part from abroad has lost that saving in beer not sold. Our guide to buying brewery equipment lists the lines to include.

Build quality and steel

Good fabricators exist everywhere. So do poor ones. Judge the kit, not the country. Ask for:

  • The stainless steel grade for each vessel and part, with mill certificates.
  • Weld quality on product-contact surfaces: smooth, fully penetrated, ground and passivated.
  • Internal finish specification and how it is measured.
  • Pressure ratings and test certificates for fermenters and brite tanks.
  • Jacket design and insulation for Indian ambient temperatures.

Visit a working brewery that uses the same supplier's kit and talk to the brewer, not just the owner.

Lead time and delivery

Imported systems usually take longer from order to commissioning because of manufacture, sea freight and customs clearance. Indian fabricators can sometimes deliver faster, but timelines slip if their order book is full. Either way, tie payments to milestones, such as drawings approved, vessels fabricated, factory acceptance test passed and commissioning complete, rather than paying most of it upfront.

Licensing timelines matter too. If your excise licence needs installed capacity declared, the equipment schedule and the licence schedule must line up.

Service and spares decide the real cost

This is where the choice is usually won or lost. Ask each supplier:

  • Who services the kit in India? How fast can they reach your city?
  • Which spares are standard parts you can buy locally? Which are proprietary?
  • What spares kit comes with the system? What do they recommend you stock?
  • How long is the warranty, what does it cover and who pays for travel?

Standard fittings, valves, pumps and motors that are easily available in India reduce downtime. Read about brewing on an imported plant for a brewer's view of what living with one involves.

Commissioning and handover

Agree in the contract what commissioning includes: water trials, a first brew with the supplier's technician present, calibration of temperature probes and flow meters, training for your brewers and a handover pack. That pack should hold drawings, wiring diagrams, valve lists, manuals and a recommended maintenance schedule. Suppliers who are vague about the handover are often vague about support later.

For imported kit, check who pays for the technician's travel and stay if commissioning takes longer than planned because of delays on your side, such as a late power connection.

Controls and automation

Automation ranges from manual valves and a simple temperature panel to full PLC control with recipe management. More automation can improve consistency, but only if someone can fix it. Ask who wrote the control software, whether you get the source code and passwords and whether someone in India can support it. A locked panel supported only from abroad can stop a brewery for days over a sensor fault.

Sizing and layout

Whatever you buy, size it for your plan. A brewhouse that is too small means brewing twice a day. One that is too big wastes capital and makes small batches awkward. Our brewhouse size guide covers the arithmetic. Confirm the supplier's drawings fit your site, with access for cleaning, maintenance and future tanks.

One practical test of any supplier: ask how long their kettle takes to reach a rolling boil from mash-out temperature at full volume. Then ask a customer to confirm it. Undersized heating is a common corner cut. It affects boil vigour and DMS removal on every brew.

Making the decision

Build a comparison sheet with total landed cost, ten-year spares and service estimate, lead time, references and your gut feeling after the site visits. Weigh service and support heavily. Founders who do this usually find the decision is about which supplier will answer the phone in three years, not about where the steel was welded.

Equipment is often the biggest single cheque a brewery writes. Making that call on solid numbers is a core part of planning the business.

Common questions

Is an imported brewhouse better than an Indian-made one?

Not automatically. Build quality varies by supplier in every country. Compare steel, welds, controls, service and spares for the specific suppliers you are considering.

What costs do people forget with imported kit?

Freight, insurance, customs duty, port and clearing charges, inland transport and the cost of a commissioning technician.

Why do spares matter so much?

Downtime waiting for a proprietary part can cost more than any saving on the purchase price. Prefer standard parts available locally.

How should I structure payments?

Tie them to milestones such as approved drawings, completed fabrication, factory acceptance and commissioning, rather than paying most upfront.
