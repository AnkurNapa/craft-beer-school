Sladek hops for a fruitier Czech style pale lager

Sladek is a Czech aroma hop with the spicy backbone of its homeland and a gentle fruity, floral character on top. It is a useful tool for brewers who want a Czech style lager with a little more lift.

A Czech hop named for the brewer

Sladek was bred at the Hop Research Institute in Žatec. Its name comes from the Czech word for a brewmaster. Like most modern Czech varieties, it was bred to keep the character Czech brewers expect while yielding better and standing up to disease.

Alpha acid is moderate, usually around 5 to 8%. The aroma sits on a Saaz like base of spice and herbs, then adds fruit and flowers: tasters mention light citrus, stone fruit and sometimes a faint tropical note. It is gentle, which is why it works in lager.

Fruit without leaving the style

A Czech premium pale lager runs around 30 to 45 IBU with a spicy, floral hop aroma and a rich but clean pale malt body. The style does not want an IPA's fruit salad. It does allow some range. Sladek sits at the fruity end of what still reads as Czech.

That gives a brewer a way to make a pale lager feel more approachable, with a softer aroma for drinkers who find a pure Saaz beer a little grassy or austere. In a taproom where most drinkers are new to lager beyond mainstream brands, that can make the difference between a beer they try once and one they reorder.

Using it in the boil and whirlpool

The fruit is carried in the oils, so the later the addition, the more fruit you keep. An early addition gives bitterness with a little spice. A late addition or whirlpool gives the fruit and flowers.

For a 1,000 litre Czech style pale lager at 35 IBU, one approach:

  • 60 minutes: a clean bittering hop or Sladek for about 24 IBU. With Sladek at 6% alpha and 28% utilisation, that is 24 x 1,000 / (0.06 x 0.28 x 1,000), about 1,430 g.
  • 20 minutes: Saaz for about 7 IBU, to keep the classic spice.
  • Whirlpool around 80 °C: Sladek at about 1 g per litre for the fruity aroma.

The whirlpool will add a few IBU too, so measure the finished beer once and adjust the bittering charge.

Pairing with its Czech siblings

Sladek blends easily with other Czech hops. With Saaz it gives a classic beer with a gentle fruity lift. With Kazbek it brings fruit and lemon together for a brighter, more modern pale lager. With the high alpha Agnus or Premiant handling the bitterness, Sladek can carry all the aroma work.

For a single hop beer, Sladek alone makes an interesting study: spicy enough to taste Czech, fruity enough to show what the breeding added. Our guide to hop oil chemistry explains why a late addition keeps the fruit that a long boil drives off.

The process still matters most

No hop rescues a lager with fermentation faults. Czech pale lagers traditionally carry a little more residual body than German ones, but they should be clean: no sulphur left after lagering, no green apple from rushed fermentation and only as much diacetyl as the style tolerates, which for most drinkers outside the Czech Republic is none.

Ferment cold, typically 8 to 12 °C, allow a diacetyl rest as fermentation finishes and lager for several weeks near 0 °C. A soft fruity hop like Sladek is quickly masked by any off flavour, so it doubles as a test of how clean your process really is.

Keeping the fruit in the bag

Fruity aroma oils are among the first things to fade when hops are stored warm or exposed to air. Keep Sladek frozen at around minus 18 °C in sealed foil. Bring out only what you need on brew day. In India, where hops often travel long distances before they reach a brewery, check the harvest year and packing date on every lot. A hop that smells flat in the bag will not smell better in the beer.

Serve it the Czech way

A soft hop aroma needs help to reach the drinker. Czech pubs pour pale lager with a generous, dense head, served around 6 to 8 °C rather than ice cold. The foam carries aroma to the nose and the moderate temperature lets the fruit show. Very cold beer numbs the palate and hides exactly what Sladek adds.

Czech pouring styles range from a standard pour with a firm head to the mostly foam mlíko. You do not need to copy them all, but a clean glass, a proper head and a serving temperature a few degrees above the fridge make a Sladek lager taste noticeably fruitier. Ask your bar team to pour two glasses: one cold and flat, the other at 7 °C with a good head. Then taste the difference.

Where to start

If you already brew a Czech style pale lager, try replacing half the whirlpool Saaz with Sladek. Keep the same weight and the same timing. Taste the beers side by side after lagering. That single change tells you whether your drinkers want a fruitier lager before you redesign the whole hop bill.

Common questions

What does Sladek taste like?

A spicy, herbal Czech base with gentle fruit and floral notes, often described as light citrus and stone fruit.

What alpha acid does Sladek have?

Usually around 5 to 8%, higher than Saaz.

Can I use Sladek as the only hop in a Czech lager?

Yes. It can bitter and aroma a pale lager on its own, though many brewers keep some Saaz for the classic spice.
