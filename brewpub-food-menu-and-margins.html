Food at a brewpub: menu design and margins

A brewpub kitchen can carry the business or quietly drain it. We look at how to cost each dish, how long the menu should be and how food and beer should work together on the same bill.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

On this page

  1. Why the kitchen matters more than founders expect
  2. Cost every plate before it goes on the menu
  3. Keep the menu short
  4. Design food that sells beer
  5. Read the menu as a margin map
  6. Where the floor bites

Why the kitchen matters more than founders expect

Most brewpub founders start with the beer and treat food as the thing the licence or the landlord insists on. Then the first month's numbers arrive and the kitchen turns out to be the biggest line of cost after rent.

Food does two jobs in an Indian brewpub. It keeps people seated long enough to order a second and third beer. It also brings in the guests who do not drink at all. Every group has some. A weak kitchen hurts the beer sales even when the beer is excellent.

Cost every plate before it goes on the menu

Food cost is the raw ingredient cost of a dish divided by its menu price before tax. Many Indian restaurant operators aim for somewhere around 30 percent across the menu, with some dishes higher and some lower. The exact target is yours to set, but you cannot manage a number you never measured.

For each dish, write a recipe card with:

  • every ingredient by weight, including oil, garnish and the chutney on the side
  • the purchase price per kilo and the usable yield after trimming (a whole chicken does not become 100 percent boneless meat)
  • the portion size the kitchen must actually plate

Then re-cost the cards whenever a major ingredient moves. Onion and tomato prices in India can swing hard within a season and a fixed menu price does not swing with them.

Keep the menu short

A long menu feels generous to the guest and punishing to the kitchen. Every extra dish means more stock, more waste and more ways for a busy Saturday to fall apart. For a first menu, something in the range of 20 to 30 items is plenty for most brewpubs, built around a small set of base ingredients that appear in several dishes.

Cross-utilisation is the quiet trick. The same marinated chicken can go into a tikka starter, a wrap and a rice bowl. The same fries base can carry two or three toppings. Fewer ingredients means fresher stock and a tighter food cost.

Design food that sells beer

Salt, spice, char and fat all make people reach for a cold glass. That is why bar snacks look the way they do. Think chilli peanuts, masala fries, tandoori starters, Kerala-style fried chicken, pork chilli fry, paneer tikka and fish fry.

Write the menu with the tap list in mind. A crisp lager or wheat beer next to fried and spicy starters is an easy match. A malty amber or a stout carries smoky tandoor flavours and grilled meats. Our guide to pairing beer with Indian food goes deeper. Put one suggested beer under a few dishes and let the staff talk about it. Pairings are suggestions, never rules.

Read the menu as a margin map

Once each dish has a cost and a sales count, sort them into four groups:

  • Popular and profitable. Protect these. Keep them consistent.
  • Popular but thin margin. Re-engineer the portion or the recipe before you raise the price.
  • Profitable but slow. Move them up the page or have staff recommend them.
  • Slow and thin. Remove them at the next menu change.

Your POS should give you the sales count. The cost comes from your recipe cards. Doing this every quarter is where most of the margin is found.

Watch the comped and wasted plates as well. A kitchen that throws away a tray of prepped paneer every Sunday night has a food cost problem that no recipe card will show. Log waste daily for a month and the pattern usually becomes obvious.

Where the floor bites

The kitchen and the brewhouse often share a building, a drain line and an exhaust budget. A tandoor running at full heat beside a fermentation room adds load to the glycol chiller on a May afternoon. Plan the layout so the hot kitchen is not leaning against the cold side.

Food and beer also sit under different tax regimes in India. Food is billed under GST while beer falls under state excise and state taxes, so your menu prices and your billing system have to keep the two apart. The rules vary by state and change, so confirm the current position with your accountant before you print a menu.

This is the point where many founders realise the brewpub is two businesses under one roof, each with its own costing logic. Pricing the beer side is covered in our beer pricing guide.

Common questions

What food cost percentage should a brewpub aim for?

Many Indian restaurants work to roughly 30 percent across the menu, but the right figure depends on your rent, staffing and price point. Measure it per dish first, then set your own target.

How many dishes should a new brewpub menu have?

Fewer than most founders think. Around 20 to 30 items built on shared base ingredients is enough to open with. It also keeps waste and training manageable.

Should a brewpub price food low to sell more beer?

Some do, but cheap food with a thin margin only works if the beer sales really follow. Track food and beer per cover before deciding that food is a loss leader.
