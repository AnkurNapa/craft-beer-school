Molasses spirit versus grain spirit in India

Behind most Indian spirits brands sits a highly purified base spirit made from one of two raw materials. This guide explains both in plain terms and shows why the difference reaches your desk.

3 min read · Updated August 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Two feedstocks behind most Indian spirits

Most mainstream Indian whisky, rum, vodka and gin starts as extra neutral alcohol, or ENA. It is spirit distilled to around 95% ABV and purified until little flavour of the raw material is left. ENA is made from one of two broad feedstocks: sugarcane molasses or grain. The blender then builds a brand on top of it with malt or grain spirit, flavours and colour.

Molasses: what the sugar mill leaves behind

Sugar mills crush cane, boil the juice and crystallise sugar out of it. The dark syrup left after the sugar is removed is molasses. It still contains fermentable sugars, which yeast turns into alcohol.

India is one of the world's large sugarcane growers, so molasses has long been the cheaper and more plentiful feedstock for spirit. Molasses does not need cooking or enzymes, because its sugar is already free. It is diluted, fermented and distilled in multi-column plants that strip out unwanted compounds and concentrate the alcohol. How much spirit a tonne of molasses gives depends on how much sugar the mill left in it.

Grain: rice, maize and other cereals

Grain distilleries use broken rice, maize, sorghum and other cereals. Unlike molasses, grain holds its sugar as starch. It must be milled, cooked to open up the starch and treated with enzymes to convert it to sugar before fermentation. Distillation then follows the same multi-column route to ENA.

Grain plants also produce a useful by-product: the spent grain is dried into animal feed, known as distillers' dried grains with solubles. Molasses distilleries produce a strong liquid effluent called spent wash, which pollution rules require them to treat and dispose of carefully.

Does the base spirit change the taste?

This is the question new joiners ask first, usually after someone in a meeting has said grain spirit is smoother with total confidence. At ENA strength the differences are small, because purification removes most raw-material character. Many blenders still find grain ENA softer on the palate, while molasses spirit can carry a faint trace of its origin. In a finished blend the malt or grain spirit, flavouring and dilution matter more than the base. On a tasting bench next to each other, though, a trained nose can often tell them apart.

Why the label cares

Scotch, Irish, American and European Union rules define whisky as spirit made from cereals. Spirit from molasses cannot be sold as whisky in those markets. Indian standards take a different approach and allow whisky to be blended from neutral spirit with malt or grain spirit. The exact wording sits in the current food standards, which change, so check them rather than relying on memory.

For a brand with export plans, this is a production decision made years in advance. A recipe built on molasses ENA may need a grain-based version for some markets. Our guide to whisky styles of the world sets out those rulebooks.

Feedstock economics

Molasses supply follows the sugarcane crop, which follows the monsoon. States also control how molasses is allocated and moved. The rules vary by state. Grain prices follow their own harvests and government procurement. Both feedstocks are also used for fuel ethanol under the national blending programme, so spirits makers compete with fuel for the same raw material.

A shift in that balance moves the cost of ENA, which moves the cost of almost every mainstream bottle.

Supply models differ too. Some producers run their own distilleries and make ENA in-house. Others buy it from independent distillers and sugar mills with attached distilleries. Buying gives flexibility but exposes the company to market prices. Making it in-house brings control along with the full cost of running a plant, its effluent treatment and its compliance work. The same brand can sit on either model. The cost line looks quite different in each.

What it means at your desk

A cost report shows ENA as one line. Behind that line sit a sugar season, a grain harvest, state allocation rules and a fuel policy. An analyst who reads it as a simple commodity price will be surprised every year. One who knows what drives it can explain the variance before the review meeting asks.

Common questions

What is ENA?

Extra neutral alcohol: spirit distilled to around 95% ABV and purified until little raw-material flavour remains. It is the base of most mainstream Indian spirits.

Is molasses spirit worse than grain spirit?

Not worse, different. At neutral strength the differences are subtle. Grain spirit is often described as softer and it meets cereal-only whisky rules abroad.

Can molasses-based whisky be sold in Europe?

Not as whisky. EU rules, like Scotch, Irish and American rules, require whisky to be made from cereals.

Why do Indian ENA costs move so much?

Molasses supply follows the cane crop, grain follows its own harvests and both feed fuel ethanol too. State allocation rules add a further layer.
