Cans versus bottles

Choosing a package format is partly a cost and logistics decision, covered elsewhere, and partly a branding decision: what a can or a bottle signals about the beer inside. Here is that side of it.

3 min read · Updated July 2022 · By Chatty Girija

Written byChatty GirijaBeer Podcaster · Creative Strategist

On this page

  1. What each format signals, rightly or not
  2. The canvas each format actually gives you
  3. Protection has a branding consequence too
  4. Range consistency across formats
  5. Choosing based on your actual beer and brand
  6. Cost is still part of the branding decision

What each format signals, rightly or not

In much of the Indian market, bottles still carry a slightly more premium or traditional association for some drinkers, while cans have shifted rapidly toward being seen as the modern, craft-forward choice, partly because so many well-regarded craft breweries globally have moved to cans over the last decade. Neither association is fixed or universal, and it varies by drinker segment and by region, but it is worth knowing which signal your target customer is more likely to read before you choose one over the other.

The canvas each format actually gives you

A can offers a full 360-degree printable surface with strong, vivid colour reproduction, which suits bold, illustration-led or highly graphic label design that needs room to breathe. A bottle typically carries a smaller front-and-back label on a glass surface that also refracts light, changing how colour actually appears to the eye compared with a flat printed proof.

Test your actual label art on the real material and shape you plan to use, not just as flat artwork on a screen, since colour and detail read differently on each surface.

Protection has a branding consequence too

Cans block light and oxygen more completely than bottles, protecting hop aroma and flavour for longer, which matters directly to hop-forward styles that are popular precisely because of their aroma and would suffer more from any protection gap. A beer brand built around a big, aromatic IPA has a genuine, honest reason to choose cans beyond aesthetics, and can say so on the label without overstating the claim.

Clear or green glass bottles, in particular, risk lightstruck character if exposed to light, which undercuts exactly the flavour the brand is trying to sell in the first place.

Range consistency across formats

Some brands run bottles for a flagship or premium line and cans for a wider, more accessible range, using the format itself to signal positioning within their own portfolio of beers. This works, but only if the visual identity stays clearly connected across both, otherwise customers may not recognise the flagship and the accessible range as coming from the same brand at all.

See beer label design that works for how to keep that identity consistent across formats and price points.

Choosing based on your actual beer and brand

Match the format to both the practical needs of your specific beer, hop-forward styles benefit more from can protection, and to the story you are actually telling about your brand to the drinker holding it. See designing a beer can for the specifics of working within a can's printable surface once you have made this decision.

Cost is still part of the branding decision

A branding choice that the business cannot actually afford is not really a choice, so weigh the format decision against your realistic production volume, since minimum order quantities for custom-printed cans or bottles can make either option uneconomical at very small batch sizes. Some new breweries start with plain cans and a strong sticker label, or a simpler one or two colour bottle label, to keep costs sane while volume builds toward a quantity that justifies a full custom print run.

This is a pragmatic middle step, not a compromise on brand quality, provided the simpler design is still executed with the same care given to hierarchy and legibility, since drinkers judge the label in front of them, not the production method behind it.

Revisit the format decision once a year or once volume roughly doubles, since the right answer for a brewery's first six months is rarely the right answer once it has a proven, repeatable seller on its hands.

Treat the first format choice as a working starting point rather than a permanent commitment, since the goal early on is to keep the beer moving and the brand consistent, not to lock in a packaging decision the business has not yet earned the volume to justify, and a founder willing to revisit the choice usually makes a better one the second time round, once real sales data has replaced the early guesswork.

A common mistake is switching from bottles to cans partway through a brand's life without revisiting the whole design system. A label built for a rectangular bottle face rarely wraps well around a can's curve, and a rushed resize usually looks like exactly that, a resize rather than a considered choice.

Common questions

Do cans or bottles look more premium?

It varies by drinker segment and region, and neither association is fixed, though bottles retain some traditional premium association for certain customers while cans read as modern and craft-forward for others.

Which format is better for hop-forward beers?

Cans, generally, since they block light and oxygen more completely than bottles, better protecting the aroma compounds that hop-forward styles depend on.

Can a brand use both bottles and cans?

Yes, and some brands do, often using bottles for a premium line and cans for a wider range, but the visual identity needs to stay clearly connected across both.

Does label art look the same on a can and a bottle?

No. Cans offer a full printable wraparound surface with vivid colour, while bottle labels sit on refractive glass, so always test actual artwork on the real material before finalising.
