Designing a beer can

A can gives a brand a full wraparound canvas, but that canvas curves, gets handled, and gets seen from odd angles in a fridge. Here is how to design for the object it actually is.

3 min read · Updated September 2022 · By Chatty Girija

Written byChatty GirijaBeer Podcaster · Creative Strategist

On this page

  1. Design around the curve, not against it
  2. Print process shapes what you can actually achieve
  3. Legibility in a fridge, not just on a screen
  4. Consistency across a range without repetition
  5. Bringing the name and format together on the can
  6. Minimum order quantities shape your options early

Design around the curve, not against it

Text and key graphic elements placed near the can's side seams can distort or become hard to read as the surface curves away from the viewer, so keep critical information, brand name, style, ABV, toward the front-facing centre of the design where it stays legible. Wraparound illustrations can work beautifully, but test them printed on an actual can mock-up, not just on a flat digital proof, since flat and curved artwork read quite differently to the eye.

Print process shapes what you can actually achieve

Most can printing uses a limited number of ink colours applied in registered passes, which rewards bold, distinct colour blocks over subtle gradients or photographic detail that can look muddy or misregistered on a production run at scale. Talk to your can supplier about their specific print process and colour limitations before finalising artwork, since a design that looks perfect on a screen can print inconsistently if it assumes capabilities the actual production line does not have.

Legibility in a fridge, not just on a screen

Cans are commonly viewed cold, wet with condensation, and often at an angle inside a crowded fridge door, all of which reduce legibility compared with a clean studio photograph of the finished artwork under ideal lighting. Test your design printed, chilled and wet, under normal shop lighting, before committing to a full print run that cannot easily be redone.

Small type and fine detail that read clearly on a monitor frequently disappear under these real, everyday conditions.

Take the mock-up out of the studio entirely, into a real shop fridge or your own taproom cooler if you have one, since even a good office simulation of cold, wet, dim conditions rarely matches the real thing closely enough to trust completely, and the extra half hour this takes is cheap insurance against a full print run that turns out to be harder to read than anyone realised on a screen back in the design studio.

Consistency across a range without repetition

If you are designing multiple beers in one range, keep a shared structural template, logo placement, information hierarchy, a base layout, while varying colour and any illustrative element by beer style. This lets customers recognise the brand instantly across a fridge shelf while still telling different styles apart at a glance, echoing the colour-as-information point in beer label design that works.

Bringing the name and format together on the can

The name chosen through the process in naming a beer brand, and the format decision covered in cans versus bottles, both converge here: the can is where the brand's identity actually meets a drinker's hand for the first time. Get a physical printed sample before committing to a full production run, whatever the design looks like on a screen beforehand.

Minimum order quantities shape your options early

Custom-printed cans typically need a minimum order quantity per design that can be a meaningful commitment for a young brewery still validating a recipe, so weigh a fully custom print against a shrink-sleeve or sticker label applied to a plain can, which usually allows much smaller runs at a modest cost premium per unit. This lets a brewery test a new beer or a limited release without over-committing to a print quantity it may not sell through while the beer is still fresh.

Reserve fully custom can printing for beers with proven, repeatable demand, where the lower per-unit cost at volume actually pays off.

Ask your can and label suppliers for their actual minimum order figures early in the design process, since designing a range around a print method you cannot yet afford at your current volume only means redesigning later once the real numbers arrive.

Get quotes from more than one supplier before finalising a print method, since minimum order sizes and per-unit costs can vary considerably between vendors offering what looks, on paper, like the same basic service, and a founder who only requests one quote rarely knows whether it was actually competitive.

A frequent mistake is finishing the design before confirming the exact can dimensions and print template from your chosen supplier. Wrap height, seam allowance and dome curvature differ slightly between manufacturers, and a design built to the wrong template will either crop badly or leave an awkward gap near the seam.

Common questions

Should I avoid putting important text near the can's seam?

Yes. Text and detail near the side seams can distort or become harder to read as the surface curves, so keep critical information toward the front-facing centre.

Does can printing limit what colours or detail I can use?

Often yes. Most can printing uses a limited set of registered ink passes, favouring bold, distinct colour blocks over subtle gradients or fine photographic detail.

Why test a can design chilled and wet?

Because that is how it is actually seen in a fridge or in a drinker's hand, and legibility and colour can look noticeably different under those conditions than on a clean screen proof.

How do I keep a range of beers looking like one brand?

Use a shared structural template for logo placement and information hierarchy, then vary colour or illustration by individual beer to distinguish styles within that consistent frame.
