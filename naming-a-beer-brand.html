Naming a beer brand

Naming a beer brand is part creative decision, part legal check and part practical test across the languages and states you will actually sell in. Here is how to narrow the field properly.

3 min read · Updated November 2021 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. Start with what the name has to do
  2. Search before you fall in love with a name
  3. Test it across language and region
  4. Fit with the label rules you will actually face
  5. Committing to the name
  6. Names to be genuinely careful with

Start with what the name has to do

A beer name has to be memorable enough to ask for by name at a bar, distinct enough to trademark, and printable within the label rules of whichever states you plan to sell in. Chasing cleverness before checking these three constraints wastes time on names you will have to abandon later once the legal or practical problems surface. Write your shortlist against all three before getting attached to any single option, however much you like it after the first brainstorm.

Search before you fall in love with a name

Run a trademark search early, not after you have designed a label and ordered cans, since discovering a conflict late is expensive and can force a full rebrand at the worst possible moment. India's trademark register is searchable, and a beer name too close to an existing registered mark, even in an unrelated category in some cases, can create real legal risk that a founder does not want to discover after investing in packaging.

This is also the point to check domain availability and social handles, since a name locked up elsewhere online is a genuine handicap even if the trademark search clears without issue.

Test it across language and region

A name that reads well in English can land oddly, or mean something unintended, in Hindi, Kannada, Marathi or another regional language, and India's beer market spans all of them across different regions. If you plan to sell beyond one state, test the name's pronunciation and connotation with people from your target markets, not just your immediate circle of friends and colleagues.

This matters more for beer than for many products, because the name is said aloud, at a bar, far more than it is read silently on a page or screen, and a name a bartender fumbles over is a name that quietly loses orders every single night.

Fit with the label rules you will actually face

Whatever name you land on needs to sit cleanly on a label alongside the mandatory information every state requires, ABV, volume, manufacturer details, and the specific text and warning placement rules for beer labels covered in beer label rules in India. A very long name, or one that needs a lot of surrounding explanation, makes label layout harder than it needs to be for a designer working within tight constraints.

Committing to the name

Once a name clears trademark, translates cleanly, and fits your label constraints, resist the urge to keep second-guessing it against every new idea that comes up during design, since that path leads nowhere useful. A good-enough name that is legally clean and consistently used builds recognition faster than a marginally better name changed twice in the first year of trading.

Names to be genuinely careful with

Steer clear of names that reference religious figures, symbols or festivals, which several states restrict specifically for alcohol branding, and names that lean on a place name or community identity you have no real connection to, which can read as opportunistic rather than authentic once the brand is public. A name that pokes fun at intoxication or plays up excessive drinking as a selling point also sits awkwardly against most states' advertising standards for alcohol, beyond simply being poor branding.

None of this is a comprehensive legal list, since restrictions vary by state, but it is worth a deliberate pause before locking in a name that leans on any of these ideas.

If in doubt, run a shortlist past a licensing consultant alongside your trademark search, since they will often have seen a name rejected for exactly this reason before and can save you a wasted design cycle.

A cautious approach here costs a little creative freedom but protects you from a far more expensive and public problem than a slightly less daring name ever would, and the shortlist that survives this filter is usually still plenty distinctive once you actually sit down and look at what remains.

Common questions

Should I check trademarks before designing a label?

Yes, always, and as early as possible. A trademark conflict discovered after label design and can printing is expensive to fix and can force a full rebrand.

Does a beer name need to work in more than one language?

If you plan to sell beyond one state or region, yes. Test pronunciation and meaning across the languages of your target markets before committing.

Can a long or complex name cause label problems?

It can. Beer labels must carry mandatory information alongside the brand name, and a long or intricate name makes fitting everything clearly much harder.

How do I know when to stop iterating on a name?

Once it clears trademark search, translates cleanly and fits your label layout, further changes usually cost more in delayed recognition than they gain in polish.
