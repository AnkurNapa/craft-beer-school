Berliner weisse

Berliner Weisse is one of the lightest, tartest beer styles still brewed, and understanding how it gets sour tells you a lot about how sour beer works generally.

3 min read · Updated August 2023 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

A very old, very light Berlin tradition

Berliner Weisse is a centuries old style from Berlin, a low alcohol, lightly sour wheat beer that was reportedly popular enough with occupying French troops in the early 1800s to earn the nickname champagne of the north, more likely for its pale colour and lively carbonation than any real similarity to actual champagne. By the twentieth century the style had nearly disappeared, kept alive by only a handful of producers before a wider craft revival brought it back.

It remains one of the lowest alcohol traditional beer styles still commonly brewed, generally 2.8 to 3.8% ABV.

What to expect in the glass

Berliner Weisse pours very pale straw, often slightly hazy, with minimal aroma beyond a light grainy tartness. Bitterness is extremely low, 3 to 8 IBU, since hops would clash with the sourness rather than support it, and the finish is bright, sharp and refreshingly tart rather than aggressively sour.

Traditionally it is often served with a flavoured syrup, woodruff for a green tinge and herbal sweetness, or raspberry for a red tinge and fruitiness, called a Schuss, though a well made Berliner Weisse is genuinely enjoyable unadorned too.

How the sourness actually gets there

Traditional Berliner Weisse used a mixed fermentation with both Saccharomyces yeast and Lactobacillus bacteria working together, a slower, less predictable process that can take weeks to reach the right balance of sourness and dryness. Most modern breweries instead use kettle souring, adding Lactobacillus to the wort before the boil, souring it over a day or two at a controlled temperature, then boiling to kill the bacteria before pitching a clean yeast for a standard, predictable fermentation.

Kettle souring is faster and far easier to control consistently, which is why most small breweries producing this style today use it rather than the slower traditional mixed fermentation.

Why this is a genuinely useful entry point into sour beer

Berliner Weisse's low alcohol, low hop and low malt intensity mean the sourness itself has to be clean and well controlled, since there is nothing else in the beer to distract from a flaw. A brewery learning to manage Lactobacillus safely, avoiding unwanted wild contamination, controlling souring temperature and pH, often starts here before attempting a more complex sour style.

Cleanliness and separation of sour and clean fermentation equipment matter enormously with any Lactobacillus process, since cross contamination into a regular clean beer line can be a costly, hard to fix mistake.

A dedicated kettle sour vessel, even a simple one used only for this purpose, is worth the investment once a brewery is producing sour beers regularly, since it removes the ongoing risk of a shared vessel carrying bacteria into a batch that was never meant to be sour at all.

The grain bill stays simple

A roughly even split of wheat and pilsner malt is standard, kept simple deliberately so the tartness and light body carry the beer rather than any malt complexity. Because the beer is so pale and delicate, any oxidation or off flavour shows up quickly, so gentle handling and good packaging discipline matter more here than the recipe complexity might suggest.

Carbonation is usually kept high, contributing to the lively, prickly mouthfeel the style is known for, so check your carbonation stone and CO2 pressure carefully rather than relying on the setting you use for a fuller bodied ale.

Serving it, with or without the syrup

Serve very cold, 3 to 6°C, traditionally in a wide, shallow bowl glass, though a standard tulip works fine too. If using a Schuss syrup, add it to taste rather than following a fixed measurement, since tartness levels vary between batches and producers.

Its very low alcohol and bright acidity make it a genuinely refreshing option in Indian heat, closer to a shandy in drinking occasion than a typical craft beer, without actually being one.

A taproom offering both a plain pour and a syrup option side by side is a good way to introduce new drinkers to the style, letting them decide for themselves whether they prefer the traditional sweetened version or the sharper unadorned beer, and most first timers have a strong opinion either way.

Common questions

Is Berliner Weisse always served with syrup?

No, that is traditional but optional. A well made Berliner Weisse is enjoyable on its own, and the woodruff or raspberry syrup, called a Schuss, is a regional custom rather than a requirement.

How is Berliner Weisse soured?

Traditionally with a mixed fermentation of yeast and Lactobacillus bacteria together. Most modern breweries use kettle souring instead, souring the wort with Lactobacillus before boiling and then fermenting normally with clean yeast.

Is Berliner Weisse a strong beer?

No, it is one of the lowest alcohol traditional styles still brewed, generally 2.8 to 3.8% ABV.

Is Berliner Weisse a good beer for someone new to sour beer?

Yes. Its clean, straightforward sourness with minimal other flavour complexity makes it a genuinely useful introduction to how sour beer works before trying a more complex style.
