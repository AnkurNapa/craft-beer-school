Keeping a milkshake IPA balanced rather than cloying

The milkshake IPA is a hazy IPA with lactose, fruit and often vanilla. It is meant to taste rich and indulgent, but the line between luscious and cloying is thin. Here is how to build one that people order a second time.

3 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

What makes it a milkshake

The base is a hazy IPA: a soft grist with oats or wheat, a fruity yeast, low bitterness and heavy late and dry hopping. Our New England IPA guide covers that foundation. To make it a milkshake IPA, brewers add lactose (milk sugar) for sweetness and body, fruit purée for flavour and colour and often vanilla for a creamy, dessert-like aroma.

Each addition pulls the beer towards sweetness. Balance is the whole game.

Lactose: how much is enough

Lactose is not fermented by brewer's yeast, so every gram you add stays in the beer as sweetness and body. Typical rates run around 20 to 40 g per litre, added in the last 10 to 15 minutes of the boil so it dissolves fully and is sanitised. Below that range the effect is subtle. Above it, the beer starts to taste heavy and sugary.

Lactose raises final gravity. Where a hazy IPA might finish around 1.012 to 1.016, a milkshake IPA often finishes at 1.018 to 1.024. That is intended, but it means the beer needs something else to stop it tasting syrupy. Lactose is also a milk product, so it must be declared as an allergen on labels and menus. Check the current FSSAI labelling rules for the exact wording.

Acidity is your best friend

The single most useful tool for balance is acidity. Fruit brings its own: mango, passion fruit, raspberry and pineapple all lower pH and add brightness. A milkshake IPA with a finished pH around 4.0 to 4.3 tastes much fresher than one sitting at 4.6. If the fruit you use is low in acid, such as banana or very ripe mango, consider pairing it with a sharper fruit, or a small addition of food-grade lactic or citric acid at packaging, tasted in carefully.

In India, good-quality fruit purées and pulps are easy to find. Alphonso or Totapuri mango pulp suits the style well. Check the Brix and acidity of each batch of purée, because they vary through the season and adjust the addition rather than pouring in the same volume every time.

Bitterness and hops

A milkshake IPA should have low but present bitterness, often around 20 to 35 IBU. Too little and the sweetness takes over. Too much clashes with the lactose and fruit. Dry hops matter more than bittering hops: fruity varieties such as Citra, Mosaic, Galaxy and El Dorado reinforce the fruit and add aroma that cuts through sweetness. Heavy dry hopping also adds some perceived bitterness and astringency, which helps balance.

Vanilla is easy to overdo. Real vanilla pods or a good extract added at the end of conditioning, a little at a time with tasting, gives control. Once vanilla is in, it does not come out.

Fruit, refermentation and safe packaging

Fruit purée adds fermentable sugar. Add it after primary fermentation and the yeast will start again, raising alcohol and producing CO2. Give it time to finish fully before packaging, check gravity is stable over two or three days and taste for diacetyl, which a second fermentation can produce.

This is the safety point. Packaging a fruited beer before refermentation has finished can lead to over-carbonated cans or kegs. In Indian heat a can sitting warm can swell or burst. If fermentation is not certain to be complete, keep the beer cold throughout distribution or pasteurise it. Our guide to adding fruit to beer covers timing and rates.

Tasting for balance

The test is simple: would someone want a second glass? Taste the beer at serving temperature, not straight from a cold tank. Look for fruit up front, creamy body in the middle and a finish that is clean enough to make you want another sip. If the finish lingers as sugar, cut the lactose or push the acidity next time. If it tastes thin, add a little more oats or lactose.

Balancing sweetness, acidity, bitterness and body is the core skill behind every adjunct-heavy beer, from pastry stouts to fruited sours. It is the kind of judgement that improves with structured practice and feedback rather than trial and error alone.

Common questions

How much lactose should go in a milkshake IPA?

Typical rates are around 20 to 40 g per litre, added late in the boil. Start at the lower end and adjust, since lactose does not ferment and stays as sweetness and body.

Why does my milkshake IPA taste cloying?

Usually too much lactose or residual sugar without enough acidity or bitterness to balance it. Raise acidity with sharper fruit, cut the lactose or increase dry hopping.

Do I need to label lactose in beer?

Yes. Lactose is a milk product and should be declared as an allergen. Check the current FSSAI labelling requirements for the exact format.
