Hop pellets or whole cone?

Pellets, whole cone and the concentrated lupulin powders in between each trade off differently on utilisation, storage and mess. For most brewers in India, the choice is more practical than romantic.

3 min read · Updated April 2024 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  2. Pellets: the practical default
  3. Concentrated pellets and lupulin powder
  4. What actually changes in the finished beer
  5. A sensible default for most brewers

Whole cone hops: tradition, and a genuine drawback

Whole cone (or leaf) hops are the dried flower essentially unchanged from harvest, and they are what most people picture when they imagine a hop. They absorb wort as they float and settle, typically 6 to 10 times their dry weight, which reduces your usable batch volume and needs a false bottom, hop bag or hop spider to filter effectively during transfer. In India, whole cone hops are also harder to source fresh, since almost none are grown domestically and imported cones have already spent time in transit before reaching a brewer.

Pellets: the practical default

Type 90 pellets are whole cones milled and compressed, retaining roughly 90 percent of the original cone's weight and most of its resin content in a much smaller, denser form. They absorb far less wort, pack more compactly for shipping and storage, a genuine advantage over long import distances, and generally give slightly higher utilisation than whole cone hops of the same alpha acid, because the milling process exposes more surface area to the wort.

Concentrated pellets and lupulin powder

Type 45 pellets and cryogenically processed lupulin powders (sold under names like Cryo Hops) concentrate the lupulin glands, where most of the alpha acid and aromatic oils actually live, separating them from the vegetal plant matter of the cone. These give more bitterness and aroma per gram, useful where shipping weight or storage space is genuinely limited, at a higher cost per gram than standard pellets.

What actually changes in the finished beer

Careful side by side trials generally find the flavour difference between pellets and whole cone, at equivalent alpha acid and freshness, is smaller than most brewers expect, and well within the batch to batch variation covered in our guide to keeping a brew log. Freshness at the point of use, covered fully in our guide to hop storage and freshness, has a far bigger effect on the final aroma than the format the hop was purchased in.

A sensible default for most brewers

Pellets are the practical choice for nearly everyone brewing in India: easier to source, better shipping economics, easier to filter and lower wort loss. Whole cone hops remain worth using when a specific brewery or homebrewer has reliable access to fresh cones and values the traditional character of brewing with them, but that is a preference rather than a quality requirement, and it applies to any of the American or New World varieties regardless of which form they are bought in.

Storage behaviour: pellets have a real edge

Pelletising compresses the hop and reduces its exposed surface area relative to whole cone material of the same weight, which slightly slows the oxidation process covered in detail in our guide to hop storage and freshness. Combined with vacuum sealing or nitrogen flushing, standard practice for commercially packaged pellets, this gives pellets a genuine practical advantage in a market like India's, where hops routinely spend weeks in transit and storage before reaching a brewer, compared to whole cone hops which are both bulkier to ship refrigerated and more exposed to oxygen once a package is opened.

For a brewery in India specifically, the practical recommendation is close to unavoidable: build a hop programme around pellets as the default, reserve whole cone for a specific traditional beer where the format itself is part of the story you are telling the drinker, and treat lupulin powder as a tool for situations where minimising shipped weight or storage space genuinely outweighs its higher cost per gram of usable bitterness or aroma.

Cost per usable gram of alpha acid or aroma oil is the fairest way to compare formats directly, since a lower price per kilogram on whole cone hops can be misleading once the extra wort absorption and lower shelf stability are factored in. Working this comparison out once for your own typical batch size and supplier pricing is more useful than following a generic rule of thumb from elsewhere.

If you do want to try whole cone hops at least once, a small single batch using fresh, well stored cones from a reliable supplier, tasted directly against the same recipe made with pellets, is the fairest way to form your own opinion rather than relying on secondhand claims about which format tastes better.

Common questions

Do pellets give a worse flavour than whole cone hops?

Not meaningfully, at equal freshness and alpha acid. Freshness at the point of use matters far more to the final aroma than whether the hop was pelletised.

Why are pellets more practical in India specifically?

Almost no hops are grown domestically, so everything is imported. Pellets pack denser, ship more efficiently and store better over the transit time that reaching India involves.

What is lupulin powder and is it worth the extra cost?

A cryogenically concentrated form of the lupulin glands where alpha acid and aroma oils live, giving more potency per gram. It is worth it mainly where shipping weight or storage space is the binding constraint.
