Rauchbock explained

Rauchbock combines two German traditions: the smoked malt of Bamberg and the strength of a bock. The result is a rich, smoky lager of around 6 to 7% ABV. Here is where it comes from, what it tastes like and what to eat with it.

Bamberg's smoky speciality

Bamberg, in Franconia in northern Bavaria, is the home of smoked beer. Before modern kilns, most malt was dried over open fires and picked up some smoke. As indirect kilning spread in the nineteenth century, smoky beer disappeared almost everywhere. Bamberg's brewers kept the old method and turned it into a speciality.

Their everyday smoked beer is rauchbier, usually a Märzen-strength amber lager. Our guide to smoked beer and rauchbier covers it in full. Rauchbock is its stronger sibling, brewed to bock gravity and usually released for the autumn and winter season.

Bock strength, smoked malt

A bock is a strong German lager, usually around 6.3 to 7.2% ABV, with rich malt and modest bitterness. Our guide to bock and doppelbock explains the family. A rauchbock follows the same pattern but replaces some or all of the base malt with smoked malt.

Bamberg's best known example is the Urbock from the Schlenkerla brewery, a dark, deeply smoky beer that many drinkers treat as the benchmark. Other breweries make lighter or less smoky versions. The BJCP covers rauchbock within its classic style smoked beer category, judged as a bock with smoke.

Where the smoke comes from

The smoke in a Bamberg beer comes from the malt, not from anything added later. Maltsters dry the green malt over burning beechwood. The smoke deposits phenolic compounds on the grain, especially ones such as guaiacol and syringol, which give smoky, savoury and slightly sweet notes.

Brewers can use smoked malt for anything from a small fraction of the grist to almost all of it. Commercial beech-smoked malt is widely available. Our guide to beech-smoked barley malt covers one example. The more of it in the grist, the more the beer tastes of smoke rather than malt.

Smoked malt also changes with storage. The smoky compounds fade slowly once the malt leaves the kiln, so a sack that has sat for a year gives less smoke than a fresh one. In Indian heat and humidity, imported smoked malt should be kept sealed, cool and dry, then used within its shelf life. A brewer who expects a big smoky bock from tired malt may be disappointed.

What it tastes like

Pour a rauchbock and the smoke arrives first, often described as ham, bacon or a wood fire. Under it sits a rich malt base of bread crust, toast and caramel. Bitterness is moderate. The finish is smooth from long lagering, with the smoke lingering after you swallow.

Colour runs from deep amber to dark brown. Good examples feel balanced: the malt is strong enough to carry the smoke. Poor examples taste ashy, medicinal or like a mouthful of liquid smoke. Clove or plaster-like phenols point to a yeast or infection problem rather than malt smoke. A trained taster learns to separate them.

Why smoke and strength work together

A light beer with heavy smoke can taste thin and harsh. A strong bock base gives the smoke something to sit on. The extra malt sweetness and fuller body soften the phenols, so the smoke reads as savoury and warming rather than acrid.

The higher alcohol also carries aroma. At 6.5 to 7% ABV, the beer releases its smoke and malt aromas more readily as it warms in the glass. Serve rauchbock around 8 to 10 °C rather than ice cold. Too cold and the smoke turns flat and the malt disappears.

Smoke and Indian food

Indian cooking has plenty of smoke already. Tandoori meats, smoked baingan bharta, Naga smoked pork and dhungar, the trick of smoking a dal or curry with a piece of hot charcoal and ghee, all share flavours with a rauchbock. A smoked lager next to smoked food doubles the effect. Some find it wonderful. Others find it too much, so try both a match and a contrast.

For a contrast, try rauchbock with a sweet dish such as a jaggery-based dessert or with sharp aged cheese. As with any bock, keep pours modest in hot weather, drink water alongside and never drive after drinking.

Training your nose on smoke

Smoke is not one flavour. Beechwood, oak, peat and the phenols from certain yeasts all smell different. Telling a pleasant ham-like malt smoke from a medicinal or plastic fault is a sensory skill that improves with guided practice and reference samples, not with guesswork.

For a first step, taste a rauchbier and a rauchbock side by side. Note how the stronger malt base changes the way the smoke comes across. If you can, add a peated whisky to the comparison and smell it, without drinking, to notice how differently peat smoke reads.

Common questions

What is a rauchbock?

A rauchbock is a strong German lager brewed with beechwood-smoked malt, following the bock style at around 6 to 7% ABV.

Where does rauchbock come from?

It comes from Bamberg in Franconia, northern Bavaria, the home of German smoked beer.

How is the smoke added to rauchbock?

The malt itself is dried over burning beechwood, which gives it smoky phenolic compounds.

What temperature should rauchbock be served at?

Around 8 to 10 °C, so the smoke and malt aromas can come through.
