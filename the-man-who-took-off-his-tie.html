The man who took off his tie

Some careers are planned. Some are brewed. Mine was simply a matter of deciding where I belonged.

6 min read · Updated September 2026 · By Chatty Girija

Prologue

I was fresh out of campus. Engineer. MBA from Allahabad. Young enough to be fearless and old enough to believe I knew what I wanted. Campus recruitment had done its job. I had been selected by Godrej for its air conditioning business in Vikhroli, Mumbai.

Two of my friends had joined McDowell. So, naturally, I went to visit them. I liked the office. I liked the people. I liked the culture.

Something clicked. I thought, "I belong here." There was just one small problem. I already had a job. At Godrej.

The interview I wasn't invited to

Chapter one

My friend was understandably reluctant to introduce me to his boss. He had just joined himself and had enough to prove without bringing an uninvited candidate into the room.

But I persisted. Eventually, through the secretary, I got my meeting. I walked in, exchanged the usual pleasantries and got straight to the point.

"I like this place. I like this industry. I want to join McDowell. I have a job with Godrej, but I am not going back. I want to work here." The boss looked at me as though I had got the interview process slightly mixed up. There was no vacancy in Bombay. I told him I still wanted the job. He told me there was nothing in Bombay. I told him I could go to Delhi.

At that point, I suspect he wondered who was actually conducting the interview. He finally picked up the phone. "There may be something in sales in Delhi. Go and meet them." And just like that, I had created my own interview.

Same story. New boss.

Chapter two

Two days later, I was in Delhi. Formal shirt. Formal trousers. Tie.

The Delhi boss heard my story. I repeated myself. I wanted the job. I wanted to be in the industry. I was not going back to Godrej.

He listened. Then he leaned forward, pulled at my tie and said, "Agar yahan kaam karna hai to tie nikalni padegi."

If you want to work here, you will have to take off the tie. I looked at him and said, "Main to sab kuch nikalne ke liye taiyar hoon."

I was ready to give it everything. He must have thought, "This fellow has come for an interview as though he is interviewing me." Perhaps he was right. The difference was that I had already made my decision.

One more small problem

Chapter three

We had discussed the job. We had discussed the salary. We were almost done.

Then I mentioned one small thing. I had signed a bond with Godrej. If I left before completing probation, I had to pay Rs 5,000. In those days, Rs 5,000 was serious money. My starting salary was around Rs 1,200. And I did not have it. The Delhi boss looked at me. "You want an advance before you have even joined?" "Yes." "You realise Rs 5,000 is a lot of money?" "Yes." I knew I was asking for too much.

But I also knew I was not going back. "I don't have a choice. I am meant to be here." By now, the boss must have felt he was dealing with a strong drink. One more surprise and the glass might overflow. But he heard me out. He spoke to his senior. McDowell settled the Rs 5,000 directly with Godrej.

I got the job. I got the salary I had negotiated. And then came another request.

The last pour

Chapter four

I wanted to change my joining date. I wanted to go home and meet my parents before starting. Godrej had already sent a letter to my home in Allahabad because I had stopped reporting to work. My parents needed to know what was happening.

The Delhi boss agreed. By now, I think he had understood that saying no to me was probably going to require more effort than saying yes. But there was no entitlement in it. I knew exactly what I was asking for. And I knew I had to earn every bit of it once I got there.

Then came the real test

Chapter five

The senior boss asked my Delhi boss three questions.

"Are you sure about Gopal?" "Yes."

"Do you like him?" "Yes."

"Is he worth it?" "Yes."

That was it. McDowell settled the bond. My joining was sorted. The salary was agreed. I went home. Then I went to Delhi. And then I got to work. Because getting the opportunity is only the first pour. What you do after that is what counts.

Before careers came with algorithms

Chapter six

This was another time. There was no LinkedIn profile telling you who you were. No personal branding. No online courses. No algorithm deciding whether people should notice you. Your character had to do the talking. Your word mattered. Your handshake mattered. And once you said you would do something, you had to do it. I came from a generation that believed in taking responsibility, taking a calculated risk and then giving it everything.

Not reckless. Not privileged. Just convinced.

Thirty years later

Chapter seven

I never entered the alcohol industry with a five year plan. I entered because I felt I belonged. Over three decades later, that instinct has taken me through the alcohol industry and to my present role as Secretary General of the All India Brewers Association.

I have always believed that life gives you challenges for a reason. You can walk away from them. Or you can take the next sip and see where it takes you. I chose the latter. Because I never wanted to look back and say, "I wish I had tried."

The tie

And then there is the tie. A young man walked into McDowell wearing one.

The boss pulled it and said, "Agar yahan kaam karna hai to tie nikalni padegi." I said I was ready to give everything. The tie came off. The job stayed. And thirty years later, the story remains. Maybe the tie was never really about the tie. It was about knowing what you wanted, having the courage to ask for it and then having the character to prove you deserved it.

The tie came off. The conviction stayed. This is Gopal Joshi's story.
