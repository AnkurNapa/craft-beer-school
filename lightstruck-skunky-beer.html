Lightstruck, or skunked, beer

Lightstruck character is one of the fastest off flavours to form and one of the easiest to prevent. Once you know what causes it, you will never look at a green bottle the same way again.

3 min read · Updated August 2025 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. What lightstruck beer smells like
  2. The chemistry, in plain terms
  3. Why cans changed the conversation
  4. Where it happens, and it is rarely the brewery
  5. How to avoid drinking a skunked beer
  6. A quick tasting exercise

What lightstruck beer smells like

The classic description is skunk, and it is accurate enough that most drinkers recognise it instantly even without training. Some tasters get there through a slightly rubbery or burnt match note instead, but the association with skunk sticks because the compound involved is chemically close to what a skunk actually sprays.

It is unmistakable once you have smelled it deliberately, and mild cases can be easy to write off as just how a beer tastes, especially if you have never had the same beer packaged properly.

The chemistry, in plain terms

Hops contain bitter acids that, when exposed to visible light, particularly the blue and ultraviolet end of the spectrum, break down into a compound almost identical to a skunk's defence spray. This is a light reaction, not a heat or age reaction, which is why it can happen to a beer that has been out of the fridge for five minutes in bright sun, long before oxidation or staling would have a chance to set in.

Clear and green glass let far more of that light through than brown glass does. This is why beers packaged in green bottles, a tradition that started for marketing reasons rather than protection, are the most exposed to this fault of any packaging format.

Fluorescent and LED shop lighting can trigger the same reaction, just more slowly than direct sun. A bottle sitting under a bright tube light on a shelf for a full day will pick up a noticeable skunk character even indoors, away from any window.

Why cans changed the conversation

Aluminium cans block light completely. That single fact is a large part of why so many quality focused craft breweries in India and elsewhere have moved their flagship beers to cans over the last decade, even when the brand had built its identity around a bottle.

Brown glass is a reasonable middle ground, filtering out most of the damaging wavelengths without blocking light entirely, which is why traditional brewing regions have used brown bottles for a long time. Clear and green glass offer almost no protection at all.

Where it happens, and it is rarely the brewery

A brewery filling a green bottle in a dim packaging hall is not the point of failure. The damage happens afterwards: on a lit shop shelf, under fluorescent tube lighting in cold storage, or simply carried home in a clear bag on a sunny afternoon. It takes minutes of direct exposure for the reaction to become noticeable, not days.

This is one of the few faults where the retailer's lighting and display choices matter as much as anything the brewery did. A brewery can do everything right in the tank room and still see its beer skunked two shops down the road.

How to avoid drinking a skunked beer

Favour cans and brown glass over clear or green bottles when you have the choice. If you do buy a beer in green or clear glass, keep it in its box or a dark bag until you are ready to drink it, and never leave a bottle sitting on a sunlit table or windowsill before pouring.

At a bar, watch where bottles are stored. A fridge with the door glass exposed to direct sun, or a bottle store lit by daylight, will skunk stock that arrived perfectly fine.

If you are carrying beer home from a shop, ask for a paper or opaque bag rather than a clear plastic one. It sounds like a small thing, but that short walk in the afternoon sun is often the exact window in which the damage is done.

A quick tasting exercise

If you want to see the reaction for yourself, take two bottles of the same clear or green glass beer. Leave one in direct sunlight for ten minutes and keep the other in the dark, then taste both side by side. Most people identify the skunked one within a single sniff, which makes this one of the easiest off flavours to learn through direct experience rather than description, alongside faults like oxidation that take longer to develop.

Common questions

Is lightstruck beer safe to drink?

Yes. It is purely a flavour and aroma fault caused by light breaking down hop compounds. It does not make the beer unsafe, just unpleasant.

Why do some breweries still use green bottles?

Mostly tradition and brand identity built up before the science was well understood. Green glass offers very little protection against light, and a brewery using it is relying entirely on fast turnover and careful storage.

Can you fix a beer once it has gone skunky?

No. The reaction is not reversible, so the only real solution is prevention through packaging choice and keeping bottles out of light.

Does draught beer in a keg get lightstruck?

Almost never, because a sealed metal keg blocks light completely. The risk is confined to bottles and, to a much smaller extent, the short time beer spends in a clear glass once poured.
