Beer and dessert pairing

Dessert is the pairing most beginners get wrong, because they reach for whatever beer is already open. Here is how to pick on purpose.

3 min read · Updated January 2024 · Craft Beer School

On this page

  1. The rule that matters most: match sweetness
  2. Chocolate desserts
  3. Fruit based desserts
  4. Caramel and toffee desserts
  5. Spiced and Indian sweets
  6. When to skip beer entirely

The rule that matters most: match sweetness

A beer needs to be at least as sweet as the dessert it sits next to, or the dessert will make the beer taste thin, sour and unpleasant by direct comparison. This is the single most common mistake in dessert pairing, reaching for whatever pale ale happens to already be open rather than something built with enough residual sweetness to hold its own against the plate.

This mirrors a rule familiar from wine pairing, where a dry wine next to a sweet dessert almost always tastes worse than the wine did on its own, and beer follows exactly the same logic despite the different drink.

Chocolate desserts

A chocolate cake or brownie pairs naturally with a stout or porter, since roasted malt already carries chocolate and coffee notes that echo the dessert rather than fighting against it. An imperial stout, with real sweetness and body behind its roast character, is the more reliable choice here than a dry, bitter dry stout, which can taste thin next to something genuinely sweet.

Coffee flavoured desserts, tiramisu being the obvious example, pair unusually well with a coffee stout or porter, since matching the actual flavour compound directly, rather than merely the sweetness level, gives a result that needs almost no further thought to enjoy.

Fruit based desserts

A fruity Belgian style or a wheat beer with some sweetness works well against fruit desserts, particularly anything built around stone fruit or berries, since the beer's own esters can echo the fruit already on the plate. This is the same match versus contrast decision from food pairing basics, and either move can work well depending on the specific fruit involved.

A fruit beer built with real fruit addition, rather than just a fruity yeast character, can work particularly well here, since it brings both the matching fruit note and enough sweetness to hold up against the dessert.

Caramel and toffee desserts

A malty amber ale or a beer with real caramel character from the malt bill matches this family of desserts closely, particularly kheer, halwa and other Indian sweets built around a slow caramelisation of milk and sugar over low heat.

Salted caramel desserts, increasingly common on Indian dessert menus, pair particularly well with a Scotch ale or a barleywine, both of which carry enough caramel malt character and body to stand up to the richness of caramel and butter together on the plate.

Jalebi and other syrup soaked Indian sweets, intensely sweet by design, need a beer with real body and sweetness of its own to avoid tasting completely washed out, which rules out almost anything dry or heavily hopped as a pairing option here.

Spiced and Indian sweets

Cardamom, saffron and rosewater common in Indian sweets pair well with Belgian styles that carry their own spice character from the yeast strain used, since the two sets of spice notes tend to complement each other rather than clash on the palate.

Ice cream and beer floats, built by pouring a stout or a fruit beer over vanilla ice cream, are a playful way to explore this pairing logic directly, and a good way to introduce dessert pairing to guests who have never tried it.

Cardamom in particular carries a slightly menthol, cooling quality that plays well against a beer's own warming alcohol character in a stronger style, which is part of why this pairing works better than it might sound on paper.

When to skip beer entirely

A very light, delicate dessert, plain vanilla or a simple fruit salad, may be better left without a beer pairing at all, since almost anything will overwhelm it completely. Knowing when not to pair is as useful a skill as knowing what to pair, a judgement call covered properly in our guide on how beer judges score a beer, where balance is scored as its own separate category.

None of these pairings are fixed laws. Personal sweetness tolerance varies a good deal from person to person, so treat every suggestion here as a sensible starting point worth adjusting once you know your own preference.

Common questions

What is the golden rule of dessert pairing?

The beer should be at least as sweet as the dessert, or the dessert will make the beer taste thin and sour by comparison.

What beer goes with chocolate cake?

An imperial stout or porter, since roasted malt already carries chocolate and coffee character that echoes the dessert.

Can I pair beer with Indian sweets like kheer or halwa?

Yes, a malty amber ale with real caramel character works well against the slow caramelised milk and sugar flavour common in these sweets.

Should every dessert get a beer pairing?

Not necessarily. Very delicate desserts are sometimes better left without a pairing, since almost any beer will overwhelm them.
