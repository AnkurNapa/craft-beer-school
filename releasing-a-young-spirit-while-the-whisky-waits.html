Releasing a young spirit while the whisky waits

Most new whisky distilleries release something else first. Done well, it pays the bills and builds a following for the whisky to come. Done badly, it confuses the brand. Here are the options and what each one asks of you.

Why release early at all

A distillery that fills casks today might not release whisky for three years or more. Scotch must mature at least three years in oak. During that time the distillery pays for staff, energy, warehouse costs and loan interest. Revenue from an early product reduces the cash gap and proves the team can sell.

An early release also builds a brand. Drinkers who like your gin may follow you to your whisky. Bartenders who list your first product already know your name when the whisky arrives.

Gin is the usual choice

Gin is the most common early release for a reason. It needs no ageing, so it can be sold within weeks of production. Most gin is made by redistilling neutral spirit with juniper and other botanicals. Juniper must be the dominant flavour for it to be called gin under widely used definitions.

The catch is that gin is often made from bought-in neutral spirit, not from your own malt. That is legitimate and widely done, but it means the gin says little about your whisky spirit. Some distilleries use their own new make as part of the base, which links the two. Others choose Indian botanicals that tie the gin to the place. Either way, gin needs its own recipe development, packaging and marketing budget.

New make and white whisky

Some distilleries bottle their unaged spirit, often called new make spirit. It shows drinkers the raw character of the distillery: fruit, cereal, the effect of the cut. It is usually high in strength, often reduced to around 40 to 46% ABV for bottling.

The labelling needs care. Unaged spirit cannot be sold as Scotch. In many markets it cannot be called whisky at all. In India, check the current state and FSSAI rules on what the label may say. New make also has a narrow audience. It sells well as a curiosity or to fans, less well as a volume product.

Young aged spirit

Some distilleries release spirit that has spent a short time in cask, often in small casks to speed extraction. In India's heat, a year in wood can extract noticeable colour and flavour.

The question is what to call it. Rules on minimum ageing for whisky vary by country and category. A spirit that has not met the minimum cannot use the whisky name in those markets. Calling it whisky when the rules do not allow it creates a compliance problem and damages trust. Our guide to grain whisky versus neutral spirit explains some of the category lines.

Protect the whisky brand

The biggest risk of an early release is confusing people. If the gin becomes famous, the distillery may end up known as a gin maker. If the young spirit is harsh, drinkers may assume the whisky will be too.

Some distilleries give the early product a separate brand. Others put the distillery name on everything and accept the link. Decide on purpose, not by accident. A clear brand architecture, with the whisky as the main product and the early release as a supporting act, helps. Our guide to what a new distillery sells while whisky ages looks at the commercial side.

Count the real cost

Each early product needs its own licence conditions, label registration in each state, packaging, a route to market and marketing money. Gin also needs botanical storage, a still or still time and its own quality checks. A distillery that tries to launch gin, vodka and new make at once spreads a small team thin.

One well chosen early product usually beats three rushed ones.

Timing the early release

An early product needs its own timeline. Recipe trials for a gin can take a few months. Label registration in each state usually takes longer still. If the distillery starts filling whisky casks in month one, the gin may not reach the shelf until month six or later, even though it needs no ageing.

Work backwards from the date you need revenue. List recipe development, trial batches, packaging design and orders, label approval, price approval and distributor onboarding. Many of these can run in parallel with commissioning the stills.

Remember that the early product also uses still time. A gin run on the spirit still is a run that is not making whisky. Plan production days so the whisky casks keep filling at the rate your stock plan needs.

Your first step

Write down three questions for each option: what it costs to launch, how soon it earns money and whether it helps or hurts the whisky brand. Score gin, new make and young aged spirit against them. Then check what the rules in your state allow on the label for each one before you design anything.

Common questions

What do new whisky distilleries sell first?

Gin is the most common early release because it needs no ageing. Some distilleries also release new make or young spirit aged for a short time.

Can unaged spirit be called whisky?

Under Scotch rules and in many other markets it cannot. In India, check the current state and FSSAI rules before naming any unaged product.

Does releasing gin hurt a whisky brand?

It can if the brand becomes known mainly for gin. A clear plan for how the two brands relate helps avoid that.
