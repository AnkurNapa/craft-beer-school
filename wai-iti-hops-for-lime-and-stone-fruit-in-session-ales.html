Wai-iti hops for lime and stone fruit in session ales

Wai-iti is a New Zealand aroma hop with alpha acid of about 2.5 to 3.5% and a bright lime and stone fruit aroma. In a session ale you can use a lot of it late without making the beer harsh. Here is how.

A hop that hardly bitters

Wai-iti was released in New Zealand in 2011. It grows in the Nelson region, like most of the country's hops. The striking number is the alpha acid: usually around 2.5 to 3.5%, low for a commercial hop.

Low alpha with a decent oil content means you get a lot of aroma per IBU. Descriptions mention lime zest, peach, apricot and mandarin, with a soft, sweet fruit character. It is a gentle hop. Think fruit bowl, not grapefruit pith.

Why low alpha suits a session ale

A session ale at 3.5 to 4.5% ABV has a light malt body. Bitterness that would sit nicely in a 6% IPA can feel thin and harsh here. Most session pale ales aim for 20 to 35 IBU.

With a high alpha hop, a big late addition quickly piles up bitterness. With Wai-iti, you can add a large whirlpool charge and still stay in range. For a 20 litre batch, 60 g of Wai-iti at 3.5% alpha in a whirlpool adds only around 5 IBU. You get fruit without the bitterness bill.

A 20 litre session ale with Wai-iti

Here is a recipe at about OG 1.040, 4% ABV and 25 IBU:

  • Pale ale malt 3.0 kg (88%).
  • Wheat malt 0.24 kg (7%).
  • Light crystal malt around 20 EBC 0.17 kg (5%).
  • A clean bittering hop, 60 minutes, for about 14 IBU.
  • Wai-iti 40 g at 10 minutes for about 6 IBU.
  • Wai-iti 60 g in a 20 minute whirlpool at 80 °C.
  • Wai-iti 60 g dry hop for three days.

Mash at 67 °C to keep some body in a small beer. Ferment with a clean ale yeast at 18 to 19 °C. The aim is a beer you can drink two of without feeling it, with lime and peach right through.

The floor detail: thin beer, loud hops

The risk in a low gravity, heavily hopped beer is imbalance. Pile in late hops and the beer can taste grassy and watery at the same time. The hop material adds polyphenols and green flavours that a small malt bill cannot cover.

Two things help. Keep the dry hop moderate, around 3 g per litre, with contact short at three days. Then give the beer some body through mash temperature and a little wheat or oats. A session ale needs enough malt to stand up. Fermentable sugar alone is not enough.

Where Wai-iti fits

Wai-iti suits light, fruit-led beers:

  • Session pale ales and golden ales at 3.5 to 4.5% ABV.
  • Wheat beers with an American or New Zealand twist.
  • Light lagers or Kölsch-style beers, as a late hop for a soft fruit edge.

It is less useful in a big IPA, where you would need very large amounts for any impact. Pair it with something punchier there, such as Amarillo.

Session beer and Indian weather

A light, fruity beer at 4% makes a lot of sense in an Indian summer. It is refreshing at a cold serve and does not leave drinkers flagging. It is also one of the easiest beers to show off a hop. With little malt, alcohol or bitterness in the way, Wai-iti's lime and peach come straight through.

The flip side is that every fault shows too. A slightly warm fermentation, some oxygen pickup or a little infection will be obvious in a beer this clean. Keep fermentation temperature under control and package with as little air as you can.

Buying and storing Wai-iti

Wai-iti is not always on Indian homebrew suppliers' lists. When you find it, buy sealed foil packs and check the harvest year. New Zealand's crop is harvested from February to April, so fresh hops reach India months later. Store them frozen. Gentle aromas like Wai-iti's fade faster than loud ones once oil starts to oxidise.

If it is out, Motueka gives a similar lime character with more punch. Use a little less by weight late in the boil.

A split batch to learn the hop

The fastest way to learn Wai-iti is to taste it against something you know. Brew the session ale above to the end of the boil with only the bittering hop. Split the wort into two 10 litre fermenters. Dry hop one with 30 g of Wai-iti and the other with 30 g of Cascade. Same yeast, same temperature, same day.

Taste them side by side after packaging. Cascade will read as grapefruit and pine. Wai-iti will read as lime and soft stone fruit, with noticeably less bite. Once you have that contrast in your memory, you will know when a recipe needs Wai-iti and when it needs something louder.

Common questions

What is the alpha acid of Wai-iti?

Usually around 2.5 to 3.5%, which is low. It is used for aroma and flavour rather than bittering.

What does Wai-iti taste like?

Lime zest, peach, apricot and mandarin. It is a soft, sweet fruit hop rather than a sharp citrus one.

Can Wai-iti be used in an IPA?

It can, but you need large amounts for impact. Most brewers pair it with a punchier hop in bigger beers.
