Red IPA and rye IPA: two hybrid IPAs and how they differ

Red IPA and rye IPA sit next to each other on tap lists and often get mixed up. They start from the same hoppy base but change different things. Once you know which, you can order the one you actually want.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Two IPAs that changed one thing each

A standard American IPA is pale malt with a lot of hops on top. Brewers who wanted something different kept the hops and changed the grain. Red IPA changes the colour and the sweetness by adding caramel and red malts. Rye IPA changes the texture and adds a peppery edge by swapping some barley for rye.

That is the whole difference in one line. A red IPA tastes like an IPA with toffee underneath. A rye IPA tastes like an IPA with a dry, spicy grip on the finish. Both are usually 6 to 7.5% ABV and firmly bitter.

What makes a red IPA red

The colour comes from crystal or caramel malts, sometimes with a small pinch of a very dark malt for a ruby tint. These malts are stewed and kilned so their starch turns to sugars that the yeast cannot fully eat. They leave sweetness, body and flavours of toffee, raisin and toast.

In the glass a red IPA runs from deep amber to garnet. On the nose you get the usual citrus and pine from American hops, then a bready, caramel note. On the palate the malt pushes back against the bitterness, so a red IPA at 60 IBU can feel softer than a pale West Coast IPA at the same number.

If that sounds like an amber or red ale with extra hops, you are close. The line between a hoppy red ale and a red IPA is mostly how hard the brewer leans on the hops.

What rye brings to an IPA

Rye is a cereal grain that brewers malt like barley. It has no husk worth speaking of and it is full of sticky compounds called beta-glucans and pentosans. In a beer they give a fuller, slightly oily mouthfeel that people often describe as silky.

The flavour is the other half. Rye malt adds a dry, peppery, slightly earthy spice, close to the crust of a dark rye bread. Paired with resinous hops it makes the finish snap shut rather than linger sweetly. Most rye IPAs use 10 to 20% rye malt in the grist. Go past about a quarter and the mash turns to porridge, which is why brewers keep a close eye on it. Our guide to pale rye malt covers the grain in detail.

A rye IPA is usually golden to light copper. Some brewers add a little caramel rye for colour, which is where the two styles can overlap.

Tasting them side by side

The easiest way to learn the difference is to pour one of each into the same glassware at the same temperature, around 8 to 10 °C. Taste them one after the other.

  • Colour. Red IPA is clearly darker, often with red light at the edge of the glass. Rye IPA looks closer to a pale IPA.
  • Aroma. Both lead with hops. The red adds caramel and bread. The rye may smell faintly of black pepper or rye toast.
  • Body. The red feels rounder because of residual sugar. The rye feels slick in the middle but dries out at the end.
  • Finish. The red finishes with a sweet and bitter balance. The rye finishes dry, spicy and bitter.

Write down what you notice before you read the tap card. You will be surprised how often your first impressions match the grain bill.

Which one to order

If you like a West Coast IPA but find it a little sharp, try a red IPA. The malt softens the edges and it holds up well against grilled meat and tandoori dishes. If you like your beer dry and bracing, try the rye. It cuts through fried food and works well with a plate of pepper chicken.

One caution in Indian conditions: both styles depend on fresh hop aroma. A red IPA that has sat warm for a few months loses its hops first and turns into a flat caramel beer. Ask the bar when the keg was tapped. Favour beer that has been kept cold.

Why brewers find these styles harder than they look

On paper these are easy recipes: add some crystal malt or some rye to an IPA. In practice the balance is the hard part. Too much caramel malt and a red IPA tastes like hoppy syrup that fights the bitterness. Too much rye and the mash sticks, the run-off slows to a trickle and the beer comes out heavy.

Getting either style right means understanding how each malt behaves in the mash and what it does to body, colour and hop perception. That is grist design. It is the part of brewing that separates a recipe someone copied from a beer someone designed.

Common questions

Is a red IPA the same as an amber IPA?

Mostly yes. Brewers use both names for an IPA built on caramel malts. Amber versions are a little lighter in colour and caramel, red versions a little darker and richer.

Does rye IPA taste of rye bread?

A little. Rye malt adds a dry, peppery spice similar to rye bread crust, but the hops still lead. Expect the spice mostly on the finish.

Which is more bitter, red IPA or rye IPA?

They are often brewed to similar bitterness, but the rye version usually tastes more bitter because it finishes drier. The caramel sweetness in a red IPA softens how bitter it feels.

Are these official beer styles?

Rye IPA and red IPA appear as specialty IPA categories in the BJCP style guidelines. Breweries also use the names loosely, so read the tap card for the actual grain and hops.
