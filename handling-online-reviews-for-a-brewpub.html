Handling online reviews and star ratings for a brewpub, good and bad

Star ratings decide whether a stranger walks in on a Friday. We look at how to answer good and bad reviews, what to fix behind the bar and what never to do to chase a higher score.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

Why the reply matters more than the review

Most people choosing a brewpub on a phone do not read every review. They glance at the star average, read two or three recent comments and then look at how the owner answered. A calm, specific reply to a bad review tells a stranger more about your place than ten five-star posts that say "great vibe".

So treat reviews on Google, Zomato and similar apps as part of your front of house. Somebody owns them, the way somebody owns the cold room. In a small brewpub that is usually the founder or the floor manager, with a set time each day to read new posts.

Sort the reviews before you answer them

Reviews fall into a few honest groups and each needs a different reply.

  • Praise with detail. "The Hefeweizen tasted of banana and clove, loved it." Thank them and mention the detail back. It shows a person read it.
  • Praise with no detail. A short thank you is enough. Do not paste the same line under fifty reviews.
  • Service complaints. Slow food, a rude server, a long wait for the bill. These are almost always fair and fixable.
  • Beer complaints. "Flat", "sour", "tasted off". Take these seriously, because they may be true.
  • Rants and fakes. Abuse, competitor attacks or reviews from people who were never there.

Answering a bad review

Write the reply when you are calm, not at midnight after a hard shift. A good reply has four parts: thank them for telling you, own the specific problem, say what you have changed and invite them to contact you directly. Keep it to four or five sentences.

Never argue facts in public, even when you are right. If someone says the beer was warm and your logs show the tap was fine, the reply still says you are sorry they had a poor pint and asks them to message you. The audience is the next customer, not the reviewer.

Do not post the customer's bill, booking details or phone number in a reply. It looks like a threat. It is also their personal data.

When the complaint is about the beer

A brewpub has an advantage here: you can check the claim. When a review says the IPA tasted like cardboard or the lager was sour, pull a sample from that tap and taste it against a fresh one from the tank. Check the line cleaning log. In Indian heat a beer line left a few weeks without cleaning can pick up sourness and haze. The customer will blame the recipe.

If the beer really was off, say so plainly in the reply and fix it. Our guide to beer off flavours helps a floor team name what they are tasting. "Flat" often means low carbonation from a gas leak or a warm keg, which is a ten-minute check, not a mystery.

Reviews are free quality data. Keep a simple sheet with the date, the beer and the complaint. Three "sour" comments about the same tap in a month is a cleaning problem, whatever the star average says.

Asking for reviews the honest way

The best way to raise a rating is to give more happy customers a simple way to say so. A small card on the table or a QR code on the bill folder that links straight to your review page works well. Staff can mention it when a table has clearly had a good night.

What you must not do:

  • Offer free beer, discounts or gifts in exchange for a review.
  • Write reviews yourself or ask friends and staff to post as customers.
  • Buy reviews from an agency, however cheap.
  • Ask only the happy tables and quietly steer unhappy ones away.

Review platforms ban incentivised and fake reviews and can remove them or penalise the listing. India also has a national standard on how online consumer reviews should be collected and published. Consumer protection rules also treat fake reviews as misleading. Beyond the rules, regulars notice when a page suddenly fills with five-star posts in identical English.

Dealing with abuse and fake reviews

If a review contains abuse, personal attacks on staff or is clearly from someone who never visited, use the platform's report or flag option and give a short factual reason. Platforms do not remove reviews just because they are negative, so only flag the ones that break their rules.

While you wait, reply once, briefly and politely: you have no record of the visit and would like to hear from them directly. Then leave it. A long public fight with a troll does more damage than the review.

Building a weekly review routine

Fifteen minutes a day is enough for most brewpubs. Read new reviews, reply to anything negative within a day or two, note any beer or service pattern and share one good review with the team at the pre-shift briefing. Once a month, look at the spread of comments rather than the average: what do people praise, what do they keep mentioning as a problem?

The harder part is joining reviews to the rest of your marketing, your social media and your regulars' WhatsApp group, so that one tells you what to say on the others. That is where a structured plan beats replying on instinct.

Common questions

Should a brewpub reply to every review?

Reply to every negative review and to positive ones that mention something specific. A short thank you on the rest is fine, but avoid pasting the same line everywhere.

Can we ask customers to delete a bad review?

You can fix the problem and invite them to update their review if they feel differently. Never pressure them or offer anything in return for changing it.

Is it allowed to give a free pint for a Google review?

No. Review platforms ban incentivised reviews and they can be treated as misleading under consumer rules. Ask for honest reviews with nothing attached.

How do we get a fake review removed?

Flag it through the platform's report option with a short factual reason, such as no record of the visit. Reply once politely and move on while the platform reviews it.
