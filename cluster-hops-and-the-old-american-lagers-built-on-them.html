Cluster hops and the old American lagers built on them

Cluster was the workhorse of American brewing for well over a century. It tastes of blackcurrant, earth and a little spice. It also shaped a style of lager far more bitter than the beer most people picture today.

4 min read · Updated October 2026 · By Ankur Napa

Written byAnkur NapaCourse Instructor · Master Brewer

The oldest American hop still in the ground

Cluster is the hop American brewing ran on for generations. Its exact origin is a mystery. The usual explanation is that hops brought over by European settlers crossed with wild American hops while growers kept the plants that cropped well. By the late nineteenth century it was the main hop of American farms. For much of the twentieth century it remained the most widely planted variety in the United States.

That makes Cluster a living record of what American beer tasted like before the craft movement. When you drink a beer hopped with it, you are tasting the base the rest of the American hop story was built on.

What Cluster tastes like

Cluster is a modest hop by modern standards. Its alpha acids, the compounds that become bitterness when hops are boiled, usually sit around 5.5 to 8.5%. Modern bittering hops often reach 15% or more.

In the glass, Cluster gives a rounded, firm bitterness with an earthy, slightly spicy character. Many tasters pick up blackcurrant and a floral note. Used heavily, that blackcurrant can tip into something brewers politely call catty, a sharper fruity smell that reminds some people of a tomcat. In a balanced lager it reads as fruit and earth, with no sign of the cat.

It is not a citrus hop. If you expect grapefruit because it is American, Cluster will surprise you. It tastes older than that.

The lagers built on it

Cluster was the hop of the pre-Prohibition American lager, brewed before the United States banned alcohol in 1920. German immigrant brewers wanted to make the lagers of home, but American six-row barley was high in protein and husk. They thinned it out with corn or rice, in some recipes up to a third of the grist, which lightened the body and helped the beer stay clear.

These beers were stronger and more bitter than the American lager that came later. Beer judges today describe the style, often called classic American pilsner, at about 4.5 to 6% ABV with 25 to 40 IBU (international bitterness units). A modern mass market lager often sits well under 15 IBU. Cluster carried most of that bitterness, sometimes with a European hop added late for aroma.

Why this style feels familiar in India

Indian beer drinkers already know the adjunct idea. Many Indian lagers use rice, maize or sugar alongside malt to keep the beer light and crisp in the heat. Our guide to brewing for India covers why that works.

The difference is bitterness and hop character. A pre-Prohibition lager is what you get if you take a familiar light, adjunct lager and give it real hop backbone and that earthy blackcurrant note. For drinkers who find most lagers sweet or empty, it can be a revelation.

Why Cluster faded and why it is coming back

Cluster also bittered many of the American ales and porters of the same period. Brewers used what the farms grew. The farms grew Cluster.

Cluster lost ground for practical reasons. Breeders produced varieties with two or three times the alpha acids, so brewers needed fewer kilograms of hops for the same bitterness. Craft brewers then chased bright citrus and tropical aromas that Cluster does not have. The variety dropped to a niche crop.

It never vanished, though. Brewers recreating historical beers seek it out because no other hop tastes quite right in a pre-Prohibition recipe. It also has a following among brewers making dark lagers and porters in an old American style. Our guide on resin and bitterness from old hops explains why a hop this old can taste coarse when it is stale. It also covers why storage matters.

How to taste Cluster for yourself

The clearest way to meet Cluster is in a lager labelled pre-Prohibition, classic American pilsner or historical American lager. Craft brewers in India experiment widely, so ask at a brewpub whether anyone has brewed one.

Taste it beside a mainstream lager, both at about 4 to 6 °C. Notice the bitterness first: it should linger without scraping. Then warm the glass in your hand for a minute and smell again. The earthy, blackcurrant character opens up as the beer warms. That contrast teaches you more about hops than reading a dozen tasting notes. Our guide on how to taste beer walks through the method.

If you brew at home, Cluster is easy to find from American suppliers and cheap compared with newer varieties. A simple test batch with six-row or Pilsner malt, a fifth of flaked maize and Cluster alone at about 30 IBU will show you the hop more honestly than any blend. For a reddish amber version of the same beer, add a small share of a light caramel malt such as CARARED.

Common questions

Is Cluster a bittering or aroma hop?

Cluster is used for both. Its alpha acids are moderate, around 5.5 to 8.5%, so it gives a rounded bitterness, while its earthy blackcurrant character adds flavour.

What is a pre-Prohibition lager?

It is the style of lager brewed in the United States before 1920, made with six-row barley plus corn or rice and hopped more firmly than modern American lager, often with Cluster.

Why does Cluster sometimes smell catty?

Its blackcurrant character comes from compounds that can smell sharp and catty at high concentrations. In balanced amounts they read as dark fruit.
